In the 2024/25 season Liverpool brought in $911m. The Los Angeles Lakers brought in $551m. Liverpool's revenue was 65% larger, and they had just won the Premier League. Yet Forbes values the Lakers at $10bn and Liverpool at $6.2bn, so the basketball team is put at 61% more.

The dates matter: the Lakers figure is Forbes's October 2025 estimate and Liverpool's is from May 2026. In August 2026, a newer Lakers sale agreement was reported at $12.5bn. That reported agreement is separate from Forbes's estimate; it is not treated here as a verified completed sale.

That comparison has become more than a curiosity. In August, Fenway Sports Group (FSG) agreed to sell a sizeable minority stake in Liverpool to a consortium whose backers include Jeff Bezos. That raises a bigger question: could Liverpool one day be valued like an American franchise rather than a European football team, at something like £10bn?

Liverpool's commercial growth under FSG has been substantial. The investigation therefore starts with what buyers are paying for each pound of revenue, and whether Liverpool could justify a higher price.

## What has actually been agreed

On 14 August the club announced that FSG had agreed a strategic minority investment from 1892 Holdings, a group led and managed by Amit Bhatia. The participants named included Bhatia and the Mittal Family Trusts, EE Capital (the family office of Elaine and Eduardo Saverin), and K5 Sports. K5 Sports is a fund of K5 Global in which Bezos is the lead investor. Under the announced agreement, FSG retains majority ownership and operational control.

That is the full extent of what has been officially disclosed. Bezos is not a direct Liverpool shareholder on the club's account; his link is through a fund taking part in the consortium. The announcement gave no percentage and no price.

The numbers in circulation come from reporting. Andy Hunter, writing in the Irish Times on 18 August, reported that the stake is 38%, higher than earlier reports of about a third. He reported that it values the club at around £5.5bn, and that the group paid more than £2bn. Those are reported transaction terms, not confirmation that the agreement has completed.

Hunter also reported that the consortium would have first refusal on a majority stake if FSG chose to sell within the next 12 months. That gives the consortium an advantage if FSG decides to sell. It does not let them force a sale. He named Bhatia as the expected vice-chair, with Elaine Saverin and K5 Global's Bryan Baum joining an expanded board, and described Bezos as a passive investor at this stage.

Several things are still not public:

**Completion.** The deal was subject to regulatory approval. Bhatia said in a club interview in August that the process was under way. No primary completion notice was found in the evidence reviewed up to 9 October. That does not establish that approval is still outstanding or that the transaction has not completed. Its completion remains unverified.

**FSG's remaining share.** It is tempting to subtract 38% from 100% and call FSG a 62% owner. That would be wrong. Dynasty Equity already bought a minority stake in the club in 2023, and the evidence reviewed does not establish the full post-deal ownership structure.

**Where the money goes.** The £2bn-plus is a price paid for shares. Nothing published says how much, if any, goes into the club rather than to selling shareholders. It should not be read as a transfer budget.

## Revenue is not profit, and profit is not value

Much of the confusion about Liverpool's value comes from mixing up three different numbers.

The first is revenue, the money coming in. Liverpool's audited accounts for the year to 31 May 2025 show £702.7m. That was made up of £323.5m commercial, £263.7m media and £115.6m matchday.

The second is profit, and here the accounts tell a more modest story. Staff costs were £427.7m, about 61% of revenue, before anything else is paid. Liverpool made a profit before tax of £15.2m and £8m after tax. The statutory operating result included £53.3m of profit from selling players and a £12.9m insurance recovery. Player sales can be a recurring part of a football club's business, but they are excluded from the operating measure used for the international comparison. Remove those gains and the insurance recovery, and Liverpool's operating result changes from a £23.8m profit to a £42.4m loss. That loss is after depreciation and amortisation, including the annual accounting charge that spreads transfer fees over players' contracts.

Forbes uses an earnings measure before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, commonly called EBITDA. For football clubs it also excludes player trading. Its estimate for Liverpool is $133m, a margin of 14.6%. A separate calculation from Liverpool's audited accounts gives £102.3m of EBITDA excluding player-disposal gains, or £89.4m after also removing the insurance recovery. The latter is a calculated indication of recurring earnings, not a club-reported measure. EBITDA is not cash available to spend: maintaining the squad and stadium still costs money.

The third number is value. Forbes estimates enterprise value: the value of the operating business, expressed as equity value plus net debt. That is different from the cash price paid for a shareholder's stake. The business valuation depends on what investors expect the club to earn in future, how risky those earnings are, and how many buyers want the asset. Forbes's estimate is $6.2bn, which it quotes at approximately £4.6bn using valuation-date exchange rates. For the graphs and financial model here, converting the same dollar estimate at a common analytical rate gives £4.81bn. These are two currency presentations of the same estimate.

None of these numbers is cash in the bank, and none of them is the owners' personal wealth.

Liverpool are not unusual within football. On Forbes's most recent full all-sports list, published in December 2025, they were joint 48th. That is a dated ranking, not their verified position across all sports in October 2026.

## Why American franchises cost more

The research points to structural reasons, though the figures cannot measure how much each one contributes.

**League membership and scarcity.** NFL, NBA and MLB franchises face no relegation for poor sporting performance. New teams arrive only when the league itself chooses to expand. A buyer is purchasing a permanent seat in a closed club with a fixed number of members. Liverpool's revenue depends far more on what happens on the pitch. The difference between Europa League and Champions League football can be seen in their recent accounts.

**Rules on spending.** The NFL has a hard salary cap: $301.2m per team in 2026. The NBA's cap is softer, with exceptions and a luxury tax. Football has rules too. From this season the Premier League's new system sets an 85% reference for squad spending, measured against a club's football revenue plus its profit on player sales, and UEFA applies a 70% rule to clubs in its competitions. These are squad-cost rules, with their own definitions and enforcement arrangements; the Premier League's reference also has allowances. They do not reserve a guaranteed profit for owners. MLB, meanwhile, has a competitive-balance tax rather than an NFL-style hard salary cap.

**Long, shared media deals.** The NBA's national agreements with Disney, NBCUniversal and Amazon run for 11 years, from 2025/26 to 2035/36. That kind of guaranteed income is what investors like to pay for. The Premier League also sells its rights collectively and shares them: half of domestic money equally, a quarter by league position, a quarter by television appearances. Collective selling and revenue sharing therefore exist in football too. The relevant differences include contract length, distribution rules and how dependent a club's income is on qualification and results.

There is a strong counterexample. Higher American values do not mean higher American profits. On Forbes's figures, both the Yankees and the Dodgers made operating losses in 2025, of $53m and $20m. Both are still valued above Liverpool. Buyers are paying for more than this year's profit. Expected future media income, scarcity and prestige are plausible explanations, although this comparison cannot isolate their individual effects.

![Operating margins by league, including losses at the Yankees and Dodgers](/images/analysis/liverpool-bezos-ten-billion-question/chart-3.png)

Liverpool's own margin, about 15% on Forbes's measure, is low among Europe's leading clubs. Of the seven in this comparison only Manchester City's is lower. The data cannot show how far that holds Liverpool's valuation down. It does show that bigger revenue alone is not what buyers are paying for.

## Have Liverpool undersold themselves?

A popular version of the Bezos story says Liverpool's commercial operation has been sleepy and a tech billionaire will wake it up. The accounts do not support that.

In 2009/10, the last season before FSG bought the club for £300m, Liverpool's revenue was £184.5m, according to Deloitte. By 2024/25 it was £702.7m. That is 3.8 times larger, an average of about 9.3% growth a year over 15 years. Commercial income grew faster still, from £62.1m to £323.5m, more than five times larger, or about 11.6% a year. Commercial revenue now makes up 46% of the club's income, up from about a third.

Those are nominal revenue figures, not adjusted for inflation, and FSG cannot take all the credit: bigger Premier League television deals and Champions League runs lifted every stream.

But the comparison with Liverpool's rivals points the same way. On Deloitte's euro figures, Liverpool's revenue grew 3.7 times between 2009/10 and 2024/25:

- Manchester City grew 5.4 times

- Arsenal 3.0 times

- Bayern Munich 2.7 times

- Real Madrid 2.7 times

- Barcelona 2.5 times

- Manchester United 2.3 times

Only City grew faster among these six peers.

![European revenue growth from 2009/10 to 2024/25: only Manchester City grew faster than Liverpool](/images/analysis/liverpool-bezos-ten-billion-question/chart-4.png)

*Revenue growth over 15 years using Deloitte's euro figures. Exchange rates, accounting scope and different starting sizes affect the comparison; it does not isolate FSG's contribution.*

Liverpool finished 2024/25 fifth in Deloitte's Money League and top among English clubs. Real Madrid remain far bigger in absolute terms, so the gap at the very top is real. But the evidence does not show a club that has been losing ground on this measure. Nor does strong growth prove that every commercial opportunity has been used.

![Liverpool commercial, media and matchday revenue from 2010 to 2025](/images/analysis/liverpool-bezos-ten-billion-question/chart-5.png)

*Liverpool's reported commercial, media and matchday revenue. The 2012 accounts cover ten months. Pandemic timing and changing accounting classifications limit year-to-year comparisons.*

The commercial businesses people imagine new owners might "unlock" mostly exist already. Liverpool have global online stores and a network of official shops. They run hospitality, stadium tours, concerts and conferences at a 61,276-capacity Anfield, international academies, and a paid ALL RED membership and video service. The new Adidas kit deal had a remarkable launch in August 2025. The club said home-shirt sales in launch week were up by more than 700% on the previous year's launch, with orders from 150 countries. That shows strong demand. It does not mean annual merchandise income will rise by anything like that much, and it falls after the latest audited year. On partners, Turkish Airlines will become the main shirt partner from 2027/28, on undisclosed terms, and the club already has deals with Google and SAS.

And then there is Amazon. In its results for 2018/19, published in February 2020, the club described new selling channels on Amazon in the USA, Canada and Germany. Selling Liverpool shirts on Amazon is not something Bezos needs to open up. Those channels were already described more than six years ago.

## Where Liverpool could grow

The Adidas launch gives some evidence of demand for more merchandise. Better local-language shops, payment options and fulfilment in Asia and North America could help Liverpool build on existing distribution. But the club's share of each sale, repeat purchasing and regional margins are not publicly established here. More orders bring inventory, logistics, returns and marketing costs. There is no defensible figure for an extra annual revenue stream.

Sponsor renewals and new commercial categories offer another route. Turkish Airlines' forthcoming partnership demonstrates interest, but its undisclosed fee prevents a calculation of the uplift. Sponsorship also has finite inventory and exclusivity commitments. Introductions from new investors would be useful only if they lead to commercially sound contracts.

At Anfield, better hospitality packages and additional profitable concerts, conferences and tours could raise earnings within the existing business. Capacity, the football calendar, staffing, pitch protection, transport and neighbours limit expansion. Higher gross event sales do not necessarily produce higher profits, and no funded further stadium expansion is established in the evidence reviewed.

Paid memberships and club-produced video can reach supporters who will never visit Anfield. Liverpool already sell these products. Better content, localisation and retention could help, but free alternatives compete for attention, production costs money, and Liverpool cannot independently sell unrestricted live Premier League coverage. Fan numbers alone cannot support a subscription forecast.

These are ways to improve a substantial existing business. Their contribution to valuation depends on the earnings Liverpool retain after the costs of delivering them.

## What the investors might actually bring

That does not make the new investors irrelevant. Plausibly they offer capital, experience of building digital and consumer businesses, and introductions to companies and markets. The reported board places for Bhatia, Elaine Saverin and Baum would give them a voice in how the club is run if those appointments take effect.

But capabilities are not contracts. The evidence reviewed up to 9 October contained no announced Amazon or AWS sponsorship connected to the investment, no new investor-linked distribution agreement, no commitment from Bezos to fund transfers, and no quantified commercial plan from 1892 Holdings. A personal investment by Bezos does not give Liverpool control over Amazon's spending, any more than the Saverins' involvement creates a Meta partnership. If such a deal arrives, it will need to be judged on its own terms, including whether it would have happened anyway.

Some of what makes American franchises valuable is beyond any investor's reach. No business network can give Liverpool a permanent place in a closed league or a hard wage cap across European football. It cannot give them the right to sell live Premier League matches themselves either. Those would require changes to how football is organised, not a better commercial plan.

The clearest effect of the deal so far is financial. A well-funded group has agreed to buy a large minority stake at a reported price above Forbes's native sterling quote for its May estimate. That gives the market a new price signal. The reported £5.5bn and Forbes's £4.6bn are not a like-for-like comparison, though. Forbes measures enterprise value, including debt, while the evidence reviewed does not establish the equity-value and net-debt basis of the reported deal price. It is a reported price signal, not a directly comparable confirmed enterprise value.

## What £10bn would actually take

A simple way to test the £10bn idea is to work backwards. Pick a multiple, which is how many times its revenue a club sells for, and see how much revenue Liverpool would need to be worth £10bn. Then ask how fast that revenue would have to grow.

The growth figures below are compound annual growth rates: the steady yearly rate that gets you from one number to another over a set period. None of what follows is a forecast. It shows what would have to be true.

**If buyers keep paying approximately today's multiple.** The model uses audited revenue of £702.7m and the £4.81bn valuation normalised at the same exchange rate as the graphs. That gives a starting multiple of 6.84 times revenue, close to the table's 6.81 times based entirely on Forbes's dollar figures. At that multiple, £10bn requires £1.46bn of annual revenue. Reaching it in five years means 15.8% annual growth; over ten years, 7.6%. The ten-year rate is below Liverpool's historical nominal average, but sustained growth is harder from a much larger base. At 5% a year and the same multiple, the model reaches £7.83bn after a decade.

**If Liverpool earn a modest re-rating to 8 times revenue.** £10bn then needs £1.25bn of revenue, or 12.2% growth a year for five years and 5.9% for ten. Suppose all of that had to come from commercial income, with media and matchday flat. Commercial revenue would then need to reach about £871m, 10.4% growth a year for a decade. That is a stress test, not a prediction. It shows how much weight the commercial side would carry.

**If Liverpool are valued like an American franchise, at 12 times revenue.** That sharply reduces the revenue hurdle. Twelve times today's revenue is about £8.4bn, and £833m of annual revenue would meet the target: 3.5% annual growth over five years or 1.7% over ten. But that is the point: this scenario reaches £10bn by assuming the hard part away. Twelve times is about 75% above the model's starting multiple. None of the seven leading football clubs in this comparison commands that multiple in the Forbes estimates. That is evidence against treating it as a routine football valuation, rather than proof that it could never happen.

![Conditional revenue and growth requirements for a £10bn Liverpool valuation](/images/analysis/liverpool-bezos-ten-billion-question/chart-6.png)

*Conditional calculations for a £10bn enterprise value, using the same baseline and currency method as the text. The multiples are assumptions, not forecasts or promises of a sale.*

Profit is the other test. A revenue multiple says nothing about earnings, and serious buyers eventually look at earnings. Suppose a buyer valued Liverpool at 25 times EBITDA excluding player trading. That is an assumption for illustration, not a market rule. £10bn would then need about £400m a year of those earnings, about 4.5 times the £89.4m accounts-based figure after excluding player-disposal gains and the insurance recovery. A more modest 20% margin on £1.25bn of revenue would give £250m. A buyer would then have to pay 40 times that to reach £10bn.

The revenue model does not mathematically determine a required profit. The earnings calculation is a separate check on how demanding the valuation could be. Sustaining a high revenue multiple would require confidence in future earnings; wages, squad replacement and running costs all affect what Liverpool retain.

## What the evidence supports

The Liverpool–Lakers comparison exposes a large difference in the price attached to revenue. Closed-league membership, scarcity, spending rules and expectations of future income help explain the American premium, but the data cannot establish how much each contributes. Higher values also survive current losses at the Yankees and Dodgers.

Liverpool's revenue growth has exceeded five of the six European peers examined. That undermines the claim that a commercially dormant club needs a billionaire to wake it up. It does not establish a commercial ceiling or prove there is no room to improve.

The announced 1892 agreement could bring expertise and useful relationships. Its completion had not been publicly confirmed in the evidence reviewed, and its reported price does not establish new spending power for the club. No investor-driven revenue increase is included in the model.

£10bn is financially conceivable over a longer horizon. At approximately today's multiple it needs sustained growth to more than double annual revenue, with earnings strong enough to support that price. A premium American-style multiple lowers the revenue hurdle but has the weakest direct support in the football comparisons. Bezos's involvement has not yet supplied that missing evidence.

## How this was worked out

The evidence cutoff is 9 October 2026. The underlying investigation retrieved 84 sources. The linked sources below cover this article and its graphs. Forbes valuations and franchise financial figures are estimates, not audited results or completed sale prices.

Forbes comparisons use each league's latest retrieved valuation edition and corresponding financial season. Revenue and profit definitions differ across leagues; player accounting and venue adjustments prevent an exact comparison of cash returns. The main table divides dollar valuations by dollar revenue.

All monetary graphs use one analytical conversion: £1 = $1.29 and €1 = $1.09, taken from Forbes's stated 2024/25 average financial rates. Reusing them across the comparisons is a display convention, not a claim that they were the exchange rates on each valuation date. This is why Liverpool's $6.2bn estimate appears as £4.81bn here rather than Forbes's native £4.6bn quote.

Liverpool's financial baseline is its audited year ended 31 May 2025. Historical growth uses nominal endpoints over 15 years. Deloitte's European comparison is in euros and includes exchange-rate effects. The history graph includes ten-month accounts in 2012 and pandemic-related shifts in revenue timing; it is not an uninterrupted like-for-like annual series.

The £10bn model targets enterprise value. Required revenue is £10bn divided by the assumed revenue multiple. Required annual growth is calculated from £702.722m over five or ten years. The 8-times and 12-times revenue multiples, 20% earnings margin and 25-times EBITDA check are explicit analytical assumptions. No extra revenue is credited to the investors. Unknown completion and private terms remain unknown rather than being filled with estimates.
