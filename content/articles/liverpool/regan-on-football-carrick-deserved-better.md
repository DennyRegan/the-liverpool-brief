I thought Michael Carrick was out of his depth at Manchester United. I said as much in the last edition of this column, and I haven't changed my mind. But the way he's been treated this week has been a disgrace.

According to Sky Sports, United reached a verbal agreement with Mauricio Pochettino on Wednesday. On Thursday morning, Carrick was still turning up at Carrington for work, before his dismissal was announced.

We don't know exactly what Carrick was told privately, but the public sequence of events stinks. If you've decided a manager isn't the right man, tell him and let him leave with some dignity. Don't have everybody discussing his replacement while he's still reporting for work.

Carrick didn't deserve that. He did a good job for United last season, taking them to third place and earning the permanent position. Results have been dreadful this season, and I thought he'd lost control of his players. But that doesn't take away what he achieved or mean he deserved to be treated like this.

My biggest problem is that Carrick shouldn't be the only one losing his job.

Jason Wilcox and Omar Berrada recommended appointing him permanently. Jim Ratcliffe approved it. Now that it's gone wrong, the club is lining up his replacement. At what point are they held accountable for their decisions?

Ratcliffe should be looking at Wilcox and Berrada. They've made another managerial appointment that hasn't worked out, yet it's the manager who takes all the responsibility.

I watched Berrada celebrating United's late equaliser against Aston Villa. A point that left them sitting 18th in the Premier League. The chief executive jumping around in the stands while Manchester United are in a relegation battle. I couldn't believe it.

And Ratcliffe? I've never been convinced he understands football. He's a wealthy man who seems to think he knows everything, but from what I've seen, he knows very little about running a football club. As a Liverpool supporter, long may he continue.

As for Pochettino, I think he's one of the most overrated managers around.

He's better than Carrick, and I expect him to keep United up. But his reputation still seems to rest largely on what he achieved at Tottenham nearly a decade ago.

He had a settled team featuring Harry Kane and Dele Alli at a time when Klopp was only beginning his work at Liverpool and Guardiola had yet to build his Manchester City side. The traditional big clubs were struggling, and Tottenham had a rare opportunity to win the league.

They didn't take it. Once Klopp and Guardiola had built their teams, Liverpool and City reached levels Tottenham never matched.

Having said that, perhaps it's unfair to judge Pochettino too harshly for not winning a league title at a club that hasn't managed one since 1961. He did a good job at Tottenham, and he deserves recognition for that.

But he's been around the block since, and his major trophies have come at PSG. I don't see somebody who will turn United back into Premier League champions.

He might steady the ship and keep them in the division. But getting Manchester United back to where they ultimately want to be, winning league titles, is a completely different challenge.

And they're a million miles away from that anyway.

## Manchester City's problems go well beyond the pitch

Manchester City started the season so well. Then came the guilty verdict on their financial charges, followed by their first game afterwards, against Liverpool at Anfield.

To be fair to them, they performed very well that day, winning 3-0. But something has gone wrong since.

Nottingham Forest beat them 3-1 last weekend. I'll be honest, I only watched the goals rather than the whole match, so I'm not going to pretend I've studied every aspect of City's performance. But Liam Delap looked as though he was bullying their defence.

Following Pep Guardiola was always going to be a difficult job for Enzo Maresca, regardless of everything else happening at the club.

And what a situation he's inherited.

An independent commission has found City guilty of serious financial rule breaches. City deny the findings and have appealed. Even when that appeal is resolved, there's still the question of sanctions. There's also talk of potentially going to the High Court.

This could drag on for another couple of years.

I've seen plenty of talk in the media about City's players potentially speaking to their agents about moves elsewhere. I don't know whether those conversations are taking place, but surely the uncertainty surrounding the club's future cannot be a good thing for the players.

Could that be contributing to their recent results? Possibly. Could Maresca be struggling with the job of succeeding Guardiola? Of course. It could be a combination of both.

What I will say is that I'm glad City's results have dropped off.

It would be a travesty, in my opinion, if Manchester City won another Premier League title while the consequences of those financial breaches remained unresolved. Particularly if the legal proceedings continue to drag on.

And there's another part of this situation that I don't think gets enough consideration: the supporters of other clubs.

Some Manchester City fans seem largely unbothered by what's happened. They're concerned about their own club, but what about everybody else?

Think about Sunderland losing the 2014 League Cup final to Manchester City. Sunderland took the lead that day before eventually losing 3-1. How many opportunities does a club like Sunderland get to win a major trophy at Wembley?

For supporters of clubs like that, those occasions might come around once or twice in a lifetime. Some might never get another opportunity.

If the findings against City stand, what does that mean for the legitimacy of those successes and the opportunities other clubs missed out on?

Football isn't just about the clubs that win everything. It's about those supporters who've waited years for a chance to see their team win something special.

They deserve consideration, too.

## Henry Winter's remarkable top five

Henry Winter has been covering football for decades. He's watched some of the greatest players ever to play the game.

So imagine my surprise when I saw, on talkSPORT, his selection of the five greatest players he'd personally watched.

Maradona first. Messi second. Cristiano Ronaldo third. Zidane fourth.

And Jude Bellingham fifth.

Where has that come from?

I sent it to my dad, who texted me straight back:

"Henry Winter wants an interview with Bellingham."

That made me laugh.

This isn't a criticism of Jude Bellingham. He's a top player, and by the end of his career he may well deserve to be mentioned in the same breath as Zinedine Zidane.

But he's 23 years old. How can anybody seriously put him fifth among the greatest players they've ever seen?

I could understand a 20-year-old making that selection based on the players they've grown up watching. But somebody like Henry Winter, who's watched football for the best part of 50 years?

Come on.

Bellingham has plenty of time to build a career that justifies that kind of recognition. He might get there one day.

But he isn't there yet.
