Every international break comes with a familiar explanation. Players are scattered across three continents, get a couple of days' training together, then go straight back into a league game. If Liverpool drop points, the break is quickly part of the conversation.

Jürgen Klopp's version of the complaint was more specific. In November 2023 Liverpool were given a 12.30pm Saturday kick-off at Manchester City, the first game after the break. "How can you put a game like this on Saturday at 12.30pm?" he asked. His point was about preparation. There would be one training session. He estimated that the two squads had around 30 internationals between them. The South Americans would all come back on the same plane.

It was the third lunchtime return in a row. So did being sent out early after the break cost Liverpool? I went through every Premier League season from 2015–16 to 2025–26 to find out.

## Liverpool's record after the break

Liverpool played 40 first league games after an international break in that period. They won 25, drew nine and lost six. That is 2.10 points per game, against 2.06 in their other league matches in the same seasons.

Allowing for how strong the opposition was, and whether the game was home or away, Liverpool came out about 0.12 points per game better after breaks. The uncertainty is wide, though. The data fits anything from a cost of around 0.2 points per game to a gain of around 0.45. That is not enough to call it an advantage, and it can't rule out a small cost. What it doesn't show is an obvious drop.

The Klopp years look the same, only more so. His Liverpool played 31 first games back, winning 20, drawing nine and losing two. That is 2.23 points per game, against 2.10 everywhere else. In 2018–19 and 2021–22 they won all four first games back. The worst season was 2022–23: a 3–3 draw with Brighton and a 4–1 defeat at the Etihad, one point from two games in a season that was poor throughout.

## The scheduling was real

Klopp wasn't imagining it. Under him, 14 of Liverpool's 31 first games back kicked off before 1pm. That is 45 per cent. In their other league games, the figure was 11 per cent. The three he complained about in 2023 were Wolves away, Everton at home and City away, all at 12.30.

Liverpool were sent out at lunchtime after the break far more often than at any other point in the season.

## Did lunchtime hurt?

On the raw numbers, it looks like it might have. In the 14 lunchtime returns, Liverpool won eight, drew four and lost two. That is 2.00 points per game. In the 17 later kick-offs, they won 12, drew five and never lost: 2.41 points per game. Both defeats came at the Etihad at lunchtime, 5–0 in September 2017 and 4–1 in April 2023.

The lunchtime games were harder, though. Liverpool's early opponents averaged 1.62 points per game over their seasons, against 1.46 in the later returns. Liverpool were also away in nine of the 14 early games, compared with eight of 17 later ones.

Allowing for opposition and venue, the lunchtime returns showed no clear shortfall against Liverpool's ordinary league games. With only 14 early returns, the adjusted comparison with later kick-offs is too uncertain to establish a lunchtime penalty.

That means harder fixtures could explain much of the difference. It doesn't show that kick-off time played no part. Fourteen games can't settle it either way.

## Why points can't settle Klopp's argument

There is a limit to what results can show here, and it works in Klopp's favour. His complaint applied to both teams. City had their own internationals on the same planes. A lunchtime game between two under-prepared sides can be worse football without either of them dropping points they otherwise would have won.

Points measure Liverpool against their opponent. They don't measure how either side played compared with a fully prepared version of itself. Liverpool winning after poor preparation doesn't make the preparation adequate.

## Did the clubs with more internationals suffer more?

One rough test is to compare the big six's first games back against the rest of the league with their other games against the rest in the same seasons.

There was no clear drop in points. The big six averaged 2.20 points in those games, against 2.07 in the same kind of games elsewhere in the season. Allowing for opposition and venue, the estimate leans positive, but not clearly. This is only a rough test. Big-six membership stands in for international involvement, rather than counting actual call-ups, minutes played or flights.

## What this doesn't tell us

Nothing here measures fatigue, injuries or individual players. Someone who has flown back from South America on a Thursday might suffer in ways a team's results never show. Clubs also manage these weeks carefully, and what this shows is the result after all that management.

Across Arne Slot's two seasons, Liverpool won five of eight first games back. Last season's two home defeats after breaks, to Manchester United and Nottingham Forest, came in a difficult season overall.

Liverpool's next first game back is Manchester City at Anfield on 11 October, at 4.30pm, not lunchtime. If it goes wrong, the international break will be discussed. On eleven seasons of evidence, Liverpool's results after breaks don't back that up. Klopp's complaint about the scheduling, though, stands.

## How this was worked out

The figures were independently recalculated from Football-Data's public Premier League records for 2015–16 to 2025–26. All 4,180 fixture dates, team pairings and scores matched ESPN's records. Selected Liverpool results and kick-off times were also checked against club reports.

An international break is any league-wide gap of 12 days or more in August to November or March. The Covid suspension, the September 2022 postponements after the Queen's death and the 2022 World Cup are excluded. That leaves 40 breaks. "First game back" is each club's first league game after a break, provided it was played within a week of the league restarting.

Liverpool are compared only with their own other league games in the same season. Opponent strength is the opponent's points per game that season, not counting the match being measured. The ranges quoted are 95 per cent intervals: the range the data plausibly supports under the model's assumptions, not a hard limit. The lunchtime comparison covers Klopp's tenure and uses UK local kick-off times from ESPN.

This piece deliberately doesn't use a league-wide average. After a break, both teams in almost every fixture are playing their first game back. In total, 800 club results come from 400 matches. Across the whole league, one side's win is the other's defeat, so the average mostly reflects how many games were drawn, not whether anyone played worse.
