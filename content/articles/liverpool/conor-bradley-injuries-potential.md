During the 2022/23 season, Conor Bradley made 53 appearances on loan at Bolton Wanderers. He was just 19 years old, playing regular senior football and collecting individual awards along the way. Three years later, he's recovering from a serious knee injury, having yet to make an appearance for Liverpool this season. It's a remarkable contrast for a player who has shown so much ability, and it raises a question that could ultimately define his career: will he ever be able to stay fit consistently?

There is no doubt in my mind that Bradley has the ability to become one of the best right-backs in the Premier League. When he's fully fit, he's sensational. He gives everything, gets stuck into challenges, is defensively solid and offers plenty going forward. He's everything I like in a footballer. But at just 23 years old, his biggest obstacle isn't a lack of ability. It's his availability.

I was reading the match report from Liverpool's 4–1 victory over Chelsea in January 2024. Bradley was just 20 years old, still establishing himself in the first team, yet he produced an outstanding performance. One goal, two assists and a standing ovation when he left the pitch. Then there was his performance against Real Madrid in November that year. The crunching tackle on Kylian Mbappé is something I still remember, but his overall performance was excellent, earning him Liverpool's Player of the Match award.

These weren't ordinary performances from a young player finding his feet. They showed exactly what Liverpool had on their hands. But the longer his injury problems continue, the more difficult it becomes to dismiss them as an unfortunate spell for a young player.

## What does Bradley's injury record tell us?

Since returning to Liverpool, his first-team involvement has been limited by injuries and competition for places. He played 23 matches in 2023/24, followed by 29 in 2024/25 and 21 in 2025/26 before his serious knee injury. Including his five appearances before the Bolton loan, that gives him 78 competitive appearances for Liverpool across four seasons in which he featured. As of 29 October 2026, he has yet to appear this season.

Those appearance figures are concerning, but they don't tell the complete story. A player missing from a starting line-up isn't necessarily injured. He might be on the bench, rested or simply not selected. To understand Bradley's problems properly, we need to look at the periods when injuries actually prevented him from playing.

In the summer of 2023, he suffered a stress fracture in his back. Bradley later explained that he'd developed back pain following a running session, which led to a scan revealing the injury. That kept him out of the opening months of the 2023/24 campaign. He returned to competitive action at the end of November, only to suffer an ankle injury against Crystal Palace the following April.

Then came the muscle problems. Bradley was injured during the Real Madrid victory in November 2024 and didn't return to competitive action until January. Another injury against Aston Villa in February 2025 cost him six Liverpool matches before he resumed training. By April, Arne Slot was describing the problem as repetitive while discussing the need to manage his return carefully.

The difficulties continued into the following campaign. An injury interrupted his preparations for 2025/26, while another muscle problem in November caused him to miss four Liverpool matches. He eventually returned to the starting line-up against Leeds United on 6 December.

What makes these figures more concerning is that they represent separate periods of unavailability rather than one lengthy absence. Yet there is an important medical distinction. Although Bradley has experienced repeated muscle problems, the publicly available information doesn't establish that every injury involved the same muscle or even the same part of his body. A back stress fracture, an ankle injury and a muscle strain aren't the same medical problem.

His latest setback is significantly different again. On 8 January 2026, Bradley suffered a serious knee injury against Arsenal, landing awkwardly late in the match. Liverpool confirmed that he would require surgery and begin rehabilitation, but provided no return date. Subsequent reporting described damage involving bone and ligaments, although the injury was not understood to involve a ruptured anterior cruciate ligament.

More than nine months later, he remains unavailable. Liverpool's injury information, last updated on 23 October, still lists him among the absentees. There has been some encouragement, with Andoni Iraola indicating in mid-October that Bradley was approaching a stage where he could participate in some group work. But that isn't the same as being ready for competitive football, and the club has not confirmed when he'll return.

## Can young players eventually grow out of injury problems?

What interests me about Bradley's earlier spell of regular football is the contrast with what has followed. It raises a question I've often wondered about with young players: can playing so much football at an early age eventually catch up with them? Are some young bodies simply not ready for the demands being placed on them?

Michael Owen immediately springs to mind. He was playing regularly for Liverpool and England while still a teenager, and suffered a serious hamstring injury against Leeds United in April 1999, aged 19.

What makes Owen particularly interesting is that he has questioned this himself. In an interview with the sports medicine publication *Aspetar*, he wondered whether he'd played too many games before his body was fully developed. He explained that he'd been involved in international football from youth level through to the senior team, while also playing regularly for Liverpool from the age of 17. He questioned whether that workload had ultimately worked against him.

Owen's injury problems affected the rest of his career, although he continued to score goals and play at the highest level. But even Owen was asking a question rather than establishing a medical explanation. His experience doesn't prove that playing too much football when young causes injuries later in life.

Bradley's earlier availability tells us that he was capable of playing regularly at that stage of his career. It doesn't tell us whether his workload contributed to problems suffered years later. The demands of Premier League football are different from League One, but there is no evidence establishing that the change in level caused his injuries.

Medical research into young footballers shows that growth and physical maturity can influence certain injury patterns. However, it doesn't establish that young professionals generally grow out of their injuries as they get older.

There are examples, though, of players whose availability improved substantially as their careers progressed.

Robin van Persie suffered various injury problems earlier in his Arsenal career, making just 15 Premier League appearances in 2007/08 and 16 in 2009/10. Yet he went on to appear in all 38 league matches for Arsenal in 2011/12, scoring 30 goals, before playing another 38 for Manchester United the following season and helping them win the title.

That's an encouraging comparison. Van Persie demonstrated that an earlier history of injuries doesn't necessarily prevent a player from later enjoying prolonged periods of consistent football. It doesn't establish that Bradley will follow the same path, but it shows that improvement is possible.

And then there's Reece James, probably the most relevant comparison because they're both excellent right-backs whose careers have been affected by their availability.

James has suffered repeated injury setbacks throughout his Chelsea career. In 2022/23, he made just 16 Premier League appearances. That fell to 10 in 2023/24 before recovering to 19 in 2024/25.

There were signs of improvement last season, when James made 39 appearances in all competitions, including 29 league games. That was encouraging after several difficult years. Yet on 16 October 2026, Chelsea confirmed that James had undergone another knee operation following an injury sustained in training. At 26, he is once again facing a period on the sidelines.

James is the player Bradley reminds me of most. They play the same position, are both excellent when fit, but have struggled to put together consistent runs of football. James's problems have continued over a longer period, and his experience demonstrates just how difficult it can be to establish that consistency, even after an encouraging season.

The medical research offers some reason for caution. Studies involving professional footballers have identified previous injury as a risk factor for subsequent injury, while research into hamstring injuries has found an increased risk among athletes who have suffered them before. However, those findings concern groups of athletes, not Bradley individually, and his publicly reported injuries haven't always been described in sufficient detail to establish whether they're related.

Van Persie and James demonstrate different possible outcomes. Neither can tell us what will happen to Bradley, but both are relevant when considering what the coming years might hold.

## Could this long absence actually benefit Bradley?

I was discussing Bradley's situation with my dad recently and wondered whether this extended period away from competitive football might actually do him some good in the long run. Not the knee injury itself, obviously, but the fact that he isn't going through the demands of Premier League football every few days.

Rehabilitation isn't simply a matter of resting. It involves rebuilding strength, mobility and fitness before gradually returning to more demanding football activities. But for a period, Bradley won't face the same competitive sprints, challenges and repeated matches that come with playing regularly.

Could that give his body an opportunity to recover more fully from some of the smaller issues that may have accumulated over time? Could he eventually return in better overall condition?

I don't know. I'm not a doctor, and the idea could be completely wrong. He's recovering from a serious knee injury, which presents its own challenges, and there is no medical evidence specific to Bradley showing that this absence will improve his previous muscle problems.

But it's something I've wondered about. At 23, he could still have plenty of football ahead of him. Whether this period proves to be a turning point or another chapter in a career interrupted by injuries remains to be seen.

## What should Liverpool do?

For Liverpool, the decision ultimately comes down to whether Bradley can demonstrate that he's capable of staying fit. I wouldn't have any concerns about his ability to be the club's first-choice right-back. The concern is whether he can be relied upon throughout a long season.

But there's another problem. Liverpool's other options at right-back aren't exactly reassuring either.

Jeremie Frimpong is already at the club, but I don't think he's good enough to be the answer in that position, and he's had injury problems himself. He suffered another muscle injury against Brentford in October. Joe Gomez is another alternative, but his injury record hardly inspires confidence. The physio has his hands full at Liverpool just dealing with the right-backs.

And then there's Big Ronald. Ronald Araújo has been excellent filling in at right-back, and he seems to perform every time he's called upon. I wrote recently about his performance against Brighton, where he went flying into a 50-50 challenge and even tackled the ball with his head while lying on the floor. That's what I like about him. He gets stuck in, fights for the shirt and sets the tone with his defending. But he's only on loan from Barcelona, and his natural position is centre-back. However well he's performed, I still think Liverpool need to sign a specialist right-back.

The type of signing they make, however, should depend on what happens with Bradley.

If he returns and manages to stay fit for four or five months, I'd be happy for Liverpool to continue with him as first choice. They would still need another right-back, but I would expect that to be someone brought in as dependable backup rather than an expensive replacement.

If his injuries continue, that changes things completely. Liverpool would need to spend more money on a player capable of starting every week. They cannot keep planning around Bradley being available if he repeatedly finds himself on the sidelines.

Of course, four or five months of consistent football wouldn't guarantee that his problems were behind him. He could play until the end of the season, convince Liverpool he was ready to establish himself, and then suffer another injury after the transfer window closes. That possibility can't simply be dismissed.

But Liverpool have to make a decision based on what they see. A sustained spell of fitness would give them evidence that Bradley can cope with regular football again. Another setback would strengthen the case for signing a first-choice replacement. Either way, they need a dependable specialist in the squad rather than relying on players who have their own availability problems or are covering outside their natural position.

I genuinely hope this injury is the beginning of a change in fortune for Bradley. There would be few things better than seeing him back in the Liverpool team, performing like he did against Chelsea and Real Madrid, and finally putting together a long run of matches.

But Liverpool cannot make their plans based on hope alone. Bradley has already shown what he's capable of. What remains unanswered is whether they can rely on him to do it consistently.
