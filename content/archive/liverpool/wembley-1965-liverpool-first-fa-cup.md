Liverpool had been champions of England six times before they ever won the FA Cup.

By the spring of 1965 the omission had become conspicuous. The club was 73 years old. It had reached two finals and lost both — 1-0 to Burnley in 1914, 2-0 to Arsenal in 1950 — and the oldest cup competition in the world had never come to Anfield. Bill Shankly had already reset most of the club's expectations: Second Division when he arrived in December 1959, promoted as champions in 1961-62, champions of England in 1963-64 for the first time since 1947. The cup was the obvious gap.

The league defence had gone badly; Liverpool finished seventh. Everything else that season was expanding. In their first European Cup campaign they reached the semi-finals, and the manner of it matters to what happened at Wembley.

Their quarter-final against Cologne took three matches and six weeks: goalless in Germany, goalless at Anfield, then 2-2 after extra time on neutral ground in Rotterdam, St John and Hunt having put Liverpool two up. With no provision for penalties, the tie was settled by a coin, tossed by the Belgian referee Robert Schaut between Ron Yeats and Wolfgang Overath. Yeats won it. That was 24 March. Three days later Liverpool played Chelsea — top of the table and chasing a treble — in the FA Cup semi-final at Villa Park, and won 2-0 through Peter Thompson's 63rd-minute goal and a Willie Stevenson penalty twelve minutes from the end. Tommy Docherty had said beforehand that a Liverpool win after 120 minutes in Rotterdam would be the greatest thing any English club had done.

The earlier rounds had been no gentler: West Bromwich Albion beaten 2-1, a replay needed to see off Fourth Division Stockport County, Bolton edged 1-0, another replay against Leicester City.

## Two clubs going the same way

Leeds arrived at Wembley for the first time in their history, and in a peculiar state. Promoted from the Second Division in 1964, Don Revie's side had finished runners-up in their first season back, losing the championship to Manchester United on goal average only days before the final. Two managers were building teams that would define English football for a decade, and they met before either had won very much.

Liverpool had one enforced change. Gordon Milne was injured, and Geoff Strong — signed from Arsenal the previous November, and valued for being able to play almost anywhere — took his place at right-half.

There was a landmark on the other side. Albert Johanneson, the Leeds winger from South Africa, became the first Black player to appear in an FA Cup final, a fact the Football Association has since formally marked.

A hundred thousand were inside Wembley on Saturday 1 May, with Bill Clements refereeing. Liverpool lined up Lawrence; Lawler, Byrne; Strong, Yeats, Stevenson; Callaghan, Hunt, St John, Smith, Thompson. Leeds had Sprake, Reaney, Bell, Bremner, Charlton, Hunter, Giles, Storrie, Peacock, the captain Bobby Collins, and Johanneson.

## The third minute

By the club's own account, the collision came in the third minute. The ball ran loose towards Byrne; Collins threw himself at it, studs into Byrne's leg and shoulder into his body. Byrne's collarbone was broken.

There were no substitutes in 1965. Taking him off meant ten men for almost the whole of a cup final. He stayed on, and Shankly and Bob Paisley kept the injury from Leeds — teammates later described how white he had gone, and that he would not do anything about it. Accounts differ on how quickly Byrne himself understood the damage: Paisley's version has him grasping it early and insisting on silence, while Byrne's own recollection was that his attention went first to his leg. The central fact is not in dispute.

Nor is what he did with the rest of the afternoon, which was considerably more than stand in a position.

The ninety minutes were hard and unproductive. Liverpool had the better of the pressure and Sprake, then 20, repeatedly kept them out; Leeds offered almost nothing going forward. In steady London rain the final ended goalless and went to extra time.

Liverpool had a particular reason to settle it. A replay would have meant Maine Road four days on, and Internazionale — the European champions — were due at Anfield three days after the final for the first leg of the European Cup semi-final. Leeds had no such fixture waiting.

Three minutes into extra time, Stevenson beat two players and pushed the ball left to Byrne. The full-back with the broken collarbone beat his man on the overlap, took it to the byline and crossed. Hunt stooped to head it past Sprake, and Liverpool led a cup final for the first time in their history.

It lasted about eight minutes. Norman Hunter sent a long ball forward, Jack Charlton went up and headed it down, and Bremner met it with a half-volley that flew past Lawrence into the top corner — close to Leeds's only shot of the afternoon.

Liverpool had to win it twice. On 111 minutes Callaghan went down the right and floated a cross into the six-yard box, and St John threw himself at it, heading down and past Sprake. This time Leeds had no answer, and Yeats became the first Liverpool captain to lift the FA Cup.

## What it was worth

The goals belonged to Hunt and St John, the forwards Shankly's first championship side had been built around. But Byrne is inseparable from the afternoon, and not as decoration. He was hurt in the third minute and was still overlapping in the ninety-third. Shankly's verdict was that Byrne "should have had all the medals to himself". He would go on to be part of England's World Cup squad the following summer.

There is a temptation to improve old finals in the retelling. This one does not need it, and would not survive the attempt: the football was attritional and the first ninety minutes produced very little. Its interest lies elsewhere — in what Liverpool were trying to end, in the obduracy of a Leeds side that had just lost a title on goal average, in a player carrying an injury that would take him off the pitch inside a minute today, and in three goals arriving in half an hour after none in an hour and a half.

The fortnight did not stop there. Three nights later Byrne and Milne, neither fit to play, paraded the cup at Anfield before Liverpool beat Inter 3-1. A week after that, at San Siro, the Italians won 3-0 and went through 4-3. UEFA's own account records Liverpool's objection to the legality of Joaquín Peiró's goal in the second leg; the result stood, and Inter retained the European Cup.

Milan did not diminish Wembley. League title in 1964, FA Cup in 1965, league title again in 1966: the sequence established Shankly's first side as something more durable than a breakthrough. Later Liverpool teams made winning trophies look ordinary. In 1965 there was nothing ordinary about it at all — 73 years, six championships, two lost finals, and then a header from a cross delivered by a left-back who should not have been on the pitch.
