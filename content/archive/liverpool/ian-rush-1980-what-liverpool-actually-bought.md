When Liverpool signed Ian Rush from Chester at the end of April 1980, they were buying an 18-year-old Third Division forward who had never played in the top flight. The fee was £300,000. Within a month The Times was describing him as Britain's most expensive teenager, and Chester's own history still records the sale as the biggest in the club's existence.

The story that grew around it is easily told: Rush arrived, could not score, thought about leaving, and was remade by Bob Paisley into the greatest goalscorer Liverpool have had. The records show something more interesting. Rush did go nine senior appearances without a goal, but those nine were spread across five months and only one of them came before April 1981. Liverpool had spent a record fee on a teenager and then kept him almost entirely out of the first team.

That is the part of the transfer worth examining. His eventual return — 346 goals in 660 appearances, by the club's own count — was extraordinary. Almost nothing in his first year predicted it.

## From Chester to Anfield

Rush was being watched well before Liverpool moved. Reporting Chester's 2-1 FA Cup defeat at Ipswich in February 1980, Gerry Harrison of The Times described the 18-year-old as a player drawing representatives from First and Second Division clubs carrying substantial offers. Two months later, days before the transfer, the paper called him Chester's promising striker when he was named in a Wales squad.

Rush's own account is that Liverpool approached him in February or March and he turned them down — he was content at Chester and unsure whether he was good enough for the reigning champions. He has said he tried to price himself out of the move by demanding £100 a week, and that Alan Oakes, Chester's player-manager, simply agreed to it. When Liverpool came back, Paisley showed Rush, his father and Oakes around Anfield and Melwood, and Oakes told him that if it did not work out Chester would have him back.

Manchester City had looked too. Oakes recalled in 2005 that Tony Book and Malcolm Allison watched Rush during Chester's cup run, that Book wanted to sign him and that Allison did not rate him highly enough for the deal to proceed. That is a recollection given twenty-five years afterwards, and no contemporary City record has been found to sit alongside it, but it comes from the manager who was there and Rush has separately remembered City's interest.

Who at Liverpool found him is harder to pin down than the retellings suggest. The club names chief scout Geoff Twentyman among those whose talent-spotting produced Rush, and says his surviving scouting book contains reports on him. Rush himself remembered Tom Saunders and Paisley watching. Several people can assess the same player, and the evidence supports Liverpool having followed him closely rather than any single figure discovering him.

The Times reported on 29 April that Rush had joined the previous day for £300,000 after passing a medical, having scored 16 goals that season. That places the deal on 28 April, though Liverpool now marks 1 May as the anniversary. Later accounts, including Rush's own, have described the fee as a world record for a teenager. The contemporary reporting supports the British claim, not the wider one.

## Nine appearances, no goals

Rush played for Wales before he played for Liverpool. He came on for Ian Walsh in a 1-0 defeat by Scotland on 21 May 1980, three weeks after signing.

His Liverpool debut took until 13 December, and he got it because Dalglish was ruled out of the league match at Ipswich. Rush started in the No.7 shirt and Liverpool drew 1-1. Then he went back to the reserves for more than three months.

His second appearance was the League Cup final replay against West Ham at Villa Park on 1 April 1981. Liverpool won it, and Rush collected a winners' medal in his second game for the club.

A third start came days later against Stoke. Then, on 8 April, Liverpool played Bayern Munich at Anfield in the first leg of the European Cup semi-final, and Rush started again. He was 19, with three senior appearances behind him, in the last four of the European Cup. The tie finished goalless and Liverpool went through on away goals in Munich a fortnight later. Rush did not play in the final in Paris.

Five more starts followed through April and early May, against Nottingham Forest, Manchester United, Tottenham, Sunderland and Middlesbrough. He ended 1980-81 with nine appearances, no goals, and six further occasions as an unused substitute. What he had done instead was play reserve-team football.

That record is not what a scoring drought looks like. It is not a centre-forward being picked repeatedly and failing. It is one appearance in December, nothing until April, and then a burst of games at the end of a season in which Liverpool finished fifth. The most expensive teenager in British football spent his first year at the club being developed rather than used.

Rush has described that as deliberate — Liverpool's reserves played the same system as the first team, so a young player could learn the method somewhere mistakes were survivable. It was not comfortable. Back in the reserves at the start of 1981-82, he knocked on Paisley's door and asked to leave. By his account Paisley told him he was not scoring because he was not selfish enough, agreed to make him available for transfer, and sent him out determined to prove otherwise. He remembers scoring five in his first four reserve games, and finding out later that Paisley had never intended to sell him.

Liverpool's official history confirms the substance — Rush was unhappy, Paisley had no interest in letting him go. The dialogue is Rush's own.

Then it happened all at once. On 30 September 1981 Rush came off the bench against Oulu Palloseura in the European Cup and scored in a 7-0 win. It was his tenth senior appearance. A week later he scored twice against Exeter in the League Cup; three days after that, two more against Leeds, his first in the league. Nine appearances without a goal, then five in three.

He finished 1981-82 with 30 goals in 49 games.

## What Liverpool actually bought

The temptation is to read the transfer backwards. A forward who scored 346 goals for the club makes £300,000 look obvious. It was not obvious in April 1980.

What Liverpool committed to was a record teenage fee for an 18-year-old from the Third Division with no top-flight experience and, on the contemporary evidence, a rising reputation rather than an established one. What they did next is the more instructive half. They did not let the size of the fee push him into the team. He waited nearly eight months for a debut, three and a half more for a second appearance, and finished the season without a goal, having spent almost all of it in the reserves.

Identifying Rush was one judgement. Holding their nerve afterwards was the other, and it is the one that gets left out. Liverpool bought a player who was not yet good enough for the side they had, and then gave him the time to become good enough for it.

By the end of the following season the return was 30 in 49. Across his Liverpool career it became 346 in 660. The first year looks like a footnote to that. It was closer to the reason for it.
