For all the players who have worn the shirt, one distinction still belongs to Michael Owen alone. In December 2001, days after his 22nd birthday, he became the first Liverpool player to win the Ballon d'Or — the France Football prize then known in Britain as European Footballer of the Year. More than two decades on, he remains the only player to have won it while at the club.

He did not scrape it. Owen took 176 points, 36 clear of Real Madrid's Raúl on 140. Oliver Kahn was third with 114 and David Beckham fourth on 102; Francesco Totti (57) and the previous year's winner Luís Figo (56) were next, neither with a third of Owen's total. The electorate was 51 journalists from UEFA's member countries, each ranking five players. He was the fourth Englishman to win it, after Stanley Matthews, Bobby Charlton and Kevin Keegan — and Keegan had won both of his after leaving Anfield for Hamburg.

The award can look odd at a distance. Owen won no league title in 2001 and it was not the start of his rise; he had shared the Premier League Golden Boot in 1997-98 and 1998-99, and the 1998 World Cup had already made him famous. Injuries had interrupted him. What made 2001 different was not that he played better than before, but where the goals landed.

## Rome, Cardiff, Dortmund

Liverpool went into 2000-01 without a major trophy since 1995 and outside the Champions League. Gérard Houllier's side was becoming difficult to beat; whether it could win anything remained an open question.

Owen finished the season with 24 goals in all competitions, the best return of his Liverpool career. Two of them came at the Stadio Olimpico in February, in the first leg of a last-16 UEFA Cup tie against a Roma side assembled by Fabio Capello around Batistuta, Cafu and Samuel — a team that would win Serie A for the first time in eighteen years. Owen was making his first start in five weeks. The first half was goalless; Christian Ziege's cross for the second goal was headed past Francesco Antonioli. Liverpool lost the return at Anfield 1-0, Owen missing a penalty, but went through.

Ten days after Rome came the League Cup, won against Birmingham City on penalties in Cardiff. The FA Cup final took Liverpool back to the same stadium in May, and produced the club performance most firmly attached to Owen's year.

Liverpool were second best for most of the afternoon and trailed to Freddie Ljungberg's goal with less than twenty minutes left. Owen equalised in the 83rd minute and, five minutes later, ran onto Patrik Berger's long pass and finished across David Seaman. Two goals in five minutes turned Arsenal's final into Liverpool's second trophy of the season.

It is worth being precise about what that performance actually was. Owen did not spend the afternoon tormenting Arsenal's defence. His contribution was the capacity to settle a match the moment an opening appeared — acceleration, movement and finishing, compressed into the last few minutes of a game his team had been losing.

Four days later Liverpool met Deportivo Alavés in Dortmund. Owen did not score in the 5-4 win, but his pass released Steven Gerrard for the second goal, and the match ended in extra time on Delfí Geli's own goal under the golden-goal rule. League Cup, FA Cup and UEFA Cup; third in the league, and the Champions League with it.

## The year that kept going

The season was over. The trophies were not.

Liverpool beat Manchester United 2-1 in the Charity Shield in August, Owen among the scorers. Twelve days later came the Super Cup against Bayern Munich in Monaco, the European champions against the UEFA Cup holders, with Kahn in the Bayern goal — named European Goalkeeper of the Year in the same principality the previous evening. Owen's low cross gave Riise the opening goal on his old club's ground and Owen made it three within seconds of the restart before Bayern pulled two back. He was named man of the match. Ottmar Hitzfeld said individual mistakes had cost Bayern the game, and that his side "could not counter his threat."

Five trophies in a calendar year, then — League Cup, FA Cup, UEFA Cup, Charity Shield, Super Cup. But the performance that pushed Owen's year beyond Liverpool came a week later.

On 1 September England went to the Olympiastadion for a World Cup qualifier. Germany had lost one of their previous sixty qualifiers and had been unbeaten at that stadium since 1973. Carsten Jancker put them ahead after six minutes. Owen equalised on twelve, Gerrard scored in first-half stoppage time, and Owen added two more in the second half before Emile Heskey finished it. Germany 1, England 5. It was the last match Germany ever played at the ground.

That night matters to the Ballon d'Or case because it removed any suggestion that Owen's year could be read purely as a function of Liverpool's cup run. Kahn, third in the eventual vote, had conceded five.

## The vote, and what it did not mean

France Football's result came in the third week of December. Owen learned of it before the public did. According to Owen, Liverpool were in Rome for a Champions League match — the goalless draw at the Olimpico on 5 December — when Phil Thompson, standing in while Houllier recovered from heart surgery, sent him to the dressing room an hour before kick-off because the manager wanted to speak to him by telephone. Houllier told him it was top secret. Owen's own memory of the moment is less reverent than the occasion suggests: he has said he did not really know what the award was, because it barely registered in England at the time, and that when the trophy was finally presented to him on the Anfield pitch months later his instinct was to get it off the grass because the game was about to start.

One distinction needs making, because it is routinely collapsed. The Ballon d'Or was not then a world award and 2001 did not produce a single agreed verdict. FIFA's World Player of the Year, voted for by national-team coaches and announced in Zürich the day before, went to Figo on 250 points, with Beckham second on 238 and Raúl third on 96. Owen finished eighth with 61.

The two electorates were different and they reached different conclusions. Owen should not be described retrospectively as having been judged the best footballer on earth in 2001. What he did was win European football's most established individual prize, by a wide margin, in a field containing Raúl, Kahn, Beckham, Totti and Figo. That is the achievement, and stating it accurately makes it easier to see rather than harder.

It also makes more sense reconstructed as a sequence than reduced to a goal tally. Two in Rome. Two in the closing minutes at Cardiff. The pass for Gerrard in Dortmund. A goal against Manchester United. A goal and an assist against Bayern. Three in Munich. Not every trophy was his, and Liverpool's season was emphatically collective, but in an unusual number of the year's most visible matches the decisive contribution came from the same player.

There is an irony in the timing. Owen has since said he did not regard 2001 as the period of his best individual football, pointing instead to the earlier seasons when, still a teenager, he won consecutive Golden Boots. He may well be right. The Ballon d'Or, though, has never been a pure measure of form. It records a convergence — of personal performance, team achievement and international visibility — and in Owen's case all three arrived at once and then stopped.

Liverpool went into 2001 trying to re-establish themselves in Europe and came out of it with five trophies, a Champions League place and a 22-year-old striker ranked above the best players on the continent. Others have come close since. None has done it in red.
