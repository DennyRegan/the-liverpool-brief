Liverpool came to 16 March 1977 with a European record that was good rather than great. Two UEFA Cups, in 1973 and 1976. One European Cup semi-final, under Bill Shankly in 1965. A serious continental reputation, no trophy from the senior competition — and, that spring, a quarter-final they were losing.

Saint-Étienne had won the first leg 1-0 in France a fortnight earlier. Bob Paisley's side had to overturn that at Anfield against the team beaten in the previous season's European Cup final. Before the night was over, they would need to do considerably more than that.

## The team from the Forez

Under Robert Herbin, Saint-Étienne had been champions of France in 1974, 1975 and 1976, and had reached the 1976 European Cup final at Hampden Park, where Bayern Munich beat them in front of some 20,000 travelling supporters in green — a match French football still discusses in terms of Hampden's square goalposts and the shots that came back off them.

They were less convincing at home by 1976-77, eventually finishing fifth in Division 1 and qualifying for Europe through the Coupe de France instead. The side, though, was largely the one that had gone to Glasgow: Ivan Ćurković in goal, the Argentinian Osvaldo Piazza and Christian Lopez in defence, the captain Jean-Michel Larqué and Dominique Bathenay in midfield, Dominique Rocheteau ahead of them.

Liverpool travelled to the Stade Geoffroy-Guichard on 2 March without Kevin Keegan. He made the trip, but the injury did not clear and he was not named among the substitutes; Terry McDermott played instead, and the tie stayed goalless until Bathenay scored in the 78th minute. Piazza, suspended, would miss the return.

A 1-0 defeat was recoverable. The complication was the away-goals rule. Liverpool had to win by two, and any Saint-Étienne goal at Anfield would push that to three.

## An equation that kept changing

The attendance at Anfield was 55,043. Keegan was back; Tommy Smith came in for the injured Phil Thompson; in Piazza's place Herbin used Alain Merchadier. Charles Corver of the Netherlands refereed.

Liverpool scored inside two minutes, from a short corner. Keegan took the ball near the touchline and struck what the club's own account describes as an apparent cross that Ćurković misjudged and which floated into the net. An earlier club retelling has Heighway playing the corner short and Keegan beating the goalkeeper deliberately from an acute angle. Whichever it was, the first-leg deficit had gone before much of the ground had settled.

That was as far as it went before the interval. Saint-Étienne defended a long way up the pitch and Liverpool kept running into the offside trap. The balance stayed delicate: one more Liverpool goal would take control of the tie; one Saint-Étienne goal would leave Paisley's side needing two.

Six minutes after the restart, Heighway lost possession in midfield, Bathenay collected and beat Ray Clemence with a dipping strike from around thirty yards. The man who had settled the first leg had now scored in both. At 1-1 on the night and 2-1 down on aggregate to a side with an away goal, Liverpool needed two more. A 2-1 win at Anfield was worth nothing.

The reply came quickly. In the 59th minute Callaghan crossed, the ball broke off Toshack to Ray Kennedy on the edge of the area, and Kennedy drove a low shot past Ćurković.

Level at 2-2, Liverpool were still going out. The away goal meant one more was needed, with roughly half an hour to find it.

In the 74th minute Paisley replaced Toshack with David Fairclough. Fairclough was 20, born a mile and a half from the ground, and already carried the label: seven league goals in his breakthrough season of 1975-76, most of them off the bench, had made "Supersub" current well before this night. He has never relished it.

On 80 minutes Saint-Étienne appealed for a penalty when Clemence challenged Rocheteau after Phil Neal slipped, and Corver waved it away.

Then, in the 84th minute, Kennedy played a ball through the middle that split the French defence. Fairclough outran the covering defenders, took it down, shrugged off a challenge from Lopez and slid the ball past Ćurković. Six minutes remained, and for the first time in the tie Liverpool were in front. Fairclough's memory of the seconds afterwards is of Keegan landing on him and shouting above the noise: "Supersub, you've done it again."

Saint-Étienne needed only one goal to turn it round again. It never came.

## What the night was worth

Liverpool beat Zürich 6-1 over two legs in the semi-final and won the European Cup in Rome on 25 May, beating Borussia Mönchengladbach 3-1. The Saint-Étienne tie is usually folded into that story as a staging post, but it holds up on its own. Liverpool went to France without their best player and lost narrowly; wiped out the deficit in two minutes; were pushed towards elimination by a thirty-yard shot; recovered through Kennedy; then spent half an hour needing a goal that arrived from the bench with six minutes left.

Clemence's verdict was that Rome was where they won the trophy, but this was the game that convinced them they could.

For Fairclough the consequences were more double-edged. He finished with 55 goals for Liverpool, from 92 starts and 62 substitute appearances — and nine major honours — but he was not in the side that won the final in Rome. One run and one finish in March 1977 defined him more completely than a decade of work. That is not the same thing as a happy ending, and it is part of why the goal is still talked about.
