Liverpool had won the FA Cup two months earlier. Kevin Keegan was 23, Phil Thompson 20, and the league and UEFA Cup double of 1973 was barely a year old. Nothing about the summer of 1974 suggested an ending.

Friday 12 July was a slow news day on Merseyside. The Daily Post led on local by-elections, a proposed polytechnic at the Albert Dock and strike action disrupting the BBC's coverage of the Open at Royal Lytham. The only football item on the back page was a photograph of Thompson handing out medals to schoolchildren in Kirkby. When Liverpool called the press to Anfield, the assumption was a signing.

There was one. Ray Kennedy arrived from Arsenal as the club's record signing at £200,000, and was given a couple of paragraphs in the following morning's papers. At fourteen minutes past noon, chairman John Smith told the room that Shankly had indicated he wished to retire from active participation in league football, and that the board had accepted his decision with "extreme reluctance".

After almost fifteen years, Bill Shankly was leaving.

The surprise was not that a successful manager had resigned. It was that Shankly had become inseparable from the club he was resigning from. He had arrived in December 1959 with Liverpool in the Second Division and left them with promotion, three championships, two FA Cups and a first European trophy. Supporters jammed the switchboard hoping it was a hoax; Granada sent Tony Wilson onto the streets to record the reaction; that evening's Echo ran the news as a bombshell across its front page.

The obvious question was what Liverpool could possibly do next. The answer shaped the following two decades.

## Why he went

The simplest explanation came from Shankly. He was tired.

In his autobiography he called the decision a terrible wrench, but said he had been around a long time, wanted a rest, more time with his family and a bit more fun out of life, and described football as a hard and relentless task that went on like a river. Nessie Shankly, interviewed at a West Derby hair salon the next day, said she was glad the news was out and that the point of retirement was for the family to see more of him.

Two details are worth holding onto. The first is that Nessie put the decision itself five weeks before the announcement — made, in other words, in early June rather than in the days before the press conference. The second is the arithmetic of his life. Shankly was 60. He had been a player for half of it, had managed since March 1949, and had gone down the mines at Glenbuck before any of that.

There was also form. John Keith, then of the Daily Express, has recalled that Shankly went to the club secretary Peter Robinson at the close of every season to say he was quitting, and that Robinson called it Bill's summer madness. This time it was taken at face value.

What followed is where the story gets harder to pin down. About a fortnight after the announcement, Keith was telephoned by the Guild of Students at Liverpool University, who had invited Shankly to speak. They told him Shankly had stood up and said he knew he had made the wrong decision: he had made it with his head, and his heart could not accept it.

That is second-hand — Keith was not in the room — and it should be read as what he was told rather than what he witnessed. It does not make the original reasons insincere. A man can be worn out by a job and still find that life without it is not what he pictured. Keith's own reading is that Shankly was not calling anyone's bluff, but was tired.

Liverpool, meanwhile, could not wait to find out whether he might change his mind.

## The reluctant successor

Replacing a successful manager is hard. Replacing the man around whom a club's modern identity has been built is a different problem entirely. Liverpool's answer was striking because it was so undramatic.

Bob Paisley had been at Anfield since May 1939, when he arrived from Bishop Auckland as a 20-year-old left-half. He had been player, reserve trainer, self-taught physiotherapist and finally Shankly's assistant, and he understood the methods because he had helped build them. He was also the opposite of his predecessor in temperament, content to leave the public stage to the man who commanded it.

The board had one name on its shortlist, but it took persuasion from both the club and his family before Paisley agreed, and his verdict on the job was that it was like being handed the Queen Elizabeth to steer in a force ten gale.

The appointment was announced at the club's general meeting at the Adelphi Hotel on 26 July, with Paisley formally taking over on 13 August. He was 55 and had never managed a senior first team. The logic looks obvious now only because of what followed; in the summer of 1974 Liverpool were promoting the quiet assistant rather than hiring a name.

## One last walk at Wembley

Liverpool's next fixture was the Charity Shield against Leeds at Wembley on 10 August, the first time the match had been staged there.

It caught English football mid-upheaval. Don Revie had been appointed England manager on 4 July, eight days before Shankly's announcement, and Brian Clough had taken over at Elland Road on 30 July. Paisley picked the Liverpool side, keeping ten of the FA Cup final eleven and bringing in Phil Boersma for the injured John Toshack; Shankly was invited to lead the team out one last time, a gesture Clough said Revie declined for Leeds.

Boersma scored after twenty minutes, following up a Keegan shot parried by David Harvey. Keegan and the Leeds captain Billy Bremner were sent off together on the hour after trading punches, and both threw their shirts away as they left. Trevor Cherry headed the equaliser on 70 minutes. The shoot-out that settled it went to sudden death at 5-5, Harvey put his kick over the bar, and Ian Callaghan scored the winner despite slipping as he struck it.

There was a cost. Keegan and Bremner were charged with bringing the game into disrepute, fined £500 each and banned until the end of September — eleven matches apiece. Paisley therefore began his first season without his best player, a detail usually lost behind the fight.

His first league match came at Luton on 17 August, a 2-1 win. Shankly spent that Saturday afternoon at Goodison Park, watching Everton play Derby County.

## What continuity actually meant

There was no purge. Joe Fagan moved up as assistant. Ronnie Moran and the rest of the coaching structure stayed. Smith set the reasoning out in a matchday programme early in the new season: the theme had to be continuity and stability, and there was no sense in changing something tried and proved over years.

This was more than conservatism. Shankly had never worked alone, however singular he looked from outside. Paisley, Fagan, Reuben Bennett and Moran had done the daily work that sustained the club, and the Boot Room was not folklore invented once the trophies arrived — it was an accumulation of knowledge that could outlast any one man. That may be Shankly's largest legacy, and it is the least quotable.

The smoothness can still be overstated. Paisley inherited an outstanding team and Shankly's shadow with it. Players who had spent years calling Shankly "boss" now had to call his successor Bob. Shankly himself did not disappear: he returned to Melwood to train with the players, and the club eventually barred him from the training ground, because men who had played for him kept calling him boss and could not easily treat him as a visitor. He remained devoted to Liverpool and was hurt that no continuing role was found for him. The club, at the same time, owed its new manager the room to manage. Both obligations were legitimate and they did not fit together.

Nor did success arrive on schedule. Paisley's first season brought nothing: out of the Cup Winners' Cup to Ferencváros on away goals, out of the FA Cup to a late Ipswich goal at Portman Road, and a title challenge ended by defeat at Middlesbrough on the penultimate weekend. Liverpool finished second, two points behind Derby County under the two-points-for-a-win system then in use, an outcome Paisley took as a personal failure.

The clues were in the recruitment. Phil Neal arrived from Northampton in October 1974 and Terry McDermott from Newcastle a month later. Ray Kennedy — Shankly's last signing, bought as a powerful striker — was remade by Paisley as a left-sided midfielder two years after he arrived, and went on to win five league titles and three European Cups in the position. The new manager was not dismantling Shankly's Liverpool. He was learning to evolve it.

The breakthrough came in his second season: the title settled by a win at Wolverhampton on the final day, and the UEFA Cup won 4-3 on aggregate against Bruges. A year later Liverpool retained the championship and, four days after losing the FA Cup final to Manchester United, beat Borussia Mönchengladbach 3-1 in Rome for the European Cup that had eluded Shankly. By his retirement in 1983 Paisley had won, in nine seasons, six league championships, three European Cups, three League Cups, a UEFA Cup, a European Super Cup and six Charity Shields.

That record was his own, and reducing it to an inheritance does him a disservice. But he did inherit something unusual: a club that knew what it was trying to do. When he retired, Fagan followed him. When Fagan retired in 1985, Kenny Dalglish followed as player-manager, with Paisley on the board and advising him. From Shankly's arrival in 1959 to Dalglish's resignation in February 1991, Liverpool had four permanent managers.

Shankly's achievement is normally measured by what he won. There is another measure, and it is the one 12 July 1974 supplies. When the man who had rebuilt the club walked out of it, Liverpool did not collapse and did not go looking for another messiah. They promoted from within and let the next man develop the team rather than rebuild the institution. That the most successful period in the club's history began the summer he left is not a footnote to his career. It is part of the case for it.
