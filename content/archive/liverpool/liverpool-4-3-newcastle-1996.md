By the time Newcastle United arrived at Anfield on the evening of Wednesday 3 April 1996, a title race they had once appeared to own had turned into something far less comfortable.

Kevin Keegan's side had led Manchester United by twelve points in January. By late March that lead was gone: defeat at Arsenal on 23 March was their third in five league games, and Eric Cantona's winner against Tottenham the following day put Manchester United three points clear at the top. Newcastle still had two matches in hand, and Anfield was the first of them.

Liverpool arrived in a stranger condition. They had lost 1-0 at Nottingham Forest on the same night Newcastle lost at Highbury — their first defeat in twenty-one matches in all competitions — then beaten Aston Villa 3-0 to reach the FA Cup final, which is why this fixture had been moved from the previous Saturday to a Wednesday for Sky. Third in the table, eight points off the top with a game in hand, they were not out of the argument. Victory would cut the gap to five with the same number played.

A crowd of 40,702 saw seven goals, three changes of lead, a Liverpool equaliser each time Newcastle got away, and a winner in the second minute of stoppage time. Thirty years on, when the Premier League opened its full match archive in March 2026 and picked ten of the competition's greatest games, this was among them.

## Two teams built to attack

Roy Evans set Liverpool up in the 3-4-1-2 he had been trialling all season. David James in goal; John Scales, Mark Wright and Neil Ruddock across the back; Jason McAteer and Rob Jones wide of Jamie Redknapp and John Barnes; Steve McManaman in the space behind Stan Collymore and Robbie Fowler.

Keegan made one change from the Arsenal defeat, Steve Watson for Warren Barton. Pavel Srníček was behind Watson, Steve Howey, Philippe Albert and John Beresford, with Peter Beardsley, David Batty, Rob Lee and David Ginola in midfield and Faustino Asprilla alongside Les Ferdinand.

The attacking numbers were not hidden. Fowler would finish the league season with 28 goals, Collymore with 14. Newcastle had scored their way to the top of the table and stayed there for most of the year on the same principle.

Liverpool needed less than two minutes to make the point. Redknapp found Collymore on the left, Collymore crossed, and Fowler headed in at the far post.

The reply took eight minutes. Asprilla went past Ruddock inside the box and pulled the ball back for Ferdinand, who made room with his first touch and beat James with his second. Four minutes after that Ferdinand turned provider, releasing Ginola on the left, and Newcastle led.

Fourteen minutes, three goals, two changes of lead. The rest of the half somehow produced none, though not for want of trying: Fowler shot narrowly wide, Redknapp went close from range, McManaman mistimed a header, and at the other end Asprilla and Beardsley broke clear without finishing. Evans replaced Wright with Steve Harkness at the interval.

## Level, and level again

Nine minutes into the second half McAteer fed McManaman, who ran at the Newcastle box and picked out Fowler for his second. Liverpool were level for barely two minutes. Lee put Asprilla through and the Colombian finished with the outside of his right foot.

The pattern was becoming almost formal. Twice Liverpool had scored; twice Newcastle had answered within minutes. At 3-2 the match had swung back to Keegan, and for a spell Newcastle held the territory — Ginola's pass to an arriving Ferdinand was the chance that would have settled it.

Instead, in the 67th minute, McAteer's curling cross confused the Newcastle defence, Collymore met it, and Liverpool were level for the second time. Sources vary on the minute — contemporary and later records give 65, 67 and 68 — but not on the cross, the finish or what it did to the game.

Both managers went to the bench late on: Ian Rush on for Rob Jones, Darren Peacock on for Steve Howey to shore up the Newcastle defence. A draw would have suited neither.

With stoppage time under way, Rush and Barnes worked a series of one-twos on the edge of the Newcastle area before Barnes released Collymore on the left. Collymore drove it past Srníček; the Kop erupted; Keegan slumped over the advertising hoardings. The image has outlasted almost everything else about the season.

Afterwards Keegan called it a classic, said there was a long way to go, and declined to apologise for the way his team played. Evans, who had just taken the three points, called it kamikaze defending and observed that no side wins a championship playing like that; he admitted Liverpool had got away with defending man for man at the back.

Both readings were fair. Newcastle scored three times at Anfield, led twice, and left with nothing. Liverpool scored four against a title challenger and needed every one of them.

## What it actually changed

It is tempting, with the final table to hand, to make this the night Newcastle lost the league. The chronology does not quite allow it.

Before kick-off Newcastle were three points behind Manchester United with two games in hand. Afterwards they were three points behind with one. Liverpool moved to 62 points from 32 games, five behind United having played the same number. Both clubs were still in it; both had less room than before.

Newcastle beat Queens Park Rangers three days later, then lost at Blackburn, conceding twice in the last ten minutes, which left Manchester United six points clear with five to play. Liverpool lost 1-0 at Coventry immediately and took nine points from their final six matches — enough for third, their best finish since the Premier League began, and a season that ended with Cantona scoring against them in the FA Cup final. Manchester United lost only one more league game all season and finished four points above Newcastle.

Newcastle were not finished at Anfield, and the proof came four weeks later. On 29 April a Keith Gillespie goal won them a third straight match, at Leeds, and put them back within three points with a game in hand and two to play. It also produced the most famous outburst in the competition's history. Goaded by Alex Ferguson's suggestion that Leeds and Nottingham Forest might not try as hard against Newcastle as they had against Manchester United, Keegan turned on him live on Sky: "I will love it if we beat them. Love it." Newcastle drew their last two matches and won nothing.

So: not a decider in the strict sense. What Anfield removed was margin. One of the two games in hand had gone without producing a point, at the exact stage when the leaders had stopped dropping any.

Not everyone involved reads it that way. Ginola, asked about the match in 2003 when it was voted the Premier League's Match of the Decade, thought that had Newcastle held it at 3-2 they would have won the league — he has also called the night the turning point, the moment a side that had thought of itself as entertainers realised it could lose. That is a participant's judgement rather than an established fact, but it is a serious one, and it deserves setting alongside the arithmetic rather than being tidied away by it.

## Evans's Liverpool in ninety minutes

For Liverpool the game has a second value in retrospect, because few matches show Evans's side so completely.

Fowler scored twice, as he did all season. Collymore, the £8.5m club-record signing of the previous summer, made the first goal and scored the last two. McManaman, named man of the match, spent the evening carrying the ball at a defence that could not get near him. Barnes, no longer the winger of his first Liverpool years, supplied the final pass. Rush, close to the end of his Anfield career, came on for a defender because Liverpool were chasing the game.

And the other half of the portrait sat in Evans's own verdict. This was a team good enough to score four against the side lying second and not solid enough to make that count over a season. The 4-3 did not prove the point on its own, but it illustrated it about as clearly as ninety minutes can.

There was even a sequel. When Newcastle came back to Anfield on 11 March 1997, Liverpool were 3-0 up at half-time, Newcastle scored three in seventeen minutes, and Fowler won it in stoppage time. 4-3 again. The repetition fixed the association between these two clubs and a particular kind of chaos, but the first match remained the reference point.

Its reputation has long since detached from the title race that produced it. Plenty of games have yielded seven goals. This one joined quality to consequence: Fowler, Collymore, McManaman, Ferdinand, Ginola and Asprilla, in a match between two teams still looking upwards, where every time one side appeared to take control the game refused to let it.

Collymore's last shot won Liverpool nothing. It did not, by itself, cost Newcastle anything either. What it did was settle an extraordinary contest at the last available moment, take one more turn through an unstable title race, and leave the clearest single-match picture we have of Liverpool under Roy Evans: gifted, ambitious, exposed, and capable of football that has outlasted the table it was played for.
