Liverpool were back in Europe for the first time since Heysel, and on the evening of Wednesday 6 November 1991 they needed three goals at Anfield to stay there. Auxerre had beaten them 2-0 in France a fortnight earlier. Jan Mølby settled the first part of it from the penalty spot in the fourth minute, Mike Marsh headed a second before the half-hour, and after Frédéric Darras was sent off Mark Walters finished the tie with seven minutes left. Liverpool won 3-0 and went through 3-2. It was the best night of Graeme Souness's short managerial reign, and it is remembered now for something that supposedly happened afterwards, in a corridor rather than on the pitch.

Souness's account is that Michel Platini, then the manager of France, knocked on his office door once the ground had emptied and said he had a player for him — "a proper player", a man who had been trouble at home but who would suit Liverpool. The player was Eric Cantona. Souness, by his own telling, said he was fighting too many fires already and did not need another one. He has added, consistently, that he should have been more open-minded.

That conversation is the whole of the Liverpool story, and it rests entirely on one witness. Souness has repeated it for more than two decades — in newspaper columns, on television, and at length in the Souness chapter of Simon Hughes's *Men in White Suits* — and the core has held firm each time: Platini came to the office, Cantona was offered as a recommendation, Souness said no. The detail around it has moved, as detail does. In one version Liverpool did not need any more "problems"; in another Souness was fighting fires and did not need more trouble. He has remembered Mølby's penalty arriving after about two minutes; the record puts it in the fourth. None of that discredits the story. It is what a memory of an ordinary five minutes looks like after twenty-five years, and it is a useful warning against treating the retellings as separate confirmations. They are one man, told several times.

What has never surfaced is anything else. No contemporary report of that week mentions Cantona at Anfield. No account from Platini, from Cantona, or from anyone else inside Liverpool places the two men in that office. And in none of Souness's own versions does Liverpool do anything at all: there is no approach to Nîmes, no contact with an agent, no fee discussed, no terms explored, no trial arranged, no matter put to the board. Platini was making a suggestion, not conveying an offer. Souness declined a suggestion.

The chronology matters here, because the story is usually told backwards. In November 1991 Cantona was not a discarded footballer casting about for a way back. He had joined newly promoted Nîmes that June for a reported ten million francs, and he was still Platini's first-choice forward. Two weeks after the Auxerre tie, on 20 November, he started for France against Iceland at the Parc des Princes and scored twice in a 3-1 win. His unravelling came later: on 7 December he threw the ball at the referee Jean-Pierre Blouet during a 1-1 draw with Saint-Étienne, and on 12 December, after the disciplinary commission extended his suspension to two months, he announced that he was finished with the game. Whatever Platini said at Anfield, he said it about a serving France international, not a man in exile.

Platini has confirmed the broader effort, without ever mentioning Liverpool. Asked in 2008 whether he was responsible for Cantona's move to English football, he said that Cantona had been thinking of quitting and that he and Gérard Houllier gave him the idea of going to England, because they thought the country suited him. That supports Platini's role as the architect of the English move. It does not corroborate the Anfield conversation, and it places the initiative after the retirement rather than before it.

It is worth setting the Liverpool episode beside what an actual attempt to sign Cantona looked like, because the contrast is the point. In late January 1992 he travelled to Sheffield to train with Sheffield Wednesday and played in a six-a-side match at the newly opened Sheffield Arena. He remembers going there to sign: "I thought I was there to sign", he has said, with a lawyer present and a week's work behind him, only to be asked to stay on for a second week. Trevor Francis has always insisted the premise was different — that a former agent asked him to do Platini a favour, that Wednesday needed neither another centre-forward nor the outlay, and that a request for a few more days' training was taken by Cantona's camp as an insult. The two men agree on almost nothing except that Cantona trained, that the extension ended it, and that he left for Elland Road with Francis's blessing.

At Leeds the machinery turned properly. Howard Wilkinson, who had watched Cantona with the France Under-21s, telephoned Houllier and Platini when he heard the player wanted an English club; both endorsed him and both warned that he was a "loose cannon". Wilkinson met him and took him on loan in the first days of February. He came on as a substitute in a 2-0 defeat at Oldham on 8 February. Bill Fotherby, Leeds's managing director, later described meeting the player's advisers and committing to money the club did not have. The precise figures are disputed. A contemporary Yorkshire report has an initial £100,000 loan with £900,000 to buy him outright, a later reconstruction puts the option at £1m, Fotherby himself remembered roughly £500,000 falling due to Cantona if Leeds kept him beyond an initial spell, and a French report of the following winter simply had Leeds buying him from Nîmes for nine million francs in February. What the evidence establishes securely is a loan followed by a permanent move, not one uncontested set of numbers. But the shape is not in doubt: assessment, contact with the player, advisers in a room, a figure, a signature. Liverpool have none of those things in their column, and never did.

One official source says otherwise. UEFA's 2019 profile of Cantona, published when he received the President's Award, states that he settled on Leeds after trials at Liverpool and Sheffield Wednesday. There is no date attached to the Liverpool half of that sentence, no photograph, no training record and no participant who says it happened — and it sits against Souness's own account, which describes a conversation in an office, not a player reporting for assessment. The same passage is unreliable elsewhere: it places Cantona's arrival at Leeds in January when he signed on 4 February, and it has him submitting a transfer request in November 1992 with United signing him two days later, which is not how Fotherby described the Denis Irwin call that produced the deal. Until something surfaces to support it, the Liverpool trial should not be repeated.
