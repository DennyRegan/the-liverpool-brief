The defence of the title began with a decision not to buy. In late July 2020 Dejan Lovren left for Zenit St Petersburg for a fee reported at around £10.9m, and Adam Lallana, out of contract, joined Brighton. Lovren had been the fourth of Liverpool's senior centre-backs, behind Virgil van Dijk, Joe Gomez and Joël Matip, and he was not replaced. Asked about it before the season, Klopp said the club had a lot of centre-backs, and that Fabinho could play there as well. He was ready, he added, for a creative solution in the right moments, but hoped those moments would never come.

The money went elsewhere. Kostas Tsimikas arrived from Olympiakos on 10 August for £11.75m, according to English reports, to cover Andy Robertson at left-back, after a £10m bid for Norwich City's Jamal Lewis had been turned down. On 18 September Liverpool completed the signing of Thiago Alcântara from Bayern Munich, the Champions League winner, for a reported initial fee of £20m. Klopp used the occasion to explain how the club paid its way. "The only thing which is always true with us is what we earned on football, we spend on football," he said. A day later Liverpool completed the signing of Diogo Jota from Wolves for £41m, rising to £45m, with the young defender Ki-Jana Hoever going the other way for around £9m. Rhian Brewster, the academy striker, was sold to Sheffield United for up to £23.5m, with a buy-back clause and a sell-on share.

It was a window shaped by the pandemic. Football had come back in June behind closed doors, and the 2020–21 season would start the same way. When the club's accounts for 2019–20 were eventually published, the following April, they showed a pre-tax loss of £46m. In the season that followed, matchday income would almost disappear.

The first signs were mixed. Liverpool lost the Community Shield to Arsenal on penalties at Wembley on 29 August, Brewster striking the bar with his kick, and went out of the League Cup to Arsenal on penalties a month later. In the league they beat newly promoted Leeds 4–3 on the opening day, Mohamed Salah scoring a hat-trick, won 2–0 at Chelsea, where Thiago came on for his debut, and beat Arsenal 3–1. On 4 October, at Villa Park, they lost 7–2. Ten days later the Premier League's clubs unanimously rejected Project Big Picture, a plan to reshape English football that Liverpool and Manchester United had been closely involved in drawing up with Rick Parry of the EFL. Neither event, at the time, looked like the beginning of anything.

* * *

The match that is usually taken as the start of the decline came at Goodison Park on 17 October. Early in the game Jordan Pickford came off his line and caught Van Dijk with a high, late challenge. The video assistant ruled Van Dijk offside in the build-up, and because the officials had seen the incident, the rules allowed no retrospective action. Pickford was not punished. Michael Oliver, the referee, was later reported as saying that the decision was wrong and that Pickford should have been. Van Dijk had damaged the anterior cruciate ligament in his knee, and was operated on in London on 30 October. He would not play again that season. Thiago suffered a knee injury in the same game, which finished 2–2 after Jordan Henderson's late winner was disallowed by VAR.

Liverpool did not fall apart immediately. They went top of the table for the first time on 31 October, with a 2–1 win over West Ham, and three days later won 5–0 at Atalanta in the Champions League, Jota scoring a hat-trick. Rhys Williams, a young centre-back from the academy, made his first Champions League start that night. Then, on 11 November, Gomez damaged a tendon in his left knee in England training. He had surgery the next day. In a little under four weeks Liverpool had lost two of their three senior centre-backs for the season.

What followed, until the end of the year, was a team adapting under strain. Fabinho moved back from midfield to play alongside Matip, or alongside Williams and Nat Phillips when Matip was unavailable. Liverpool beat Leicester 3–0 at Anfield on 22 November, setting a club record for consecutive unbeaten home league games. They lost 2–0 at home to Atalanta three days later but still won their Champions League group. On 6 December around 2,000 supporters were allowed into Anfield for the first time in roughly ten months and saw Wolves beaten 4–0. Klopp called it "a proper goosebump moment".

Three days later Jota injured his knee in Denmark, against Midtjylland. He had scored nine goals in 17 appearances. Klopp expected him to be out for "one-and-a-half, two months"; it turned out to be closer to three. The following week the Premier League's clubs voted, for a third time, against allowing five substitutions, as other major leagues did. Klopp was openly critical. England, he said, was "pretty much the only league" doing it differently, and with the fixture list as compressed as it was, managing the players' load was "the most tricky thing to do as a manager".

The results did not yet show it. On 16 December Roberto Firmino's goal in the 90th minute beat Tottenham, and three days later Liverpool won 7–0 at Crystal Palace. At Christmas they were top with 31 points from 14 games, four clear of Leicester. They had scored 36 league goals. They had done this without Van Dijk for most of it, and without Gomez for half of it.

* * *

The turn came quietly. West Bromwich Albion equalised late at Anfield on 27 December. Newcastle held Liverpool to 0–0 three days later, on the day it was announced that the Liverpool city region would enter Tier 3 of the government's coronavirus restrictions, which meant that the supporters who had briefly come back would be locked out again. On 4 January Liverpool lost 1–0 at Southampton. They were still top, on goal difference, but they had begun to stop scoring. A goalless draw at home to Manchester United followed.

On 21 January, at Anfield, Burnley won 1–0 with an 83rd-minute penalty from Ashley Barnes, awarded after a foul by Alisson. Liverpool had not lost a home league match since April 2017, a run of 68 games. By the end of the night they had gone more than seven hours without a league goal. Klopp did not look for excuses. "That's our, and that means my, fault," he said. It was not the performance, he argued, so much as the decisive moments: "the decisive 10 per cent in the moment is missing." Three days later Manchester United knocked them out of the FA Cup, and on 26 January Manchester City went top of the table. They stayed there for the rest of the season.

The blow that changed the shape of the season came at Tottenham on 28 January. Liverpool won 3–1, but Matip damaged his ankle ligaments and was ruled out for the season. All three of the senior centre-backs Liverpool had kept for 2020–21 were now gone. On transfer deadline day, 1 February, the club signed Ozan Kabak on loan from Schalke, for a fee of £1m rising to £1.5m with an option to buy, and Ben Davies from Preston for around £1.6m in total. A move for the Marseille defender Duje Ćaleta-Car had fallen through. Klopp called Kabak a big talent; on Sky, Paul Merson called it "just bringing a couple of bodies in".

What came next was the worst run of results in Klopp's time at the club. Brighton won 1–0 at Anfield on 3 February. Four days later City came and won 4–1. Two of their goals came from errors by Alisson with the ball at his feet. Klopp said his team-mates had not given him enough options for the first, and that for the second he had simply mis-hit the ball. He defended him all the same. "Ali has saved our life plenty of times, no doubt about that," he said, "and tonight he made two mistakes and that's how it is." He also said what the target now was. Top four was "of course our main target, that is clear."

Two days after the City game, Klopp's mother, Elisabeth, was buried in Germany. Travel restrictions meant he could not be there. "She meant everything to me," he told his home-town newspaper, the Schwarzwälder Bote. "She was a proper mum, in every sense of the word."

On 13 February Liverpool took the lead at Leicester and lost 3–1. Asked whether the title had gone, Klopp said: "Yes. I don't think we can close the gap." A week later, on 20 February, Everton won 2–0 at Anfield, their first league win there since 1999. Henderson, who had been playing at centre-back, came off injured and needed surgery on his adductor; he was ruled out until at least April. When Phillips replaced him alongside Kabak, it made, on the Associated Press's count, Liverpool's 18th different centre-back pairing of the season. Fabinho, the other midfielder who had been filling in, was out with a muscle injury. "It is a massive blow losing Hendo again," Klopp said. "Massive." Four days after the derby, Alisson's father, José Agostinho Becker, drowned in a lake on the family's property in southern Brazil.

The run continued into March. Chelsea won 1–0 at Anfield on 4 March. Reuters reported that it was the first time in the club's history that Liverpool had lost five consecutive home league games. On the same day Sky Sports published a long interview in which Klopp gave his own explanation: "Most of the problems are as a result of the injury situation." He had never, he said, had to "change the last line every week". Three days later Fulham won 1–0 at Anfield too. It was a sixth straight home league defeat, a Premier League record for reigning champions. On Opta's count, Liverpool had now taken 115 shots at Anfield since their last open-play home league goal, against West Brom on 27 December; the only home league goal they had scored since was Salah's penalty against City. By the end of the day they were eighth, on 43 points from 28 games. "It would be a masterpiece," Klopp said, "to find out how we change that overnight."

From Burnley's visit on 21 January to Fulham's on 7 March, Liverpool played ten league games, won three and lost seven. In the first 14 games of the season they had scored 36 goals. In the next 14 they scored 11.

* * *

The explanation Klopp offered in March was the one most people reached for first, and the injuries were real. Liverpool lost Van Dijk in October, Gomez in November and Matip in January, all for the season, and then the two midfielders who had been asked to replace them. In 2019–20 Van Dijk had played every minute of the league season; in 2020–21, on Sky Sports' count, Liverpool used 20 different centre-back partnerships across all competitions. FiveThirtyEight's later analysis found that the players who stood in, whatever their other qualities, were far less able than the three senior defenders to carry the ball forward or pass through the lines, which had been part of how Liverpool built their attacks.

But injuries do not explain everything, and the timing is the problem. Liverpool were top of the league at Christmas without Van Dijk or Gomez. The decline began straight after that, in late December, while Matip was still available: in the five league games from West Brom to Burnley Liverpool did not win, scored once, and lost to Southampton and Burnley. What failed first was not the defence but the attack. Losing Matip, and then Henderson and Fabinho, turned that slump into a collapse.

Richard Jolly, writing for Sporting Life on 3 March, argued that the decline had begun long before Van Dijk was hurt. Liverpool had won 35 of 36 league games up to their defeat at Watford in February 2020. Over the next 34, they had won only half. Jolly's figures showed fewer tackles in the final third, a fall in set-piece goals from 17 to six, and sharp drops in Trent Alexander-Arnold's assists and Sadio Mané's goals per 90 minutes. Salah was the exception: his scoring rate had risen. Gareth Southgate's explanation when he left Alexander-Arnold out of the England squad on 18 March was, in its way, the same point. He had not, Southgate said, "played at the level that he found in the last couple of years".

The structural case was put most clearly by the Guardian in mid-March. Liverpool's slump, it argued, was "a matter of structure and planning, of cause and effect". The club had begun the season with three experienced centre-backs, two of them prone to injury, when most rivals at that level had four and City had six. Liverpool had spent £12m net over the previous two seasons. The system, the paper said, needed rest and reinforcement to sustain itself, and neither had come. Klopp, unsurprisingly, saw it differently. After the Everton defeat he rejected any connection with what had gone before. "I don't see any relation to the last three years," he said.

There was one more factor that affected every club but none more obviously than Liverpool. 2020–21 was the first Premier League season in which away teams won more games than home teams, 153 to 143. Liverpool's home record had been the foundation of their title: in 2019–20 they had won 18 and drawn one of their 19 league games at Anfield. In 2020–21 they won ten and lost six, and all six of those defeats came in an empty stadium. Away from home, they lost three games all season.

The idea that the team had simply grown old is harder to support from the season's own numbers. Salah scored 22 league goals, and his scoring rate went up rather than down. What the numbers do show falling away is the supply, the set pieces and the defensive platform the side had been built on. Whether the players had also declined physically, the evidence gathered here cannot say.

So was it injuries, age or something structural? The fairest answer the evidence allows is that a team which had been losing its edge since the previous February stopped scoring at the turn of the year, and that the injuries, falling on a squad too thin to absorb them, turned a bad run into the worst of Klopp's time at the club. None of those explanations alone accounts for a champion side going from first at Christmas to eighth in March.

* * *

The recovery was as unexpected as the collapse. On 10 March, in the second leg of their last-16 tie with RB Leipzig, played like the first in Budapest because of travel restrictions, Liverpool won 2–0 again. Five days later Jota, back from injury, scored the only goal at Wolves. It was the first of ten league games in which Liverpool did not lose.

The changes were not grand ones. Phillips and Williams, who had begun the season far down the order and had been used only when there was no one else, became the regular centre-back partnership. Both were strong in the air; on FiveThirtyEight's later figures, Williams won almost four in every five of his aerial duels. Klopp later said that Liverpool "wouldn't be there without them".

Europe ended in April. Real Madrid won the first leg of the quarter-final 3–1, Vinícius Júnior scoring twice, and the second leg at Anfield finished 0–0. The day after the first leg, the club condemned the racist abuse sent on Instagram to Alexander-Arnold, Naby Keïta and Mané as "abhorrent". On 10 April Alexander-Arnold scored in stoppage time to beat Aston Villa 2–1 and end the run of home defeats.

Then, on the night of 18 April, Liverpool were named as one of the founder members of a European Super League. The reaction in England was immediate. The next evening, at Elland Road, Leeds players warmed up in shirts bearing the words "Earn it". Klopp, who had spoken against a Super League before, did not change his view. "My opinion didn't change," he told Sky Sports, and he set the Super League alongside UEFA's expanding competitions and a bigger Club World Cup as part of the same push for more games and more money. Spirit of Shankly, the supporters' union, called on the club's owners, Fenway Sports Group, to "consider their positions with immediate effect".

On the evening of 20 April Henderson and seven of his team-mates posted the same statement on social media. "We don't like it and we don't want it to happen," it said. "This is our collective position." Henderson was also reported to have called a meeting of the Premier League's captains. Within a couple of hours Liverpool had withdrawn. The next morning John W. Henry, the principal owner, apologised in a video addressed to supporters, players and staff. "I'm sorry," he said, "and I alone am responsible for the unnecessary negativity brought forward over the past couple of days." Of the supporters he said: "We heard you. I heard you." Whatever the club had hoped to gain, it had damaged a relationship that Klopp had spent five years rebuilding.

On the pitch, draws with Leeds and Newcastle left Liverpool with ground to make up. They beat Southampton 2–0 and, on 13 May, won 4–2 at Old Trafford in a match rearranged after supporters' protests against United's owners had forced the original fixture to be postponed. Three days later, at The Hawthorns, they were drawing 1–1 with West Brom into the fifth minute of stoppage time when Alisson, who had come up from his goal for a corner, headed it in. It was the first competitive goal ever scored by a Liverpool goalkeeper. Afterwards, he spoke about his father. "I hope my father was there to see it with God on his side celebrating," he said.

A 3–0 win at Burnley on 19 May, in which Phillips scored, took Liverpool into the top four for the first time since February. On the final day, 23 May, around 10,000 supporters were allowed into Anfield to see them play Crystal Palace. Mané scored twice in a 2–0 win, Chelsea lost at Aston Villa, and Liverpool finished third. It was Georginio Wijnaldum's last game for the club. He left that summer as a free agent, having made 237 appearances.

Klopp was not inclined to pretend it had been a good season. The day before the Palace game he had said that, with the injuries Liverpool had suffered, "it was not the year to become champions. No chance. For nobody." Afterwards he was simply glad. "From nowhere to the Champions League in five weeks is a massive achievement," he said. And of the crowd: "To have the opportunity to do this, to have this last game in front of 10,000 people, means really the world to us."

Liverpool finished with 69 points, 30 fewer than the year before and 17 behind City. They had won 20 games, drawn nine and lost nine. From 15 March they took 26 points out of 30. The squad that ended the season was not the one that had started it: it had been rebuilt in emergency, and held together largely by players nobody had expected to need.

Five days after the final match, Liverpool announced an agreement to sign Ibrahima Konaté, a centre-back, from RB Leipzig, for a reported £36m.
