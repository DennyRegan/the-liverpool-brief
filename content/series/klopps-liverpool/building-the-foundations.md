The first thing Liverpool did in the summer of 2016 was spend a great deal of money on one player. On 28 June they completed the signing of Sadio Mané from Southampton. The club did not disclose the fee. Sky Sports News understood it to be £34m up front with a further £2m in performance add-ons, which would have made him the most expensive player in Liverpool's history, ahead of Andy Carroll; Sky also reported that Liverpool were claiming the fee was closer to £30m. Mané called it a big day and said he was very happy to be joining one of the biggest clubs in Europe. Klopp offered his own explanation later in the year. He had passed on Mané when he was in charge of Borussia Dortmund, he told reporters in November, and within a few months had regretted it so much that he resolved to take the next chance that came.

The second major arrival was Georginio Wijnaldum, who joined from Newcastle United for a reported £25m on a five-year contract. Newcastle had just been relegated, but Wijnaldum had scored 11 goals in 38 league games in his first season in England. Around those two sat a set of cheaper signings from the Bundesliga. Joël Matip had agreed in February to join on a free transfer when his contract at Schalke expired, and was registered on 1 July. Loris Karius, a goalkeeper, came from Mainz for about £4.7m. Ragnar Klavan, an Estonian centre-back, arrived from Augsburg for about £4.2m. Marko Grujić, signed from Red Star Belgrade in January and loaned straight back, joined the squad as well.

What left was more revealing. Kolo Touré, José Enrique, Jordan Rossiter, João Carlos Teixeira and Jerome Sinclair all left. Then came the sales. Jordon Ibe went to Bournemouth for £15m, Joe Allen to Stoke for £13m and Martin Škrtel to Fenerbahçe for a reported £5m to £5.5m. Brad Smith, Sergi Canós and Luis Alberto were sold too. Christian Benteke joined Crystal Palace on 20 August for a fee reported at between £27m and £32m. Mario Balotelli left for Nice on a free on deadline day, and Lazar Marković, Jon Flanagan and Andre Wisdom were among those sent out on season-long loans.

The arithmetic is untidy, because several fees were never confirmed, but every method points in the same direction. This Is Anfield calculated that Liverpool had made a profit of about £17.5m on the window once add-ons and compensation were included. Sky Sports' estimates had Liverpool's £84.4m in sales as the highest in the Premier League and their net spend at minus £14.5m. On the same estimates Manchester City, newly under Pep Guardiola, had spent a net £169m, Manchester United £141m, Chelsea around £98m and Arsenal £88m. While their direct rivals spent heavily, Liverpool came out of the summer having taken in more than they paid out.

\* \* \*

The club also made a commitment of a different kind. On 8 July Klopp signed a new six-year contract, running to 2022, in place of a deal that had been due to expire in 2018. His assistants were tied down as well. Klopp said he and his staff felt honoured and that it was fantastic the club's owners were as committed to the project as they were.

He was clear, too, about what kind of group he wanted. On 8 July UEFA's disciplinary body dismissed the doping case against Mamadou Sakho, who had tested positive in March for what was described as a fat burner, had served an interim suspension and had missed France's European Championship squad as a result. Later that month he was on his way home from Liverpool's tour of the United States. Klopp told reporters that Sakho had missed the flight, missed a training session and then been late for a meal. He had to build a group and start again, so it made sense for the defender to fly back to Liverpool. Asked about the club's transfer committee in the same week, Klopp defended it on the grounds that no single person should have all the power. RTÉ reported that the committee had been revamped and that the manager had the final say.

There was one other fact that shaped the season before it began. Liverpool had finished eighth and lost the Europa League final, so there would be no European football in 2016–17. Klopp chose to see an opportunity in it. There would be about 20 fewer games, he was reported as telling the Liverpool Echo in late July, but the possibility of 50 more training sessions, and they had to use it.

Pre-season brought a setback and an encouragement. Karius broke his hand in a collision with Dejan Lovren during a 1–0 defeat to Chelsea in Pasadena, and was expected to be out for eight to ten weeks. Ten days later Liverpool beat Barcelona 4–0 at Wembley, Mané scoring after a quarter of an hour, Javier Mascherano turning the ball into his own net, Divock Origi adding a third and Grujić heading a fourth in stoppage time.

\* \* \*

The season opened at the Emirates Stadium on 14 August, and in its first hour Liverpool showed both sides of what was to come. Simon Mignolet saved a penalty from Theo Walcott, only for Walcott to score moments later. Philippe Coutinho equalised with a curling free-kick from about 30 yards, and early in the second half Adam Lallana and Coutinho again put Liverpool ahead before Mané scored a solo goal on his debut. At 4–1, the game looked won. Arsenal scored twice more and Liverpool held on at 4–3.

A week later they lost 2–0 at Burnley, despite having most of the ball, and drew 1–1 at Tottenham. The early tracking data told its own story. After the opening weekend Sky Sports' Adam Bate noted that Liverpool had run four kilometres more than any other side and made about 15 per cent more sprints. By late October they led the league on both measures, with Henderson covering more ground than any of his team-mates and Roberto Firmino making the most sprints, closely followed by Mané and Nathaniel Clyne.

On 9 September the club unveiled the rebuilt Main Stand, with Klopp and the principal owner John W. Henry present. The project, reported at £114m to £115m, added about 8,500 seats and took Anfield's capacity to roughly 54,000. The next day Liverpool beat the champions, Leicester City, 4–1. It was, ESPN noted, Anfield's first crowd of more than 50,000 since the title was clinched against Aston Villa in 1980. Firmino scored twice, Mané and Lallana once each, and Klopp joked afterwards that they should open a new stand every week.

The first big away win came six days later. At Stamford Bridge, on a Friday night, Lovren and Henderson scored in the first half and Liverpool won 2–1. Hull were beaten 5–1, Swansea 2–1 after Liverpool had trailed, and October brought a goalless draw at home to Manchester United, wins over West Bromwich Albion and, away, Crystal Palace by 4–2. Behind those results was a team whose shape was becoming recognisable. Coutinho, Firmino, Mané and Lallana were the attacking players around whom it was built. Henderson, in midfield alongside Emre Can or Wijnaldum, was its engine. Daniel Sturridge, who had started against Leicester in the centre of the attack, now spent more time on the bench.

The most striking change was at left-back. James Milner, who was also taking the side's penalties, was asked to play there, and did so with characteristic plainness. He was reported as telling The Times that he was not going to lie and claim he loved playing in the position, but that the team came first and he would not have been pressured into it had he not been willing.

\* \* \*

On Friday 4 November Liverpool appointed Michael Edwards as sporting director. Edwards, who had joined in 2011 to run the analytics department before becoming technical director, would lead the identification, signing, sale and retention of players. Klopp called it a very natural decision, and contemporary reports were careful to note that the manager kept the final say on transfers.

Two days later Liverpool beat Watford 6–1 at Anfield and went top of the Premier League for the first time since May 2014. Mané scored twice; Coutinho, Can, Firmino and, in stoppage time, Wijnaldum scored the others. In The National, Richard Jolly called them thrillingly good. Liverpool had scored 30 goals in 11 league matches, he noted, a rate that would bring 103 over a season, and Coutinho was the side's catalytic figure. Walter Mazzarri, Watford's manager, called them title favourites. Klopp was more careful. He said he was happy rather than thrilled, and that being a point clear after 11 matches was no great sign of how the season would end.

Liverpool drew 0–0 at Southampton, and on 20 November Chelsea, now on a winning run under Antonio Conte, went above them. Six days later, against Sunderland, Coutinho was carried off on a stretcher. A scan showed ligament damage to his right ankle and he was expected to miss at least five weeks. "Of course we can handle it," Klopp said.

The evidence of the next fortnight was mixed. At Bournemouth on 4 December Liverpool led 2–0 at half-time and 3–1 with a quarter of an hour left, with Matip as well as Coutinho missing through injury. Bournemouth scored three times from the 76th minute onwards, the winner coming in stoppage time when Nathan Aké followed up after Karius fumbled a shot. The reaction was severe. Jamie Carragher said on Sky that in eight starts Karius had shown him nothing to suggest he was good enough at that level. Klopp said the error said nothing about Karius as a goalkeeper.

A week later West Ham drew 2–2 at Anfield, and Sky Sports judged Karius at fault for both goals. Klopp's response to the criticism was to turn on the critics. He questioned why Gary Neville, whose spell managing Valencia had shown how hard it was to judge players, was allowed to judge them on television. Neville replied on Twitter that he was not a chef but knew a good steak. Three days after that, at Middlesbrough, Karius was left out and Mignolet restored, and Liverpool won 3–0.

The year ended with three wins. At Goodison Park on 19 December the derby was into stoppage time when Sturridge, on as a substitute, hit the post from the edge of the area and Mané reacted first to score into an empty net. Stoke were beaten 4–1 at Anfield after taking an early lead. On New Year's Eve, against Guardiola's Manchester City, Wijnaldum's header after eight minutes was enough for a 1–0 win. Klopp had played down their history beforehand, saying that past meetings counted for little because the players and systems were different.

At the turn of the year Liverpool were second with 43 points from 19 matches. Nobody in the league had scored more than their 46 goals. But they had conceded 21, eight more than Chelsea, and Chelsea, who beat Stoke on the same day for their 13th consecutive league win, were six points clear.

\* \* \*

January undid much of what had been built. Liverpool drew 2–2 at Sunderland on 2 January, Jermain Defoe scoring twice from the penalty spot, in what was Mané's last game before he left to play for Senegal at the Africa Cup of Nations. Over the following four weeks Liverpool played eight more matches and won one of them, an FA Cup third-round replay at Plymouth Argyle, of League Two, after being held 0–0 by Plymouth at Anfield.

They lost the first leg of the League Cup semi-final 1–0 at Southampton and drew 1–1 at Old Trafford, where Zlatan Ibrahimović equalised six minutes from time. Swansea, who had been struggling against relegation, came to Anfield and won 3–2 despite two goals from Firmino. On 25 January Southampton won the second leg 1–0 with a stoppage-time goal from Shane Long, and three days later Wolverhampton Wanderers scored in the first minute at Anfield and won the FA Cup tie 2–1. Klopp, who had started several young players, called it a very bad performance, and said the second half had been better, but that it was not possible to be worse. In nine matches in January Liverpool won one, drew four and lost four, and were out of both cups within four days.

Mané's tournament ended in a quarter-final defeat to Cameroon on penalties, and it was his kick that was missed at the decisive moment. Liverpool arranged a private jet to bring him back for Chelsea's visit on 31 January. That game was drawn 1–1, David Luiz's free-kick cancelled out by Wijnaldum, and on 4 February Liverpool lost 2–0 at Hull.

On the morning of 25 January, the day of the Southampton defeat, the club confirmed that Coutinho had signed a new five-year contract. Klopp called him truly world-class, in the very top bracket. In a month when Liverpool were losing matches and their place in the title race, they had secured the player around whom much of their attack was built.

By 10 February, when Ryan O'Hanlon examined the slump for The Ringer, Liverpool had not won a league match in 2017. They had drawn with Sunderland and lost to Swansea and Hull, all three of them in danger of relegation, and were fifth, 13 points behind Chelsea. O'Hanlon found a peculiar pattern. Against the other members of the top six Liverpool had dropped only eight points, fewer than any of their rivals. Chelsea had dropped two points against the bottom 14 all season, and Liverpool were losing points to those sides at a far higher rate. He also found that Liverpool's save percentage was seven points below average, and that teams that defended deep were asking a question of their attack it struggled to answer.

By the end of the season the pattern had held. In ten league matches against the other clubs who finished in the top six Liverpool won five and drew five, and lost none. All six of their league defeats came against sides outside that group, four of them after the turn of the year. But the contrast was sharper in defeats than in points. Liverpool took 20 points from those ten games against the top six, two per game, and 56 from their other 28 matches, also two per game. The problem was not that they could not beat weaker teams. It was that when they lost, it was to them.

\* \* \*

The recovery began on 11 February, when Mané scored twice in two minutes in a 2–0 win over Tottenham. Then came another setback. Henderson missed the 3–1 defeat at Leicester on 27 February with a foot injury, and he was still missing in May, when Klopp said it was too early to say when he would return.

Without him, Liverpool beat Arsenal 3–1 and Burnley 2–1 at Anfield and drew 1–1 at Manchester City, where Milner's penalty was cancelled out by Sergio Agüero. On 1 April they beat Everton 3–1, Mané scoring the first. He was also injured in the match, damaging his knee. Four days later Liverpool drew 2–2 at home to Bournemouth, the kind of result that had haunted the season. On 7 April Klopp said that surgery was likely and that it would be pretty much impossible for him to play again that season. Liverpool had seven league matches left, and would play them without their most dangerous forward or their captain.

In the first of them Liverpool came from behind to win 2–1 at Stoke, Coutinho and Firmino scoring within two minutes, and won 1–0 at West Brom with a Firmino header. On 23 April Crystal Palace won 2–1 at Anfield, and the scorer of both their goals was Benteke.

From there the run-in was as steady as anything Liverpool produced all season. Can's goal, later voted the club's goal of the season, won the match at Watford 1–0 on 1 May. Southampton held them 0–0 at Anfield, but Liverpool won 4–0 at West Ham, Coutinho scoring twice, and went into the final day needing to beat Middlesbrough, already relegated, to be certain of fourth place. During the first half Arsenal led Everton and Liverpool were briefly fifth. Klopp said afterwards that he had asked for the other score only at half-time. By then Wijnaldum had scored. Coutinho added a free-kick early in the second half and Lallana a third soon after.

Liverpool finished fourth with 76 points, one more than Arsenal and two fewer than City. Chelsea were champions with 93, Tottenham second with 86. After the defeat by Palace they took ten points from their last four matches. Coutinho, who had missed several weeks in the winter, finished level with Mané as the club's top league scorer on 13, and scored 14 in all competitions; Firmino scored 11 league goals. Mané was named in the PFA Team of the Year and won both of the club's main player of the year awards. The club's young player award went to Trent Alexander-Arnold, who had made his debut against Tottenham in the League Cup in October.

He was not the only one. In the League Cup quarter-final against Leeds United in November, Ben Woodburn had scored aged 17 years and 45 days, becoming the youngest goalscorer in Liverpool's history and breaking a record Michael Owen had held for 19 years.

\* \* \*

"A club like Liverpool need to be in the Champions League," Klopp said after the Middlesbrough match. "We are Liverpool. We have to deliver." He said people deserved the opportunity, and that when the team's timing improved they had been very dangerous.

The case for why it mattered is clearest in the club's own numbers, though they were not published until the following spring. In the year to the end of May 2017 Liverpool's revenue rose by £62m to £364m. Media income went up by £30m, to £154m, in the first year of the Premier League's new broadcasting contract. Matchday revenue rose by £12m, to £74m, mostly through hospitality in the new Main Stand. A loss of £21m the previous year became a profit of £39m. These were good figures, and they came without any income from European competition at all.

They were not enough to close the gap. Deloitte's annual ranking, also published later, of the richest clubs placed Liverpool ninth in Europe for that season, behind Manchester United, Manchester City, Arsenal and Chelsea. Four English rivals had more money, and in the summer of 2016 all four had spent more than £85m net on players while Liverpool were net sellers. Klopp's first full season had shown how far coaching, conditioning and a handful of well-chosen signings could take a squad. It had also shown, in January, how thin that squad was when its best players were absent.

The Champions League was the one source of revenue and standing that Liverpool's position in the market did not yet give them. Qualification did not guarantee it, either. Fourth place meant a play-off in August, not a place in the group stage. But if they came through it, Liverpool would be able, for the first time under Klopp, to offer the players they wanted something more than the promise of what the club might become.

Within three weeks of the final day, the club had issued a public apology to Southampton over Virgil van Dijk and said its interest in him was over.
