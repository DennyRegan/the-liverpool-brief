The previous season had ended in relief: third place, rescued late, by a side that had spent the winter fielding whichever centre-backs were still standing. Liverpool's answer that summer was a single senior signing. Ibrahima Konaté arrived from RB Leipzig for £36m, the value of his release clause, as Sky Sports reported, and the logic was plain enough. With Virgil van Dijk and Joe Gomez lost to knee injuries in the autumn of 2020, Jürgen Klopp had used 16 different centre-back pairings in the league.

Everything else was consolidation. Georginio Wijnaldum left for Paris Saint-Germain on a free transfer and was not replaced. The money went instead on keeping the spine together: Trent Alexander-Arnold, Andy Robertson, Fabinho, Alisson, Van Dijk and Jordan Henderson all signed new long-term deals. Not everyone was persuaded. Goal graded the window a C and judged the squad "one or two players short", and Jamie Carragher observed on Sky Sports that "a lot of the money Liverpool are going to spend will be on wages". Manchester City had paid £100m for Jack Grealish; Chelsea £97.5m for Romelu Lukaku. Klopp was unmoved. "There's no need to buy a midfielder just because there's one on the market," he said, and he pointed to Harvey Elliott, 18, as the next step in midfield.

Elliott's chance lasted until the second week of September. At Elland Road on 12 September, in a 3–0 win, a challenge from Pascal Struijk left him with what the club confirmed as a fracture-dislocation of the left ankle. "We all saw the ankle was not in the right place," Klopp said afterwards, "and the medical team put it in the right place." Elliott had surgery two days later. The thinness of the midfield the critics had worried about was now a fact.

For a while it hardly seemed to matter. Liverpool opened the Champions League by coming from 2–1 down at half-time to beat AC Milan 3–2 at Anfield, then won 5–1 in Porto and 3–2 in Madrid, where Antoine Griezmann scored twice for Atlético and was sent off, and Mohamed Salah settled it with a penalty twelve minutes from time. On 3 October City came to Anfield and drew 2–2, but the afternoon was remembered for Salah's goal, a run through the City defence that Sky Sports called one of his finest for the club. "Only the best players in the world score goals like this," Klopp said, before conceding of the first half: "Our biggest problem was we didn't play football." Three weeks later Liverpool were 4–0 up at Old Trafford by half-time and won 5–0, Salah scoring a hat-trick. Klopp called it "insane".

The points were nonetheless leaking. Brentford drew 3–3 with them in September, and Brighton drew 2–2 at Anfield at the end of October. On 7 November West Ham won 3–2 at the London Stadium, the first goal an Alisson own goal from a corner that Klopp called a "clear foul" on his goalkeeper. It was Liverpool's first league defeat of the season. Three days later the club announced that Michael Edwards, the sporting director whose recruitment had built this side, would leave when his contract ended in the summer. FSG had tried and failed to extend it; his assistant, Julian Ward, would succeed him. Edwards' open letter called Klopp someone who "will go down in history as one of the club's managerial greats." At the time it registered as a boardroom story. Only later would it read as the first sign of a squad and structure approaching the end of a cycle.

The group stage closed at San Siro on 7 December with a 2–1 win, six victories from six. Liverpool were the first English club to manage that.

* * *

Then came the fortnight that, more than any other, decided the league. On 19 December Liverpool drew 2–2 at Tottenham, a match in which Robertson headed them ahead and was then sent off after a VAR review, and Son Heung-min equalised after an error by Alisson. Their Boxing Day game against Leeds was postponed because of Covid cases in the Leeds squad. On 28 December they lost 1–0 at Leicester, Salah having a first-half penalty saved by Kasper Schmeichel before Ademola Lookman scored just before the hour. On 2 January they led 2–0 at Stamford Bridge through Sadio Mané and Salah, and were pegged back to 2–2 by half-time.

Two points from nine, in 15 days. City, meanwhile, did not drop a league point between their defeat by Crystal Palace at the end of October and a draw at Southampton on 22 January. One contemporary headline, after the Chelsea draw, called the title City's "to lose". When City beat Chelsea 1–0 on 15 January, the gap stood at 14 points: City on 56 from 22 games, Liverpool on 42 from 20.

That number became the season's shorthand, and it was always slightly misleading. Liverpool had two games in hand; converted, as they would be, those were worth six points. The true deficit was nearer eight. Guardiola said as much in April, with some relish: "I'm going to tell you something: 14 points, it was fake. You were wrong." Klopp's version was simpler: "We knew it was 14 points but we didn't care."

What happened next is the core of the season. Salah and Mané left for the Africa Cup of Nations after the Chelsea game, and a Covid outbreak shut the training ground and postponed the first leg of the League Cup semi-final against Arsenal. In their absence Liverpool did not lose. They drew 0–0 with Arsenal at Anfield despite playing against ten men for most of the night after Granit Xhaka's dismissal, won the return 2–0 with two goals from Diogo Jota, and beat Brentford and Crystal Palace in the league. On 30 January the club moved early for Luis Díaz, bringing forward a summer plan because of Tottenham's interest; the reported fee was £37.5m, potentially rising to around £50m.

On 6 February, the same day Senegal beat Egypt on penalties in the AFCON final in Yaoundé, Mané scoring the decisive kick and Salah never getting to take one, Liverpool beat Cardiff in the FA Cup. Díaz made his debut. Elliott came off the bench and scored his first goal for the club, 147 days after Elland Road.

The league run that followed was ten straight wins, from Brentford on 16 January to Watford on 2 April. Defensively the side changed. After 20 league games they had conceded 18, more than City, Chelsea or Wolves; after AFCON they kept ten clean sheets in their remaining 16 league matches. The high line was the most conspicuous part of it. By mid-February Liverpool had caught opponents offside 101 times, 40 more than anyone else, and it still drew suspicion: on Match of the Day 2 Jermaine Jenas called it "shocking" after the win at Selhurst Park. The pressing, too, was the league's most productive. By the club's own figures Liverpool won the ball within 40 metres of the opponents' goal 443 times, more than any other side. Up front Díaz settled on the left and Mané moved inside to centre-forward.

City's results helped. They drew at Southampton, lost 3–2 at home to Tottenham on 19 February, and drew at Crystal Palace in March. When Liverpool won 2–0 at the Emirates on 16 March, the gap was one point with the games level: City 70, Liverpool 69, 29 played each.

* * *

In between, Liverpool won the first trophy of the season, and did so in a way that should be kept in mind whenever the word "margins" is used about this team. The League Cup final against Chelsea on 27 February finished 0–0 after 120 minutes. Four goals were disallowed: Joël Matip's header for Liverpool, ruled out by VAR because Van Dijk was offside and judged to be interfering; two from Kai Havertz and one from Lukaku for Chelsea. Klopp kept faith with his cup goalkeeper, Caoimhin Kelleher, whom he called "the best No.2 in the world". Thomas Tuchel sent on Kepa Arrizabalaga for Édouard Mendy at the very end of extra time to face the shootout.

The first 21 penalties were all scored. Kelleher took Liverpool's 11th and scored to make it 11–10. Kepa then put Chelsea's 11th over the bar. It was Liverpool's ninth League Cup, a record, and as Klopp put it, "the first time for this group but the ninth time for the club." He called the competition "the people's cup". Kelleher, who would describe his kick as the winning penalty, said: "never in my wildest dreams would I have thought I'd score a penalty at the Liverpool end."

The physical load was beginning to tell, and the league itself acknowledged it. On 31 March Premier League clubs voted to allow five substitutes from the following season, after rejecting the change three times; Klopp and Guardiola had both backed it publicly. It came too late for this campaign.

April compressed the season into a week. On 10 April Liverpool went to the Etihad and drew 2–2: Kevin De Bruyne's deflected shot, Jota, Gabriel Jesus, then Mané within moments of the restart. City stayed a point clear. "We know now that we have to win every game," said Guardiola. Klopp: "We have to be nearly perfect to beat this team in a game and over a season too."

Six days later the sides met again in the FA Cup semi-final at Wembley. The fixture had already drawn complaint, though not about timing. The West Coast Main Line was closed for Easter engineering works, the mayors of both cities asked for a northern venue, and Henderson said moving the tie "would make sense". The FA kept it at Wembley and laid on buses.

The match was played the day after the 33rd anniversary of the Hillsborough disaster, in which 97 Liverpool supporters lost their lives, the 97th being Andrew Devine, who died in 2021. A minute's silence before kick-off was ended early by the referee, Michael Oliver, after some City supporters chanted through it. Manchester City apologised that evening "to all those connected with Liverpool Football Club."

Liverpool were 3–0 up by half-time. Konaté headed in a corner, his third opener in as many games; Mané forced in the second after Zack Steffen dawdled on a back-pass; Mané volleyed the third from Thiago's chip. Grealish and Bernardo Silva made it 3–2, the second in stoppage time. City's treble was over. Liverpool's quadruple was alive.

So was the European run. Liverpool had won 2–0 at San Siro against Inter, then lost the return 1–0 at Anfield. They beat Benfica 3–1 in Lisbon and drew 3–3 at home. Against Unai Emery's Villarreal they won the first leg 2–0, then trailed 2–0 at half-time in Spain, the tie level. Fabinho, Díaz and Mané scored in the space of 12 minutes in the second half. One of those goals took Liverpool past the club's scoring record of 138 in a season, set in 1985–86; they would finish with 147.

Klopp's own future was settled in the middle of all this. On 28 April he extended his contract from 2024 to 2026. "There is a freshness about us as a club still," he said, "and this energizes me."

* * *

May exposed the cost. Fabinho went off with a hamstring injury against Aston Villa on 10 May. Three days earlier Tottenham had drawn 1–1 at Anfield, Son scoring and Díaz equalising with a deflected shot; it left the clubs level on 83 points with City a game in hand, and City beat Newcastle 5–0 the next day.

The FA Cup final, on Saturday 14 May, was Chelsea again and goalless again. Salah went off with a groin problem after 33 minutes; Van Dijk was replaced at the end of normal time. Marcos Alonso hit the crossbar with a free-kick; Robertson hit the post from close range. In the shootout César Azpilicueta struck the post, and Mané had the chance to win it with Liverpool's fifth, only for Mendy to save. In sudden death Alisson saved from Mason Mount, and Kostas Tsimikas, sent on in the 111th minute, scored. It was Liverpool's first FA Cup since 2006. Klopp captured the bind exactly: "So the quadruple is 'on' if you want, but off as well because of the situation we are in."

They won at Southampton three days later, which left them a point behind going into the last day. City needed to beat Aston Villa at home; Liverpool needed to beat Wolves and hope. Pedro Neto scored for Wolves at Anfield in the third minute and Mané equalised. At the Etihad, Matty Cash and Philippe Coutinho put Villa 2–0 up with 69 minutes gone. For those minutes, a Liverpool winner would have meant the title. Then İlkay Gündoğan headed in, Rodri scored from outside the box and Gündoğan tapped in a third: three goals in five minutes. Salah and Robertson scored late at Anfield, but by then it no longer mattered. "The moment we found a goal it changed everything," Guardiola said. Thiago came off injured. Klopp's verdict was brief: "We were close but in the end not close enough. That's how it is."

So how close was it? City finished on 93 points, Liverpool on 92. Both conceded 26; City scored five more. Liverpool lost twice and drew eight times; City lost three and drew six. The two head-to-heads were both 2–2, so the title was decided against the other 18 sides, and there Liverpool dropped 18 points to City's 17. With City's results unchanged, turning any one of Liverpool's eight draws into a win would have made them champions.

The arithmetic cannot name a single decisive match: a win over Tottenham in May would have been enough, just as a win at Leicester in December would. What it can show is where the points went. From 16 January Liverpool played 18 league games, won 16 and drew two, away at City and at home to a Tottenham side chasing the top four. That is close to the "nearly perfect" Klopp said would be required. Between 19 December and 2 January they dropped seven points in three matches against Tottenham, Leicester and Chelsea, while City won every week. No other run of three league matches cost them as much. The May draw is the one remembered, but the case here is that the title was lost at the turn of the year rather than the turn of the season: that the comeback ran out of road at one point is the clearest sign of how much ground the winter had given away.

The cups need the same honesty. Liverpool won both domestic trophies without scoring in 240 minutes of final football against Chelsea, by 11–10 and 6–5 on penalties, with a Chelsea penalty missed, another struck against a post, and one save each from the goalkeepers. They were as near to losing those finals as they would be to winning in Paris.

* * *

Before the final in Paris, Klopp had described Real Madrid as favourites: "If you look at the way Real Madrid celebrate those comebacks then I would say they are the favourites." Thiago and Fabinho were passed fit to start.

What happened outside the Stade de France on 28 May belongs in this history as much as the football. Liverpool supporters with valid tickets were held in crushes at the turnstiles, and police used tear gas. Kick-off was delayed by more than half an hour. UEFA's statement that night blamed "fake tickets which did not work in the turnstiles". The French interior minister, Gérald Darmanin, wrote that "thousands of British 'supporters' either without tickets or with fake ones forced their way through." Liverpool said it was "hugely disappointed at the stadium entry issues and breakdown of the security perimeter" and asked for a formal investigation.

The match itself followed the season's pattern. Courtois turned a Mané shot against the post in the 21st minute; Karim Benzema had a goal disallowed for offside just before half-time after a long VAR check. On 59 minutes Federico Valverde drove the ball across the six-yard box and Vinícius Júnior, unmarked at the back post, scored. Liverpool had 23 shots to Madrid's three, nine on target to one. Courtois made nine saves, including one from Salah in the 83rd minute that UEFA's report called stunning, and Madrid won 1–0.

The shot count invites the conclusion that Liverpool deserved more. Klopp was more careful. "They scored a goal and we didn't," he said, "that's the easiest explanation in the world of football." He also said, as Sky Sports reported, that only three of Liverpool's chances had forced Courtois into top saves. Robertson credited "an unbelievable goalie" and added: "When you come up against an experienced team, they know how to win finals."

The official account of the night outside took much longer to establish. In July 2022 a French Senate inquiry found a "string of dysfunctions" at every level and blamed the organisers, not the supporters. In February 2023 the independent review commissioned by UEFA found that UEFA, "as event owner, bears primary responsibility for failures which almost led to disaster." It called the night "a near-miss: a term used when an event almost turns into a mass fatality catastrophe", and said: "It is remarkable that no one lost their life." Claims about large numbers of ticketless fans, it found, "have been wrongly inflated and have been stated as fact, to deflect responsibility."

The review also noted that the false allegations against supporters "have a particular resonance with Hillsborough, where similar allegations were made."

UEFA apologised, and in March 2023 announced full refunds for Liverpool's entire allocation of 19,618 tickets. Spirit of Shankly said UEFA had "gone some way to acknowledging their part in the fiasco", but that this did not "exempt them from criticism."

* * *

The full record of 2021–22 reads: 63 matches, 46 wins, a club record; four defeats; 147 goals; two trophies; 92 points; a European Cup final. By the club's own figures, the 73 per cent win rate was the best of any Liverpool side in the top flight. No team had finished second in the Premier League with more points except Liverpool themselves, three years earlier.

The margins say something more precise than "so close". In the league, the costliest damage was done at Christmas, and Liverpool spent the next five months nearly repairing it. In the cups, they won twice by the narrowest possible margin. In Paris, they lost to the same kind of margin, on a night when the far greater failure was nothing to do with football. The fine margins did not all go against Liverpool that season. They broke roughly even, and what separated a historic season from an unprecedented one was a fortnight in midwinter and a goalkeeper in May.

Klopp's own last word on the season was characteristically forward-looking. "I have a strong feeling we will come again," he said in Paris.
