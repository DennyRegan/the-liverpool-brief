On 13 July 2022, 46 days after the Champions League final in Paris, the French Senate published its provisional findings into what had happened outside the Stade de France. Blaming Liverpool supporters, as the interior minister Gérald Darmanin had done, had been unfair, the Senate said, and a way of diverting attention from the state's failure to manage the crowds. Billy Hogan, Liverpool's chief executive, backed the report's 15 recommendations and called on the French government to apologise. UEFA's own independent review was still months away. The new season would begin before the supporters who had been in Paris had been fully cleared.

The squad that began that season was, in most of its essentials, the one that had finished the last. The great exception was in attack. On 22 June Sadio Mané joined Bayern Munich for a fee theScore reported as €32m plus €9m in add-ons, having scored 120 goals in 269 appearances since 2016. "One of Liverpool's greatest ever players is leaving," Klopp said, "and we must acknowledge how significant this is." A week earlier Liverpool had announced Darwin Núñez from Benfica on a six-year contract; Sky Sports reported the fee as £64m up front, rising to £85m if milestones were met. Fábio Carvalho and Calvin Ramsay also arrived. Takumi Minamino, Neco Williams and Divock Origi left.

The midfield was left as it was. Asked at Kirkby on 8 July whether Liverpool would sign a midfielder, Klopp said: "No. I can say that." Then he turned the question round. "So where is the need for a midfielder? I wait for the day when somebody comes to me and tells me why, exactly." In August, by This Is Anfield's record of his remarks, he was still making the case: "We have eight midfielders, we still have enough midfielders."

That did not hold for long. By 2 September Jordan Henderson had a hamstring injury and was "definitely out". Naby Keïta and Alex Oxlade-Chamberlain had muscle injuries, and Thiago, also injured, was "the closest of them all" to a return. In the window's last days Klopp's position shifted. "We were going for a midfielder," he said, "but that midfielder decided to go to another club. That can happen." 90min reported that the target was widely believed to be Aurélien Tchouaméni, who had joined Real Madrid; FourFourTwo would later state that Liverpool had tried for him. On deadline day, 1 September, Arthur Melo arrived on loan from Juventus. "He gives us rhythm, he is a really good passer," Klopp said. "Didn't work out 100% at Juventus. I see it as positive because potential is still there." By FourFourTwo's count, Arthur played 13 minutes all season.

There had been a mixed pre-season. Liverpool opened it with a 4–0 friendly defeat to Manchester United in Bangkok, and Klopp would say that he "wouldn't go, in the first week, to Asia". On 30 July, in the Community Shield at the King Power Stadium, they beat Manchester City 3–1, Núñez scoring in stoppage time.

* * *

The league season began with two points from three matches: draws at Fulham and at home to Crystal Palace, and a 2–1 defeat at Old Trafford. A 9–0 win over Bournemouth, a narrower one over Newcastle and a goalless derby at Goodison followed. Results like these could be read either way. On 7 September, in Naples, they could not. Napoli won 4–1, their third successive home victory over Liverpool, and Victor Osimhen missed a penalty and hit the post.

Klopp's diagnosis that night was precise, and it was not about personnel. "Tonight was the least compact performance I saw from us for a long, long time," he said. The high defensive line was not the problem in itself. "A high line is not a risk when you have pressure on the ball. The problem is we never got close enough to put the opponent under pressure." His conclusion, reported by Andy Hunter in the Guardian, would follow the team all autumn. "We have to reinvent ourselves, the basic things were not there." He was careful about what that meant. "It is not that we have to invent a new kind of football but we have to improve."

Data published by Sky Sports at the end of October gave the complaint a shape. Compared with the previous season, Liverpool's recoveries in the final third had fallen from 7.6 a game to 5.0, from first in the league on that measure to eighth. The team was defending about 2.3 metres deeper; only Crystal Palace had dropped further. Yet Liverpool were running more, not less, their average distance per game up from 106.78km to 109.44km. Fabinho's figures followed the same pattern. His interceptions were down by 30 per cent and his middle-third recoveries by 28 per cent, while he was covering more ground than in either of his two previous seasons. He had played 76 per cent of the available league minutes, the highest share of his Liverpool career, and 64 matches since the start of 2021–22. This was a team, and a player, working harder to win the ball back less often.

Trent Alexander-Arnold became the most visible symbol of the problem. In September Gareth Southgate left him on the bench against Italy and out of the matchday squad against Germany, saying his all-round game was weaker than Kieran Trippier's. Klopp's defence was measured. "Obviously, I would decide differently," he said, before conceding the point. "He doesn't always defend [well]; that's true as well." The player was 23, he added, and the club was working on it.

October gathered the season's contradictions into a few weeks. A 3–3 draw with Brighton was followed by a 3–2 defeat at Arsenal. Then, on 16 October, Liverpool beat City 1–0 at Anfield with a goal from Mohamed Salah, after Phil Foden's goal had been ruled out for a foul by Erling Haaland on Fabinho. It might have been one of the good days of the season, but other things happened that afternoon. Pep Guardiola confirmed that coins had been thrown at him from the home crowd. Klopp, who said he had not known, apologised: "I am sorry. I apologise for that. I had no idea about it. It never should happen, never." The club called it "totally unacceptable behaviour" and said culprits could face lifetime bans. Klopp himself was sent off for confronting the assistant referee Gary Beswick, and after an FA appeal against his original £30,000 fine he served a one-match touchline ban against Southampton on 12 November.

In between, Liverpool lost 1–0 at Nottingham Forest, Klopp blaming missed chances and a goal that should have been avoided, and a week later 2–1 at home to Leeds, ending a run of 29 top-flight home games without defeat. Wins at Tottenham and at home to Southampton steadied things before the World Cup. By a calculation from the season's results, Liverpool went into the break sixth, with 22 points from 14 games, 15 behind the leaders, Arsenal. In Europe, after Naples, they won their five remaining group games, including 7–1 at Ibrox, but finished second to Napoli on head-to-head goal difference.

* * *

November brought a different kind of uncertainty. On 7 November Fenway Sports Group said it would consider new shareholders "under the right terms and conditions", while insisting it "remains fully committed to the success of Liverpool, both on and off the pitch". Sky Sports reported that Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley had been asked to gauge interest. On 24 November the club announced that Julian Ward would leave as sporting director at the end of the season. Ward had succeeded Michael Edwards that summer and, according to the Liverpool Echo, was barely six months into the role officially. The Echo's sources called his decision surprising and said the club had gone to great lengths to make him reconsider. Sky's Melissa Reddy linked it to the upheaval around the possible sale. Liverpool would go into the winter, and towards a summer in which the midfield would have to be addressed, with the man in charge of recruitment working his notice.

The first business after the break was in attack. On 26 December ESPN reported that Liverpool had agreed to sign Cody Gakpo from PSV Eindhoven for about £37m, and he joined when the window opened. Much was made of Liverpool beating Manchester United to him, though Sky's Joe Shread judged injuries the bigger factor. On 2 January Liverpool lost 3–1 at Brentford, and Virgil van Dijk came off at half-time with a hamstring injury that would keep him out for more than a month. That week's injury list also included Luis Díaz, Diogo Jota, Roberto Firmino, Arthur and James Milner.

Klopp still rejected the idea of a single fault. "It's a general problem, not a midfield problem," he said on 7 January. Six days later he gave a different explanation. "Could anybody know how the last season of 63 games could influence this season? I don't think anybody could know. It must have. We can say that now." The next day Liverpool lost 3–0 at Brighton, Solly March scoring twice. "I can't remember a worse game," Klopp said. ESPN had his side eighth, seven points off the top four. Three days later The National reported him on his own position. He had extended his contract to 2026 the previous April, and he would not walk away: "So as far as I am concerned unless someone tells me I will not go."

The cups went in the same weeks. City had already won 3–2 in the League Cup on 22 December. In the FA Cup Liverpool needed a replay to get past Wolves, and on 29 January they went out at Brighton to a stoppage-time goal from Kaoru Mitoma. On 4 February Wolves beat them 3–0 in the league. Sky Sports noted that Liverpool were tenth, closer to the relegation zone than to the Champions League places. Klopp's response went further than anything he had said before. "How can I not be concerned? I cannot sit here and say it is ok because it is not." He returned to the 63 games, then set them aside. "It is clear that has influenced the start of the season but how long can we suffer that? It is February by my watch." The failings, he suggested, were basic ones. "Do you need a lot of confidence for that? To put your body between the ball and opponent? Stop the cross?" Three days later Sports Illustrated ran a piece headlined "Liverpool May Be Approaching the End of the Jurgen Klopp Era".

By then Klopp's public view of the squad had moved. In January he had asked: "Will we have to strengthen? Oh yes. But is this the right moment to do it?" In February he said "it's already clear that we have to do something in the summer." Even Fabinho, he said, "knows that he's not flying and has to work his way back."

* * *

On 13 February, the day Liverpool beat Everton 2–0 for their first league win in five matches, UEFA's independent review of the Paris final was published. It found that "UEFA, as event owner, bears primary responsibility for failures which almost led to disaster", and that it was "remarkable that no one lost their life". Claims about large numbers of ticketless Liverpool supporters had been "wrongly inflated", which the panel called "reprehensible", and it criticised the Paris police for using tear gas and pepper spray without sufficient justification. UEFA's general secretary, Theodore Theodoridis, apologised "most sincerely once again". The club said the report "fully vindicates Liverpool fans", whose actions had "saved lives"; Spirit of Shankly said supporters "bear no culpability". On 7 March UEFA announced full refunds for Liverpool's entire allocation of 19,618 tickets, which Spirit of Shankly said had "gone some way to acknowledging their part in the fiasco". On 16 March the UEFA president, Aleksander Čeferin, said on The Overlap: "That was a mistake, that was a mistake from our side."

The football in those weeks swung between extremes. Against Real Madrid in the first leg of the last 16 on 21 February, Núñez and Salah scored inside 14 minutes, an opening Klopp would call "the best we've played for probably the whole season". Madrid then scored five. The National called it Liverpool's worst European home defeat and noted that no team in Champions League history had led 2–0 and lost by three. "I think we gave all five goals away," Klopp said.

On 5 March came a result that seemed to belong to another season. Liverpool beat Manchester United 7–0 at Anfield, Gakpo, Núñez and Salah scoring twice each and Firmino once. ITV reported it as the biggest victory in the fixture's 211-match history and United's heaviest defeat since 1931; Salah's goals made him Liverpool's record Premier League scorer. Six days later Liverpool lost 1–0 at Bournemouth, Salah putting a penalty wide. "This game was a massive knock," Klopp said. Sky noted that they had won three of 13 away league games. "With away games, we are not even in Europe." On 15 March Karim Benzema's late goal settled the tie in Madrid, 6–2 on aggregate.

* * *

Before the trip to the Etihad on 1 April Klopp spoke about change in terms that sounded final. "After seven years, it is clear that we have to do it," he said, as ESPN reported it. City won 4–1. "We were too passive, too open, too deep, too far away from anything," Klopp said. "I have to explain it but I can't. I can only describe it." It was Liverpool's fifth league defeat in ten matches in 2023. ESPN's Mark Ogden set out the ages in midfield: Henderson 32, Thiago about to turn 32, Fabinho 30 in October. Milner and Oxlade-Chamberlain would leave as free agents, Keïta's contract was expiring and Arthur would go back to Juventus. Liverpool were seven points behind fourth-placed Tottenham.

After a goalless draw at Chelsea, Klopp changed something. At home to Arsenal on 9 April Alexander-Arnold was still the right-back when Liverpool were defending, but with the ball he moved inside to play alongside Fabinho as one of two holding midfielders, with Ibrahima Konaté covering the space behind. This Is Anfield described the shape as a 3-2-5. Liverpool came from 2–0 down to draw 2–2. Klopp said the idea was not new: Liverpool had put Alexander-Arnold inside before, but it had been less obvious.

Within days Liverpool were reported to have ended their interest in Jude Bellingham. Sky Sports reported that Borussia Dortmund wanted around €150m. "You can't spend £120m on Bellingham and then another £120m on a striker," said Julien Laurens, who argued that Liverpool should have put more into midfield instead of Núñez.

Then the run began. Liverpool won at Leeds 6–1, at home to Forest and at West Ham. On 30 April they led Tottenham 3–0 inside 15 minutes, were pegged back to 3–3 in the third minute of stoppage time and won through Jota a minute later. Klopp's remarks afterwards about the referee Paul Tierney – "We have our history with Mr Tierney" – brought an FA charge, which he admitted, and on 18 May a £75,000 fine and a touchline ban. Fulham and Brentford were beaten 1–0, and on 15 May a 3–0 win at Leicester made it seven league wins in a row and secured a place in the Europa League.

On 17 May the club announced that Firmino, Keïta, Milner and Oxlade-Chamberlain would leave when their contracts expired. Against Aston Villa three days later, Firmino came off the bench to score an 89th-minute equaliser in his last home game. "For so long we couldn't even hear the sound of the Champions League, that's how far away we were," Klopp said. "We were too long not good enough ourselves." Five days later Manchester United's 4–1 win over Chelsea put a top-four finish out of reach. The last day, at Southampton, finished 4–4. Liverpool were fifth with 67 points, four behind Newcastle, unbeaten in their last 11 league games.

Klopp's verdict was plain. "There were moments in the season where you thought the season would be four years long. We played a bad season and we came fifth, that is a message as well." He did not sound like a man describing an ending. "Imagine we are more our normal self, which we absolutely will be again next season, then we will be a contender again." Supporters had other news to weigh as well. The club announced that it would end an eight-season ticket-price freeze for 2023–24, with most prices rising by 2 per cent. Spirit of Shankly replied that in a cost-of-living crisis "any increase – even two per cent – will impact supporters."

* * *

Measured against the year before, the fall was steep. On a count from the season's results, Liverpool had taken 92 points in 2021–22, scoring 94 goals and conceding 26. In 2022–23 they took 67, scoring 75 and conceding 47. The attack had slipped. The defence had given way. When Opta's analysts reviewed the season that August, they found Liverpool had allowed 370 shots, the most in any full season under Klopp, and 103 big chances, against 53 the year before.

The contemporary evidence points to why. Klopp's words after Naples, the Sky data in October and his complaint after Wolves all describe the same failure. The team was still running, more than before, but no longer arriving together. The press that had won the ball high now chased it, the line behind it was left exposed, and the midfield between them no longer closed the space. Fabinho, covering more ground and winning the ball back far less, is the clearest case: a structure failing around a player at least as much as a player tiring. Alexander-Arnold drew the loudest criticism, but Opta counted only two errors by him leading to shots all season, and none to goals.

Behind that lay a series of choices. Liverpool replaced Mané with a centre-forward and chose, publicly and repeatedly, not to sign a midfielder. When the injuries came, the answer was a loan player who barely played, and the long-term target proved too expensive. Responsibility for recruitment passed from Edwards to Ward, and within six months Ward was leaving, while the owners explored outside investment. None of that proves the summer of 2022 would have gone differently otherwise. It does mean the club met a problem in midfield with less continuity at the top of its recruitment than it had had for years.

The 63 games were part of it. Klopp himself said so in January, and by February he was saying they could not explain everything. The spring suggests he was right. With an extra player in midfield when Liverpool had the ball, the side that had been tenth in February took, on Opta's figures, 24 points from its last ten games, a total bettered only by City. Alexander-Arnold, with two league assists in his first 27 games, had seven in those ten. That the change in shape and the run of results came together is a matter of record. That one caused the other is harder to prove: Díaz and Jota were fit again, and the fixtures were kinder. But if the problem had been only tiredness, it is hard to see why rearranging the midfield should have mattered so much.

That is why the question mark matters. Something did end in 2022–23. Four players who had shared in every trophy since 2019 were leaving, the midfield that had defined the team was about to be replaced, and Klopp said the unbeaten run would not change his plans for the summer. But the manager talking of being "a contender again" was not describing a finished project. His first great team had stopped working because one part of it, the part that joined the press to the defence, had been left alone too long. The spring showed that it could be fixed. The summer would show how far Liverpool were prepared to go to fix it.
