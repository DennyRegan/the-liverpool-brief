Jürgen Klopp's last match as Liverpool manager was a 2–0 win over Wolves at Anfield on 19 May 2024. It was his 491st game in charge. Across those games his teams won 299, drew 109 and lost 83, and by the club's own count they scored 1,035 goals. Only Bill Shankly and Tom Watson had previously managed Liverpool to a thousand.

The numbers are the easy part of any assessment. The harder part is deciding what they add up to, and that question has not stood still since he left. In the two and a half years since that afternoon against Wolves, Liverpool have won the league with his players, finished fifth with a squad rebuilt at record cost, dismissed his successor, and lost both of the senior executives who shaped the club after him. In September 2026 an independent commission found Manchester City, the club that beat Klopp's Liverpool to two titles by a single point, guilty of 114 of the 115 Premier League charges against them. A chapter like this written in May 2024 would have read differently.

So the question here is not whether Klopp was a great Liverpool manager. It is narrower and more useful: what exactly did he leave, how much of it lasted, and does the record match the feeling?

## The starting point

It is worth remembering how ordinary Liverpool looked in October 2015. Deloitte's Money League for 2014–15, the last season before he arrived, placed the club ninth in the world on revenue of £298.1m, behind Manchester City, Arsenal and Chelsea among English clubs. Opta's figures show Liverpool scored 52 league goals in Brendan Rodgers' last full season. The squad Klopp inherited had been assembled in a hurry after Luis Suárez left, and much of it did not survive his first three years.

By the end, almost none of it had. When Klopp left, Joe Gomez was the only player in the squad who had been there on the day he arrived.

That matters for the legacy, because it means the team that won everything in 2019 and 2020 was not a lucky inheritance. It was built, almost entirely, in his time.

## What was won

The list is straightforward. The Champions League in 2019. The UEFA Super Cup and the Club World Cup later that year. The Premier League in 2019–20, the club's first league title for thirty years. The FA Cup and the League Cup in 2022, followed by the Community Shield that July. The League Cup again in February 2024. That is eight trophies if the Community Shield is counted, seven if it is not.

The way the league title was won matters as much as the title itself. The club's statistician, Ged Rea, records that Klopp's side went 44 league matches unbeaten between January 2019 and February 2020, equalled the English top-flight record of 18 consecutive league wins, and went 68 league matches unbeaten at Anfield. The 2019–20 side finished with 99 points. Klopp was named FIFA's men's coach of the year in 2019 and again in 2020.

The attack is the other measurable inheritance. Three of the four highest-scoring seasons in Liverpool's history came under Klopp: 135 goals in 2017–18, 142 in 2023–24 and a club-record 147 in 2021–22. Mohamed Salah scored 211 of the 1,035 goals by himself. Opta puts Klopp's Premier League record at 2.11 points per game.

Then there are the finals that went the other way. Klopp's first season ended with two of them lost: the League Cup to City on penalties and the Europa League 3–1 to Sevilla in Basel. Real Madrid beat his side in the Champions League finals of 2018 and 2022. Counting his Dortmund defeat in 2013, Paris was Klopp's third lost Champions League final; no manager in the competition's history has lost more. He also won one, against Tottenham in Madrid, and his two domestic cup wins in 2022 both came on penalties against Chelsea.

A fair summary is that Liverpool under Klopp reached three Champions League finals in five seasons, won one, and spent most of a decade in the conversation for every trophy they entered. That is a higher level of sustained competition than the trophy count alone suggests.

## Should they have won more?

This is the question supporters argue about most, and it has two halves that are easy to confuse.

The first half is about Manchester City. Liverpool finished one point behind them in 2018–19 and again in 2021–22. The first of those seasons produced 97 points, which Opta records as the most ever by a team that did not win the English top-flight title. Liverpool lost one league match all season. In 2021–22 they took 92 points and still finished second.

On 29 September 2026, the Premier League confirmed that its independent commission had found City guilty of all 80 charges relating to breaches of its financial rules and 34 of the 35 charges of failing to co-operate with the league's investigation. The commission found that money presented as sponsorship was in fact owner funding. City deny wrongdoing and have appealed. As of early November, no sanction had been announced.

It is natural to read that verdict as a verdict on the title races Klopp lost. The dates do not support that reading. The case has two parts. The financial offences concern nine seasons, from 2009–10 to 2017–18. The separate non-co-operation charges concern City's conduct during the league's investigation, from December 2018 to February 2023. The financial findings therefore do not establish breaches in either of the seasons in which Liverpool finished a point behind City, 2018–19 and 2021–22. The Klopp seasons covered by the financial charges are his first three, when Liverpool finished eighth, fourth and fourth and were never in a title race. In the last of them, City won the league by nineteen points.

What the verdict does say, in the commission's own terms, is something about how the City side Klopp competed against was built in the years before he faced it at its peak. That is a legitimate argument, and supporters are entitled to make it. It is not the same as saying Liverpool were denied two titles, and no ruling has said that. Klopp himself was careful. Asked about it at a Germany press conference the day after the league's announcement, he said he would wait and see, and that he was "far from preparing for a title or anything like that."

The second half of the question is about Liverpool, and it is less comfortable. Some of what was not won was lost at Anfield, not in a hearing room.

The 2020–21 collapse, covered in Part 8, happened partly because the club went into a season with three senior centre-backs and had not replaced Dejan Lovren. The 2022–23 decline, covered in Part 10, was rooted in a midfield that had aged together without being renewed in time. In April 2024, Klopp's final run-in fell away with defeats including the derby at Goodison, and a team that had been top in late January finished third on 82 points. Each of those seasons had injuries and bad luck in it. Each also had decisions in it, about recruitment and squad depth, that the club made and could have made differently.

So the honest answer has two parts. Liverpool under Klopp were good enough to win more than one league title, and the margins against City were small enough that a different break here or there would have changed the record. But the gaps between the peaks, the seasons in which Liverpool were not close, were partly of the club's own making. A side that took 97 and 92 points in two seasons also finished fourth and fifth in two others.

## How it was built

The most durable part of the Klopp era may be the method, not the team.

Michael Edwards, sporting director from late 2016, ran recruitment, supported by a research department led by Ian Graham that became one of the most respected in English football. Klopp went out of his way to credit Edwards publicly when he signed a contract extension in December 2019. The relationship between a manager who wanted particular profiles of player and a recruitment team that tested those profiles against data produced the core of the great side: Sadio Mané in 2016, Salah and Andy Robertson in 2017, Virgil van Dijk that winter, then Alisson and Fabinho in 2018.

The clearest example of the method was a sale. In January 2018 Liverpool sold Philippe Coutinho to Barcelona for a reported £142m, days after signing Van Dijk from Southampton for £75m. Alisson arrived from Roma that summer for £66.8m. Liverpool had lost their most gifted attacking player and replaced him with a centre-back and a goalkeeper, and the team improved.

Spending figures vary depending on who is counting and over what period. Different studies put Liverpool's net transfer spend across the Klopp years at somewhere between roughly £300m and £450m. Every count puts City's net spend over the same period well above it. That gap is one reason the near misses are remembered with more pride than bitterness at Anfield.

The method had misses too. Naby Keïta, signed for a fee reported at £52.75m, never became the player Liverpool paid for. The midfield rebuild arrived a year late. Edwards left in 2022, and the two sporting directors who followed, Julian Ward and Jörg Schmadtke, were both gone by early 2024. Edwards returned in a new role that March. The method worked best when its architect was in the building.

The academy is a smaller but real part of the story. Football365 counted more than 40 academy graduates handed senior debuts by Klopp, and noted that nine of them were sold for close to £100m having played 58 first-team games between them. The outstanding product was Trent Alexander-Arnold, who went from a League Cup debut against Tottenham in 2016 to one of the most creative full-backs in Europe. Curtis Jones and Caoimhín Kelleher became first-team players. In February 2024, with much of the senior squad injured, Liverpool won the League Cup final against Chelsea with several teenagers on the pitch. It was the last trophy of the era, and it is hard to imagine a more fitting one.

The football itself is the hardest thing to measure and the most widely felt. Klopp's Liverpool pressed high and in numbers, attacked through their full-backs, and scored heavily for nearly a decade. Opta notes that Liverpool scored more than 80 league goals five times under Klopp, having done it only once before in the Premier League era. The team changed shape and personnel more than once, from the front three of Mané, Roberto Firmino and Salah to the more possession-heavy side of 2021–22 and the younger, more chaotic team of 2023–24. What stayed constant was intensity, and a willingness to keep attacking when a match was turning against them.

## The club around the team

Klopp did not build stadiums or sign commercial deals, and it would be wrong to credit him with work done by the owners and the club's executives. But the club around the team changed enormously during his time, and the success on the pitch was the engine for much of it.

The new Main Stand opened in September 2016, taking Anfield's capacity to 54,074. The AXA Training Centre in Kirkby opened in November 2020, moving the first team from Melwood onto the same site as the academy. Work on the expanded Anfield Road Stand began in September 2021 and took the stadium past 61,000. Revenue rose from £298.1m in 2014–15 to £614m in 2023–24, Klopp's final season, according to the club's accounts. In 2024–25, the year after he left, Liverpool became the highest-earning club in the Premier League for the first time in Deloitte's rankings.

The relationship with supporters is harder to measure and more contested. Twice in the Klopp years, Liverpool's owners provoked open revolt. In February 2016, around 10,000 supporters walked out in the 77th minute of a home match against Sunderland in protest at a planned £77 ticket. Within days, Fenway Sports Group apologised and froze ticket prices for two seasons. In April 2021 the club signed up to the European Super League and withdrew within 48 hours after protests from fans, players and former players. John W. Henry recorded a video apology in which he said he alone was responsible.

On both occasions supporters separated the manager from the owners, and Klopp's standing in the city was not damaged. If anything, the Super League episode showed how much his authority and his players' public opposition counted. That is part of the legacy too: for much of the decade, the manager was the figure who held the club and its support together when the people above him did not.

Klopp himself reversed the story that had followed him since his first press conference. In his farewell speech, he told Anfield that people said he had turned doubters into believers, and then said it was not true: the supporters had done it.

## What Slot inherited

The clearest test of any manager's legacy is what his successor can do with it, and here the evidence is unusually sharp.

Arne Slot, appointed in May 2024, made one significant signing before his first season: Federico Chiesa, who barely played. By the time Liverpool clinched the title, every player who had started a league match for them that season had been at the club the year before. Chiesa's first league start came on 19 May, three weeks after the title was won. Slot changed the rhythm of the team, making it calmer and more patient in possession, and moved Ryan Gravenberch into the holding role. Liverpool won the title with four matches to spare, sealing it with a 5–1 win over Tottenham on 27 April 2025, and finished on 84 points, two more than Klopp's last side.

That title is the strongest single piece of evidence for Klopp's legacy. The squad he left, with Slot's adjustments, was good enough to be champions straight away. It is also the strongest evidence that Slot was a better appointment than many had assumed, and the two claims are not in conflict.

The same squad contained the problems that followed. Salah, Van Dijk and Alexander-Arnold all entered Slot's first season with a year left on their contracts. Salah and Van Dijk signed two-year extensions in April 2025. Alexander-Arnold joined Real Madrid that summer, with Liverpool receiving about £8.4m to release him a few weeks early. The club then committed a reported £446m including potential add-ons, with a British-record £125m deal for Alexander Isak and a package worth up to about £116m for Florian Wirtz.

On 3 July 2025, Diogo Jota, signed by Klopp from Wolves in 2020, was killed in a car crash in Spain alongside his brother, André Silva. He was 28.

The 2025–26 season brought no trophy and a fifth-place finish, enough for the Champions League only because England had been given an extra place. Salah and Robertson played their final games for the club against Brentford on 24 May 2026. Salah left with 257 Liverpool goals, third on the club's all-time list behind Ian Rush and Roger Hunt. Six days later Slot was dismissed, and on 4 June Andoni Iraola was appointed. Edwards left Fenway Sports Group that summer and Richard Hughes stepped down as sporting director in September.

By the autumn of 2026, therefore, very little of the structure Klopp worked within was left. Many of the leading players had gone too, but a substantial Klopp-era contingent remained, including Alisson, Van Dijk and all four midfielders signed in the summer of 2023: Alexis Mac Allister, Dominik Szoboszlai, Wataru Endo and Ryan Gravenberch. Klopp himself had spent eighteen months as Red Bull's head of global soccer before being appointed Germany's head coach in July 2026.

This is where the legacy needs the most care. It would be unfair to load the failures of 2025–26 onto a manager who had been gone for more than a year, particularly when the club had just spent more on new players than in any window in its history. But it is equally true that Klopp's great side was largely signed between 2016 and 2018, peaked together, and aged together. By the summer of 2026 the leading figures of that side, Salah, Robertson and Alexander-Arnold among them, had left. The midfield he rebuilt in 2023 stayed and won a title, but it could not by itself carry the team once the attack and the full-backs around it changed. What he left was a champion team and a set of standards, not a system that renewed itself without him and Edwards.

## The verdict

Measured against where Liverpool were in October 2015, Klopp's time was transformative. He took a club ninth in the world on revenue, without a league title for a quarter of a century, and made it one of the two best teams in Europe for half a decade, on less transfer money than the side it was competing with. He won six different major trophies with Liverpool, including the Premier League and the Champions League. His team set records for points, wins, unbeaten runs and goals that still stand at the club. He left behind a squad good enough to win the league the following year.

Measured against the standard his own team set, the record is more mixed. Two titles were lost by a single point, and the City verdict, whatever it eventually means for sanctions, does not establish financial-rule breaches in either of those seasons. Two Champions League finals were lost to Real Madrid. The seasons between the peaks were sometimes poor, and recruitment decisions made at Anfield were part of the reason.

Both things are true. The club Klopp joined was not expected to win the league. The club he left was expected to win it every year, and that change in expectation, more than any single trophy, is what he gave Liverpool. On his last day at Anfield he told the crowd the club was in a better moment than it had been for a long time. On the evidence of the following season, he was right. On the evidence of the season after that, how long such a moment lasts depends on the people who come next.
