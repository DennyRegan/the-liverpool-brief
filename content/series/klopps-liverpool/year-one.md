Two days after the 4–4 draw at Southampton, Liverpool appointed a new sporting director. Jörg Schmadtke was 59, had left Wolfsburg at the start of the year and had signed only a short-term deal, according to the Guardian. He replaced Julian Ward, who was leaving after a single year in the job. Even in Germany the appointment was something of a surprise: Schmadtke, Eurosport Germany's Marc Hlusiak said, did not have a special reputation there. He began work on 1 June, and the first signing of the summer came a week later.

Alexis Mac Allister arrived from Brighton on 8 June for £35m, a fee the Guardian reported as meeting a release clause in his contract. He was 24, had won the World Cup with Argentina six months earlier and took the No 10 shirt. "Since I won the World Cup I said that I want to win more trophies," he said, "and I think that this club will help me to do that." On 2 July Liverpool added Dominik Szoboszlai from RB Leipzig, triggering a £60m release clause that had been due to expire at midnight on the Friday. Szoboszlai, 22 and already Hungary's captain, took the No 8. Klopp called it "a signing for our present and also for our future".

This was the rebuild the previous season had demanded: two midfielders in their early twenties, quick and comfortable on the ball, each available at a fixed price. Sky Sports noted that Liverpool were attempting the biggest shake-up of Klopp's tenure in an inflated market and without Champions League revenue or a major sale to pay for it. James Milner, Naby Keïta and Alex Oxlade-Chamberlain had already gone when their contracts expired, and Roberto Firmino left for Al-Ahli. What had not been planned for was the next set of departures.

* * *

On 27 July Jordan Henderson joined Al-Ettifaq, the Saudi club managed by Steven Gerrard, the man he had succeeded as Liverpool captain in 2015. British media put the fee at about £12m. Henderson had played 492 games in 12 years. Four days later Fabinho followed him to Saudi Arabia, joining Al-Ittihad for £40m after being left out of the pre-season trips to Germany and Singapore once the bid had arrived. In a single week Liverpool lost their captain and their first-choice holding midfielder.

Klopp's public complaint was about the calendar. FIFA's listing had the Saudi Pro League window open until 20 September, almost three weeks after England's closed. "Pretty much the worst thing I think," he said, "is that the transfer window in Saudi Arabia is open three weeks longer." The Saudi federation later confirmed its deadline as 7 September, six days after England's. On 31 July, in Singapore, Virgil van Dijk was named captain and Trent Alexander-Arnold vice-captain. Van Dijk spoke of "this little transition that is going on".

Klopp's own tone that August was striking. "Sometimes you need to get a knock to realise, 'Ah, there is a problem,'" he told the Associated Press. "And I would really say we got a proper knock last year. I got one for sure, so I'm on fire." The holding midfielder he needed was meant to be the next signing. Liverpool did not lack the will to spend. In the second week of August Brighton accepted their British-record bid of £111m for Moisés Caicedo, but Caicedo let it be known that he wanted to join only Chelsea, with whom, Fabrizio Romano reported, he had agreed personal terms at the end of May. Chelsea completed the signing on 14 August for a reported £100m, potentially rising to £115m. Romeo Lavia, the alternative, also chose Chelsea, even though Southampton had eventually accepted a Liverpool offer after rejecting earlier bids. "As a normal club," Klopp said, "it's really difficult to catch up with other clubs."

The answer was Wataru Endo, signed from Stuttgart on 18 August for a fee most British outlets put at around £16m. He was 30, captain of both club and country, and nobody's idea of a marquee signing. Klopp was candid about why. "He was always on my list," he said, "just usually we don't sign players of this age group." On deadline day Ryan Gravenberch arrived from Bayern Munich for £34.2m, by Sky's figure, after a year in which he had rarely started. On the eve of the deadline Al-Ittihad had made a verbal offer for Mohamed Salah of £100m, rising to £150m with add-ons, in a call to Fenway's Mike Gordon. Liverpool rejected it, although the Saudi window remained open until 7 September. Salah stayed.

By the reported fees, the four midfielders had cost about £145m, and about £52m had come back from Henderson and Fabinho. One more decision that September, made away from Anfield, belongs in this story. When Germany sacked Hansi Flick, the German FA was reported to have approached Klopp. His agent, Marc Kosicke, closed the door publicly: Klopp "has a long-term contract with Liverpool and is not available".

* * *

The new midfield did not settle at once. On the opening day at Stamford Bridge, a 1–1 draw, Cody Gakpo played in midfield alongside the two new signings. Mac Allister was sent off against Bournemouth the following week, a card later rescinded, and played much of the autumn at the base of the midfield. Then, on 27 August at St James' Park, a team that was still being assembled found something to hold on to. Anthony Gordon put Newcastle ahead after 25 minutes, Van Dijk was sent off three minutes later, and Liverpool were still behind with ten minutes to play. Darwin Núñez, a substitute, scored in the 81st minute and again in stoppage time. "In my more than 1,000 games as a coach," Klopp said, "I never had a game like this, that is the truth."

The shape behind those results was new. With the ball, Alexander-Arnold moved into midfield from right-back, as he had done in the spring, so that the team built its attacks with three defenders and a box of four in front of them. Alex Keble of premierleague.com described it as a 3-box-3. The midfield was different in kind as well as personnel. Comparing the most-used trios, Keble found pass completion up from 83.8 per cent the year before to 89.5 per cent, and tackles and interceptions up too, though recoveries per 90 minutes were slightly down. Liverpool's total presses had also risen, from 26.1 a match to 34.3. Keble warned that six varied matches made a small sample, but the early figures pointed to a midfield that kept the ball more accurately while pressing more often.

Klopp had a name for it. Before the 3–1 win over West Ham on 24 September he called the team "Liverpool 2.0". "I was really excited about the rebuild, the new way, the new energy," he said. "We decided last year we had to change a lot." Around the same time he told reporters that Liverpool wanted "to become a new team and not the replica of the other team", and that the new group gave him "completely new energy". Liverpool had won five and drawn one of their first six league games.

The seventh, at Tottenham on 30 September, became a national story for other reasons. Curtis Jones was sent off in the first half. With the score 0–0, Luis Díaz scored, was flagged offside, and the VAR, Darren England, failed to intervene. Diogo Jota was later sent off for a second yellow card, and Joël Matip's own goal in the sixth minute of stoppage time gave Spurs a 2–1 win. The referees' body PGMOL admitted that night that "a significant human error" had occurred. Liverpool said "sporting integrity" had been undermined and that the club would explore its options. Once the audio was released, Klopp said that "the outcome should be a replay", before adding that it probably would not happen. It did not.

Two days earlier, Fenway Sports Group had ended the search for outside investment that had begun in November 2022. Dynasty Equity, a New York firm, bought a minority stake for up to $200m. Sky Sports reported that the money would reduce debt rather than provide a fund for future transfers, and that the investors would be passive. The official announcement listed pandemic-era bank debt, spending on Anfield, the AXA Training Centre and Melwood, and the summer's player acquisitions among the costs it would help cover. FSG remained in charge.

* * *

Off the pitch the autumn brought something no football club plans for. On 28 October gunmen kidnapped both of Luis Díaz's parents in Barrancas, northern Colombia. His mother was rescued within hours, but his father was held by the ELN guerrilla group. Díaz missed two matches, returned to training, and on 5 November came on as a substitute at Luton. In the fifth minute of stoppage time he headed an equaliser and lifted his shirt to show the words "Libertad para papá", freedom for Dad. "It's a wonderful moment," Klopp said, "but it doesn't change the situation." Díaz's father was released on 9 November, almost two weeks after he was taken.

In Europe Liverpool won the Europa League group with a game to spare, finishing on 12 points despite defeats in Toulouse and, with a much-changed side, against Union Saint-Gilloise in Belgium. At the Etihad on 25 November, a mistake by Alisson allowed Erling Haaland to score his 50th Premier League goal, the fastest anyone had reached the mark. Alexander-Arnold equalised ten minutes from time from Salah's pass, ending City's run of 23 consecutive home wins. By the end of the day, after Arsenal had won at Brentford, Liverpool were third, a point behind City and two behind Arsenal.

Then the injuries arrived. Andy Robertson had dislocated a shoulder on Scotland duty in October. On 3 December, in a 4–3 win over Fulham, Matip ruptured an anterior cruciate ligament. "It's an ACL, ruptured," Klopp said. "Very unfortunate, but that's it." The same afternoon Endo came off the bench to equalise with four minutes left, and Alexander-Arnold won it a minute later. Over December Endo became a regular starter, partly because Mac Allister was injured, and the player signed as a fallback began to look like an answer. Klopp called him "one of the hardest-working people I ever met". At a test event for the rebuilt Anfield Road stand that month, he was more direct with the crowd: "My god, were we lucky, eh?" Other defensive midfielders, he said, had not wanted to join Liverpool, "and then we found Endo".

December also tested the relationship between team and ground. After a goalless home draw with Manchester United on 17 December, Gary Neville said the atmosphere was the worst he had known at Anfield for that fixture. Three days later Liverpool beat West Ham 5–1 in the League Cup quarter-final, and Klopp complained about the crowd again. "If it's too much football in December, I don't know, sorry, we have to play as well," he said, "but if you are not in the right shape, give your ticket to somebody else." Spirit of Shankly said it had "no argument" with him raising the issue, but pointed to younger supporters being priced out of Anfield. Against Arsenal on 23 December the noise returned. Gabriel headed Arsenal ahead after four minutes, Salah equalised with his 151st Premier League goal and Alexander-Arnold hit the bar, but the game finished 1–1. Arsenal were top at Christmas, a point ahead of Liverpool.

The new year began with a match that captured the whole season so far. Against Newcastle at Anfield on 1 January, Salah had a penalty saved in the first half before scoring twice in the second; Jones and Gakpo also scored, and Liverpool won 4–2. They had 34 shots worth 7.27 expected goals, the highest figure recorded for a Premier League side since such data began in 2010–11. It was overwhelming and wasteful at once, and it put Liverpool top by three points. By late December they had also taken 19 points from losing positions, more than any other side in the league. A team that kept falling behind was also a team that kept coming back.

* * *

January stripped the team down. Salah left for the Africa Cup of Nations and Endo for the Asian Cup. Alexander-Arnold hyperextended a knee in a 2–0 FA Cup win at Arsenal on 7 January and was out for three weeks. Szoboszlai missed most of the month with a hamstring injury, Kostas Tsimikas had broken a collarbone, and Robertson and Matip were still out. On 18 January Salah tore a hamstring against Ghana; Liverpool's assistant manager, Pep Lijnders, said he would be out for three to four weeks. "We have many weapons so there is no-one irreplaceable here," Lijnders had said earlier in the month.

The players who filled the gaps were often young. Conor Bradley, a 20-year-old from Northern Ireland, played right-back. Jarell Quansah, an academy centre-back, had made his debut as a substitute at St James' Park in August. On 24 January Liverpool drew 1–1 at Fulham, Díaz scoring early, to reach the League Cup final 3–2 on aggregate. According to the club, their starting XI that night, with an average age of 24 years and 194 days, was Liverpool's youngest in a major semi-final since 2001. "This was not the night to shine or play a bit of fancy football," Klopp said. "This was the night to qualify for a final." Chelsea would be the opponents at Wembley on 25 February. Three days earlier Liverpool had won 4–0 at Bournemouth and gone five points clear at the top of the league.

On the morning of Friday 26 January the club released a video interview. "I will leave the club at the end of the season," Klopp said. "It is that I am, how can I say it, running out of energy." He said he could not do the job "again and again and again and again", and that he had told the club "already in November". The decision, he explained, had come out of the ordinary work of planning, when discussions about future signings and the next summer's training camp had left him thinking: "I am not sure I am here then anymore." He described the team as "a really good team with massive potential" and said it had been "super, super, super-important" to him to help bring it "back onto the rails". At a press conference that afternoon he said the decision was independent of any kind of results, that he had felt relief when he made it, and that he would manage no club, no country, "for the next year, and no other English club ever".

He was not leaving alone. Lijnders and Peter Krawietz, his assistants, would go at the end of the season, as would Vitor Matos, the elite development coach. A separate announcement confirmed that Schmadtke would leave once the January window closed. Mike Gordon said that FSG "fully respect his wishes and the reasons why he has decided the current season will be his last". That night, after City's FA Cup win at Tottenham, Pep Guardiola called Klopp the best rival he had ever had, and joked that he would now "sleep better" before playing Liverpool.

The players had been told only hours before the news broke. Van Dijk said it was hard to take and that he wanted to send Klopp off on a high. He also said he was curious to see where the club was heading. He, Salah and Alexander-Arnold all had 18 months left on their contracts.

The first match afterwards, an FA Cup fourth-round tie against Norwich on 28 January, began with "You'll Never Walk Alone", banners of thanks and Klopp's name sung around all four stands. Liverpool won 5–2, and Robertson, Alexander-Arnold and Szoboszlai all came back as substitutes. "It's emotional but I have to pull myself together," Klopp said. "I received all the messages. I'm not made of wood." Three days later Chelsea came to Anfield. Klopp kept Bradley at right-back even though Alexander-Arnold was fit again. Bradley set up Jota for the first goal, scored the second himself, made the third for Szoboszlai and was sung by the Kop before half-time. Liverpool won 4–1 and were five points clear again. It was Klopp's 200th Premier League win as Liverpool manager.

* * *

Was this a second great team? On 31 January, the honest answer was that it was a genuinely new one whose greatness had not yet been proved.

The case for it was substantial. Liverpool had rebuilt a midfield in one summer, lost their captain and their holding midfielder in the same week, missed their first two choices to replace them, and still ended January top of the league. By a count from the season's results, they had 51 points from 22 games, five more than City, who had a game in hand, and Arsenal, and they had conceded 19 goals, the fewest in the division. They were in the League Cup final, the last 16 of the Europa League and the fifth round of the FA Cup. The new midfielders were not simply replacements: on the early evidence they kept the ball more accurately than their predecessors while pressing at least as often. The fallback signing had become indispensable, and a 20-year-old right-back had just outplayed Chelsea.

The case against calling it great was also real. The same count shows that Liverpool had taken 57 points from their first 22 games in 2018–19 and 64 in 2019–20, so this team was some way short of the pace of its predecessors. In the league it had drawn all three meetings with City, Arsenal and Manchester United. The comebacks were a measure of character, but also of how often Liverpool had fallen behind. The night of 7.27 expected goals and four actual ones said something about the finishing. And the team's spine was not new at all. Alisson, Van Dijk, Alexander-Arnold and Salah, the players most central to the old team, were still the players most central to this one. The rebuild had taken place around them.

What the announcement changed is clearer. It did not change the football, at least not yet. Klopp had made his decision by November, and from December onwards, with the manager privately certain he was leaving, Liverpool were unbeaten in the league: seven wins and two draws up to the Chelsea game. What changed was the purpose of the season and the ownership of the project. "Liverpool 2.0" had been Klopp's description of his next team. From 26 January it was something he would hand to someone else, along with a coaching staff that was also leaving, a sporting director on his way out and three of its most important players entering the last 18 months of their contracts. The man who had spoken in August of being "on fire" and in September of "completely new energy" now said his energy was running out. Both statements were on the record, and Klopp offered only one explanation for the difference: the job itself, "again and again and again and again".

That left the new team with a question it would have to answer without the manager who had built it. For five months it had been Klopp's Year One. Now it was also his last.
