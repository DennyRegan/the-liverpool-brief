Liverpool went into the 2019–20 season as champions of Europe, and in the eyes of most people paid to predict such things, still only the second-best team in England.

The summer after Madrid was strikingly quiet. Liverpool's incoming business could be listed in one sentence: Sepp van den Berg from PEC Zwolle for £1.3m, Harvey Elliott from Fulham, and two goalkeepers on free transfers, Adrián from West Ham and Andy Lonergan from Middlesbrough. Going the other way, Danny Ings joined Southampton for £18m, and Simon Mignolet, Ryan Kent and Rafael Camacho were sold. Daniel Sturridge and Alberto Moreno left when their contracts ran out. The club had spent heavily a year earlier on Alisson, Fabinho and Naby Keïta. Klopp saw no reason to add for the sake of it. "We will not make signings just for the sake of signing someone," he said, in comments reported by Reuters. "That makes no sense."

The thinking was simple enough. A side that had just won the European Cup had a settled starting eleven, most of it at or near peak age. The risk was just as clear. Any injury would be absorbed by the existing squad or not at all. During the pre-season tour Klopp gave an interview to Goal in a Manhattan hotel and returned to the phrase he had used in May, on the night of the comeback against Barcelona. "We are not 'mentality giants' when we are born," he said. "Nobody is. Life makes us what we are."

Most of the pundits' predictions suggested it would not be enough. A survey of predictions in The Week found that City started the campaign as favourites, with the BBC's Phil McNulty and Sky's Charlie Nicholas among those backing them for a third consecutive title. "I am going for Manchester City to win the league again," Nicholas told Sky Sports. Phil Thompson, the former Liverpool captain, was in the minority on the same panel: "I think it is Liverpool's year." Nothing in the first official fixture changed many minds. At Wembley on 4 August, in the Community Shield, Raheem Sterling scored early for City and Joël Matip equalised in the second half. City won the shootout 5–4. Georginio Wijnaldum's was the only kick missed by either side.

* * *

Five days later the league season began, and the first problem arrived inside 40 minutes. Liverpool were 3–0 up against newly promoted Norwich at Anfield, on the way to a 4–1 win, when Alisson pulled up with a calf injury and was replaced six minutes before half-time. Klopp would not put a date on his return, saying only that the goalkeeper would miss the next few weeks. His replacement was Adrián, who had arrived on a free transfer from West Ham only days earlier.

Adrián's first big night came five days later in Istanbul, in the UEFA Super Cup against Chelsea. The referee was Stéphanie Frappart, the first woman to take charge of a UEFA men's final. Olivier Giroud gave Chelsea the lead. Sadio Mané equalised within three minutes of the restart after Roberto Firmino found him. At 1–1 Chelsea's goalkeeper Kepa Arrizabalaga made a double save, first from Mohamed Salah and then from Van Dijk's follow-up. Mané scored again in extra time after a one-two with Firmino. Then Adrián brought down Tammy Abraham, and Jorginho levelled from the penalty spot. Liverpool scored all five of their kicks in the shootout, Fabinho taking his while suffering from cramp. Adrián saved Abraham's fifth kick, and Liverpool had their first trophy of the season. It was won by a goalkeeper who had not been in the building a fortnight earlier.

The league started with a run of wins. Southampton, Arsenal, Burnley, Newcastle, Chelsea at Stamford Bridge and Sheffield United were beaten in turn. On 5 October Leicester were seen off at Anfield by a James Milner penalty five minutes into stoppage time. That made eight wins from eight, and the 2–1 margin of that game was becoming typical. City, meanwhile, were already being tested. On 31 August Aymeric Laporte damaged cartilage and the lateral meniscus in his right knee against Brighton. Asked how long he would be out, Pep Guardiola said: "Five months, six months." Now they faced most of the campaign without him. In September they lost 3–2 at Norwich. In early October they lost at home to Wolves.

The first league points Liverpool dropped came at Old Trafford on 20 October. Marcus Rashford scored for Manchester United, and Adam Lallana equalised five minutes from time from Andy Robertson's low cross. It was a minor setback, but as it turned out it came with a lasting cost. Matip was absent after that afternoon with a knee injury that kept him out until late January. With Dejan Lovren and Joe Gomez sharing the place beside Van Dijk, the defence had to carry on without its most settled partnership.

The following Sunday, 27 October, Tottenham were beaten 2–1 at Anfield. Nobody knew it then, but this was the first of 18 consecutive league wins.

* * *

The weeks either side of the international break in November did most to shape how the season was seen. At Villa Park on 2 November Liverpool trailed to a Trezeguet volley until the 87th minute. Robertson then headed in Mané's cross, and in the fourth minute of stoppage time Mané headed in Trent Alexander-Arnold's corner. Eight days later City came to Anfield. Within six minutes City were appealing for handball against Alexander-Arnold, after the ball struck him off Bernardo Silva. Michael Oliver waved the claim away, VAR agreed, and Fabinho scored at the other end in the same passage of play. Salah headed a second from Robertson's cross after 13 minutes. Mané added a third at the back post early in the second half, and Bernardo's late goal changed nothing.

Afterwards Liverpool were eight points clear, and City were fourth, nine behind. Sky Sports called it a "significant marker". Guardiola chose his words carefully: "We cannot deny how good Liverpool are but the way we played, the personality, it was good." Klopp looked to the fixture list rather than the table. "Each point we have now," he said, "we need to go through the most intense period ever for us."

He was right about the intensity. On 27 November Fabinho went off after 18 minutes of a Champions League draw with Napoli with ankle ligament damage, and the club said he would be out until the new year. Three days later Van Dijk scored twice in a 2–1 win over Brighton, a game in which Alisson was sent off. On 4 December Everton were beaten 5–2 at Anfield, with Divock Origi scoring twice. December then brought a schedule that the club had to split in two.

Liverpool had won the Champions League, so they were due in Qatar for the Club World Cup, with a semi-final on 18 December. The League Cup quarter-final at Aston Villa was fixed for the night before. The first team flew to Doha. At Villa Park a side drawn largely from the academy lost 5–0. The next evening the senior side beat Monterrey 2–1 with a winner from Firmino. In the final on 21 December, against Jorge Jesus's Flamengo, a late penalty awarded for a foul on Mané was overturned after the referee reviewed it on the pitchside monitor. In the 99th minute Mané set up Firmino for the only goal, and Liverpool won the Club World Cup for the first time. Klopp called the win "absolutely sensational". Jesus offered the night's most memorable verdict: "We lost, but only in terms of the result."

The fixture list had not finished with them. Five days after the final, Liverpool made one change, Wijnaldum for Alex Oxlade-Chamberlain, and went to Leicester, who were second. They won 4–0. Firmino scored twice. Milner scored a penalty with his first touch as a substitute, and Alexander-Arnold scored the fourth. The lead was now 13 points, with a game in hand created by the trip to Qatar. Klopp refused to treat the number as meaningful. "It was 10, 11, now 13," he said. He even joked about the headline he expected if it all went wrong: that never before in British football had a team lost a bigger lead. A 1–0 win over Wolves on 29 December closed the calendar year. Liverpool had 55 points from 19 matches, and the only points dropped had been at Old Trafford.

Business before Christmas showed how the club meant to keep this going. On 19 December Liverpool confirmed an agreement with Red Bull Salzburg for Takumi Minamino, whose release clause was reported at £7.25m. Klopp described him as "brave with the ball but also brave without the ball".

* * *

The new year brought more wins. Sheffield United were beaten 2–0, and Tottenham 1–0. On 19 January Manchester United were beaten 2–0 at Anfield: Van Dijk headed in early, and in stoppage time Alisson's long kick sent Salah clear to finish. At Molineux, Henderson scored early and Firmino got an 84th-minute winner. In the FA Cup the young players got their chance again. Curtis Jones curled in the winner against Everton on 5 January, on the day Minamino made his debut. In the replay against Shrewsbury, Jones captained the youngest side in the club's history.

By the middle of February there was little left for the title race to decide. A Mané goal at Carrow Road on 15 February put Liverpool 25 points clear. City had a game in hand. The previous day UEFA's Club Financial Control Body had banned City from European competition for two seasons and fined them €30m for breaches of its licensing and financial fair play rules. City appealed.

On 24 February West Ham were beaten 3–2 at Anfield. That was 18 straight league wins. It also left Liverpool on 79 points from a possible 81 after 27 games, which the club's own statistician later called the best start to a season by any team in Europe's top five leagues.

The decline came quickly. Henderson had injured his hamstring in the Champions League first leg in Madrid on 18 February, where Saúl Ñíguez's early goal gave Atlético a 1–0 win, and Klopp expected him to be out "for three weeks or so". Without him, Liverpool went to Watford on 29 February. Ismaïla Sarr scored twice and Troy Deeney added a third, and Liverpool lost 3–0. That ended a run of 44 league games without defeat dating back to 3 January 2019, and with it any talk of matching Arsenal's Invincibles. "There is no reason for panic," Van Dijk said. "The (unbeaten) record, and the talk of the records, is all media." Three days later Chelsea knocked Liverpool out of the FA Cup.

Football's rhythms were by then being disturbed by something else. After the Chelsea defeat Klopp was asked about the coronavirus. He replied with some impatience that he was a man in "a baseball cap and a bad shave", and said: "So, it's not important what famous people say." On 7 March Bournemouth were beaten 2–1 at Anfield. Liverpool were on 82 points from 29 matches. City had 57 from 28. Two more wins would have been enough.

Four days later Atlético came to Anfield for the second leg. Wijnaldum's header just before half-time levelled the tie, and Firmino put Liverpool ahead in the fourth minute of extra time. Minutes later an error by Adrián, still deputising for Alisson, let in Marcos Llorente to score from distance. Llorente scored again before the break in extra time, and Álvaro Morata added a fourth on the counter-attack at the end. Liverpool lost 3–2 on the night and 4–2 on aggregate. Their reign as European champions was over. Around 3,000 Atlético supporters had travelled from Spain, where estimated infections at the time were several times higher than in Britain. As it turned out, it was the last game Liverpool played in front of a crowd that season.

* * *

On 13 March the Premier League suspended its season, initially intending to return in early April. That weekend West Ham's vice-chair, Karren Brady, argued in her column in The Sun that "the only fair and reasonable thing to do is declare the whole season null and void." Others, including Brighton's chief executive Paul Barber, took the opposite view. He said that if the league were frozen, it would be unjust for Liverpool not to be awarded the title. For a few weeks, a title that had been all but won in February seemed genuinely in doubt. The doubt was not that anyone might catch Liverpool. It was that the season might never be completed.

Klopp's response, in a message to supporters published that weekend, refused to rank the title above anything else. "If it's a choice between football and the good of the wider society, it's no contest," he wrote. "Really, it isn't." Then, more plainly: "Today, football and football matches really aren't important at all." He urged people to put their health first and to look out for one another. On 19 March the suspension was extended to no earlier than 30 April, and the FA lifted the usual June deadline for finishing the season.

The club did not always find the same tone. In early April Liverpool announced that around 200 non-playing staff would be placed on the government's furlough scheme. Former players condemned it. Jamie Carragher said it had embarrassed Liverpool supporters. Within days the club reversed it. In an open letter the chief executive, Peter Moore, wrote that the club "came to the wrong conclusion" and was "truly sorry".

The Atlético match came back into the news in late May. The Sunday Times reported an analysis by Edge Health, a firm that works with NHS data. It estimated that the match could be linked to around 41 additional deaths at nearby hospitals in the weeks that followed. The figure came from a model comparing hospital trusts, and it was an estimate rather than a proven chain of cause and effect; the same work linked the Cheltenham Festival, held the same week, to 37. The match that ended Liverpool's European defence was now discussed as a public-health question.

Football's return was slow and conditional. Clubs resumed socially distanced group training on 19 May and voted for contact training on 27 May. The next day the Premier League's shareholders provisionally agreed to restart on 17 June, with every remaining game played behind closed doors and shown live on television. Liverpool would finish the job in empty stadiums or not at all.

* * *

There was already a word waiting for them. Some commentators suggested that a title completed in these conditions would carry an asterisk. Before Liverpool's first game back, Klopp took the idea and turned it round. "I had to Google the word 'asterisk'," he told reporters. If the asterisk marked the strangest and hardest season in which anyone had won a league, he was happy to have it.

The first game back, at Goodison Park on 21 June, finished 0–0. Three days later Crystal Palace were beaten 4–0 at an empty Anfield. Alexander-Arnold scored with a direct free kick, and Salah, Fabinho and Mané added the rest. Liverpool now needed City to drop points at Stamford Bridge the following evening. On 25 June Fernandinho was sent off for handball, Willian scored the penalty, and Chelsea won 2–1.

Liverpool were champions without kicking a ball. The squad had gathered to watch together at Formby Hall. With seven games left, it was the earliest any English title had been won by games remaining; the previous record was five. Because of the shutdown, it was also the latest by date.

Klopp spoke to Sky Sports that night. "This is a big moment, I have no real words," he said. "I am completely overwhelmed." He also acknowledged the world beyond the celebrations: "It's difficult out there still for a lot of people." Henderson credited the manager: "I felt from day one, he came in the door and he just changed everything." Kenny Dalglish, who had led the last Liverpool side to win the league in 1990, told BT Sport that anyone predicting a 30-year wait "would have been arrested and sectioned".

Supporters could not be inside a stadium, so many went to one anyway. Hundreds gathered outside Anfield that night, some with flares. The following evening a much larger crowd gathered at the Pier Head. A fire broke out on a balcony of the Liver Building, one man suffered serious head injuries, and Merseyside Police made 25 arrests across the two nights. The club, the police, the city council and the supporters' union Spirit of Shankly issued a joint statement calling the behaviour "wholly unacceptable". The city's mayor, Joe Anderson, put it in local terms: "Covid-19 is still a major risk and our city has already lost far too many people to the illness." One reading is that thirty years of waiting had built a need to celebrate together that empty stadiums could not meet, and in a city still in a pandemic it had consequences.

The title was won, but the season still had seven games to run, and they were uneven. On 2 July Liverpool lost 4–0 at the Etihad. They then beat Aston Villa, with Jones scoring late, and won 3–1 at Brighton, where Henderson suffered a knee injury. On 11 July Burnley drew 1–1 at Anfield, ending a run of 24 consecutive home league wins that stretched back into the previous season. Four days later Liverpool lost 2–1 at Arsenal. With 93 points and two games left, they could no longer reach City's 100-point record of 2017–18.

Some writers felt the strangeness of those weeks had diminished the prize. "The Covid-19 disruption has turned Liverpool's title into something more prosaic and functional than it deserved to be," Rob O'Connor wrote in Sporting Life on 6 July. City's own story ended in a way that cut both ways. On 13 July the Court of Arbitration for Sport set aside UEFA's ban, finding most of the alleged breaches either not established or time-barred, and cut the fine to €10m. City called it "a validation of the Club's position". It lifted a cloud that had hung over the champions' nearest rivals since February, and it meant City would be in Europe the following season after all.

On 22 July Chelsea came to Anfield for the final home game. It was a lively 5–3 win. Keïta scored early, Alexander-Arnold scored another direct free kick, and Wijnaldum, Firmino and Oxlade-Chamberlain added the others. Then came the ceremony, staged in the absence of a crowd. A stage was built on the Kop and surrounded by supporters' banners. The players' families watched from the stands, and fans gathered in the streets outside. Dalglish handed the trophy to Henderson, who was still injured, and Henderson lifted it to fireworks above an empty Kop. "Special," Klopp said. "Absolutely special."

Four days later the season ended at St James' Park. Dwight Gayle scored for Newcastle about 25 seconds in. Van Dijk, Origi and Mané replied, and Liverpool won 3–1. Van Dijk had played every minute of the league campaign. The final table showed 99 points, a club record and the second-highest total in the competition's history. Liverpool had 32 wins, equalling the record, and finished 18 points ahead of City.

* * *

So what made this the peak? The obvious answer is the results, and they were extraordinary. The 79 points from 27 games, 18 straight wins and a lead that at one stage reached 25 points are not the record of a team that was fortunate for a few weeks. They describe a team that, for six months, simply did not lose concentration. Analysts who looked closely afterwards found a more complicated picture than the table suggested. Fourteen of the 32 league wins were by a single goal. One analysis of Understat data, published that September, calculated that Liverpool had collected around 25 more points than the quality of their chances would predict, and that on expected points they would have finished behind City. StatsBomb, writing at the same time, noted that the underlying numbers had slipped from the season before, and asked: "did it really matter?" It pointed instead to a defensive structure that from September 2017 until that afternoon at Watford had not once let an opponent create two goals' worth of non-penalty expected goals in a league match.

These findings are not in conflict. The 2018–19 side may have been the better team on paper; the 2019–20 side was the more complete winner. It scored first and then shut games down. It found late goals at Villa Park, against Leicester and against West Ham. It won with Adrián in goal, without Fabinho, without Matip, and with its senior squad on another continent while the academy played a cup quarter-final. It did all this with a group that had barely changed in a year. The quiet summer looked, by February, like the reason the side worked so well together. Whether it could be repeated was a different question, and one that some analysts were already asking by September.

What the pandemic changed was not the scale of the achievement but the way it was experienced. Any title Liverpool supporters had pictured over three decades would have come with a full Anfield and a parade through the city. This one was won on a Thursday night in someone else's stadium, watched on television by players gathered at Formby Hall. It was lifted on a stage built over an empty Kop, and celebrated in the streets in ways that the club itself had to condemn. For a few weeks in March it had seemed possible that the title would not be awarded at all. Then it was won in a summer when Klopp himself had told supporters that football really was not important. That was the paradox at the heart of it. The longest wait in the club's modern history ended at the moment when supporters were least able to be there and least sure that it should matter.

On the night the title was confirmed he said it was "for you out there". The people he meant were at home, or outside the ground, but not inside it. At St James' Park on 26 July the champions finished their season in front of empty seats, exactly as they had won it.
