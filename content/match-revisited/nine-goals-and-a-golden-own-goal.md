Four days after beating Arsenal in the FA Cup final in Cardiff, Liverpool were in Dortmund for the UEFA Cup final on Wednesday 16 May 2001. They had already won the League Cup in February. One more win would complete a treble of cups that no English club had managed.

On the way Gérard Houllier's side had knocked out Roma, with Michael Owen scoring twice in Rome, then Porto, then Barcelona in the semi-final, where Gary McAllister's penalty at Anfield was the only goal of the tie.

Their opponents were Deportivo Alavés, from Vitoria in the Basque Country, playing their first season in European competition. José Manuel Esnal, known as Mané, had built a side that had beaten Inter Milan 2–0 at the San Siro and thrashed Kaiserslautern 9–2 on aggregate in the semi-final. They had Jordi Cruyff in midfield, Cosmin Contra at right wing-back and Javi Moreno up front.

Houllier picked Sander Westerveld; Markus Babbel, Stéphane Henchoz, Sami Hyypiä, Jamie Carragher; Steven Gerrard, Dietmar Hamann, Gary McAllister, Danny Murphy; Emile Heskey, Michael Owen. The Press Association reported up to 25,000 Liverpool supporters in the Westfalenstadion.

* * *

Liverpool scored early. McAllister swung in a free-kick in the fourth minute and Babbel headed it in. On 16 minutes Owen took a pass from Hamann and played the ball across for Gerrard, who ran through and scored. It was 2–0.

Alavés had started with three centre-backs. Mané took off Dan Eggen and sent on the striker Iván Alonso, switching to a back four. Mark Bradley of the Aberdeen Press and Journal noted that the change worked at once. Within four minutes of coming on, Alonso rose above Babbel to head in Contra's cross.

Alavés were playing a risky offside line, and shortly before half-time Owen made the most of it, timing his run on to Hamann's pass and going round the goalkeeper, Martín Herrera. Herrera brought him down. He escaped a red card because a defender was covering, but McAllister scored the penalty. Liverpool led 3–1 at half-time.

* * *

Within eight minutes of the restart it was 3–3. Javi Moreno headed in another cross from Contra, and three minutes later his low free-kick from about 25 yards went through Liverpool's wall. The Press and Journal put both goals down to defensive lapses, and noted that Alavés had found Liverpool's weakness in the air.

Robbie Fowler came on for Heskey, and with 17 minutes left he collected a pass from McAllister on the left, cut inside and shot into the far corner. Liverpool led 4–3, and it looked like the winner.

With a minute and 35 seconds of normal time left, by the Press and Journal's count, Cruyff headed in from a corner. It was 4–4, and the final went into extra time under the golden-goal rule: the first goal would win it.

* * *

Alavés were reduced to ten men in the first period of extra time, when Magno, who had already been booked, was sent off for a two-footed challenge on Babbel. Late in the second period of extra time Antonio Karmona was shown a second yellow card for bringing down Vladimír Šmicer. Alavés were down to nine.

McAllister took the free-kick from the right. Delfí Geli, the Alavés left-back, rose to meet it and headed it past his own goalkeeper. Liverpool had won 5–4, with three minutes of extra time remaining.

McAllister, 36, was named man of the match. The Press and Journal counted his contribution: he had set up the first, fourth and fifth goals and scored the third.

* * *

Houllier would not let the players celebrate yet. Liverpool still needed to win at Charlton on the Saturday to finish third and reach the Champions League. He said the champagne would stay on ice until Sunday, and told the Press Association that he had always said he was not in favour of the golden goal, because it changed the way teams approached extra time.

Mané said it had possibly been the smallest team in the competition that made the final great. Alan Hansen called it the best final ever.

On the Saturday Liverpool won 4–0 at The Valley. The next day the squad paraded three trophies on an open-top bus through the city.
