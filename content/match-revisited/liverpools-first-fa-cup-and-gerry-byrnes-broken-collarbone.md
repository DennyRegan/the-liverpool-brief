Before 1965 the FA Cup was the one major trophy Liverpool had never won. They had reached the final twice and lost both times, to Burnley in 1914 and to Arsenal in 1950. Bill Shankly had arrived in December 1959, the year Liverpool lost an FA Cup tie to non-league Worcester City, and by 1964 he had taken the club from the Second Division to the league title.

The road to Wembley had been unremarkable. Liverpool beat West Bromwich Albion away, needed a replay to get past Stockport County of the Fourth Division and another to beat Leicester City, won at Bolton, and beat Chelsea 2–0 in the semi-final at Villa Park.

Leeds United, back in the First Division only that season after promotion, were in their first final. They had nearly won the league as well, finishing level on points with Manchester United and losing out on goal average. Don Revie's side were hard, organised and young. Their captain, Bobby Collins, was the Footballer of the Year, and Billy Bremner, Jack Charlton and Norman Hunter were already among their best players. On the left wing was Albert Johanneson, who that afternoon became the first black player to appear in an FA Cup final.

Liverpool went to Wembley without Gordon Milne, the right-half, who had injured a knee. Geoff Strong took his place. The side was Tommy Lawrence; Chris Lawler, Gerry Byrne, Geoff Strong, Ron Yeats, Willie Stevenson; Ian Callaghan, Roger Hunt, Ian St John, Tommy Smith, Peter Thompson.

* * *

Early in the game Byrne, the left-back, went into a challenge with Collins and went down. Bob Paisley, then the club's trainer, ran on. By his later account his first instinct was to call for a stretcher, but Byrne spoke first and asked him not to tell anyone. The collarbone was broken.

There were no substitutes in the FA Cup in 1965. If Byrne went off, Liverpool would play the rest of the final with ten men. He stayed on. The Nottingham Guardian reported on the Monday that it was only when the players were interviewed after the match that anyone learned how badly he was hurt.

What followed was not a classic. It rained almost throughout, according to Horace Yates in the Liverpool Daily Post, and the national papers were unkind about the football. Bill Irwin in the Belfast News-Letter headlined his report "the most boring English Cup final", and the Leicester Mercury's Laurie Simpkin wrote that he had heard spectators outside Wembley wishing the coaches would take their "method" football to China.

The criticism was aimed mostly at Leeds. Simpkin thought they seldom gave a forward a chance and took the bulk of the blame for the caution. Liverpool, he wrote, always played the better and more polished football, with Stevenson the man of the match.

Irwin described how Shankly's side had set up. Smith, wearing the inside-left's number, played alongside Yeats between the full-backs, with Strong and Stevenson linking the play. In his view Leeds had no answer to it, yet the possession rarely turned into chances. Irwin counted one second-half move of 13 passes without a Leeds player touching the ball, and still Gary Sprake was seldom troubled. Twice the goalkeeper kept Leeds in the Cup with saves from Thompson.

After 90 minutes it was 0–0. It was the first FA Cup final to go to extra time since 1947.

* * *

Three minutes into extra time Liverpool scored, and Byrne made the goal. He got down the left and crossed, and Hunt headed in from close range. Hunt later paid tribute to Byrne and to Stevenson, and said he was glad Byrne had squared the ball to him.

Leeds came back. Eleven minutes into extra time, the Daily Mirror reported, Bremner half-volleyed the equaliser and quietened the chanting Liverpool supporters. For five tense minutes, the Mirror wrote, the Cup looked to be slipping away.

The break between the two periods of extra time gave Liverpool a breather. Then, with nine minutes left, Callaghan got past the Leeds line on the right and crossed, and St John threw himself at the ball and headed it in.

Liverpool held on. Yeats went up to collect the Cup from the Queen.

* * *

Nobody argued with the result. In the Daily Post, Yates called it the noisiest and most one-sided Cup final Wembley had known, and wrote that on all sides that night the toast was "at last". The Liverpool Daily Post's leader column gave the crown to Byrne, for a performance unimpaired by what it called the secret agony of a broken collarbone. It also spared a thought for Milne, cheated by injury of his share of the glory.

Shankly said his team had won because they had matured in the hothouse of European Cup football and gone to Wembley confident in themselves. Later he would say that Byrne should have had all the medals to himself.

Revie, the Leeds manager, was gracious. As his team left King's Cross for home he said he had no second thoughts about Wembley: "The better team won."

Liverpool brought the Cup home on the Sunday. The Birmingham Post put the crowd along the route from Lime Street station to the Town Hall at between 200,000 and 250,000. Supporters broke through the barriers, and ambulance crews dealt with more than 500 casualties, from fainting cases to broken limbs. Herbert Balmer, Liverpool's acting chief constable, said it was the biggest crowd he had ever set eyes on, but a good-humoured one. Yeats held the Cup up from the Town Hall balcony.

Three days after Wembley, Liverpool played Inter Milan at Anfield in the first leg of the European Cup semi-final. Before kick-off the two injured men, Byrne with his arm in a sling and Milne, walked round the pitch with the FA Cup. Liverpool won 3–1.
