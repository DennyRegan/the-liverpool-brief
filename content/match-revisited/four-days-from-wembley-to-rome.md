On Saturday 21 May 1977 Liverpool lost at Wembley. They were already league champions, having won the title with two games to spare, and they finished the season a point ahead of Manchester City. Manchester United beat them 2–1 in the FA Cup final, with all three goals coming in a five-minute spell early in the second half. With that went the chance to become the first English club to win the league, the FA Cup and the European Cup in the same season.

The European Cup was still to be played, and there were four days to recover before the final against Borussia Mönchengladbach in Rome.

It was the trophy the club had been chasing longest. Bill Shankly first took Liverpool into the competition in 1964–65, when they reached the semi-final and went out to Inter Milan. By LFChistory's account his two later attempts both ended in the second round. Bob Paisley, Shankly's long-serving assistant and now his successor, had won the league and the UEFA Cup in 1975–76. This was his first attempt at the European Cup. The Guardian, reporting from Rome, would call the result the reward for 13 years of persistence on the Continent.

* * *

Paisley's first job was to put right a decision he had got wrong. He had named Ian Callaghan only as a substitute at Wembley, and he later admitted to John Keith of the Liverpool Daily Post that it had been a mistake. In training two days after the defeat, Callaghan was told he would start in Rome. He came in for David Johnson. At 35 he was the one player left from Liverpool's first European game, in 1964, and the final would be his 83rd European match, which the Daily Post's Horace Yates reported as an English record.

The defence included a player who might not have been there at all. Tommy Smith had fallen out of favour and was expected to leave in the summer, according to LFChistory, but Phil Thompson was injured, so Smith kept his place alongside Emlyn Hughes, the captain.

One player was missing for good. John Toshack had been out for seven weeks with injury, and the Liverpool Echo reported that Paisley judged him not fit enough even for the bench. He was the player the Germans feared most, the Echo noted, because his heading had beaten them in the 1973 UEFA Cup final. He watched in a shirt and tie.

Kevin Keegan was playing his last match for the club. He had announced at the start of the season that it would be his last at Anfield. Phil Neal later recalled Paisley warning Keegan that Berti Vogts would mark him more closely than he had ever been marked, and telling him to "run over every blade of grass".

Liverpool lined up: Ray Clemence; Phil Neal, Joey Jones, Tommy Smith, Ray Kennedy, Emlyn Hughes; Kevin Keegan, Jimmy Case, Steve Heighway, Ian Callaghan, Terry McDermott.

The opposition was strong. Mönchengladbach were West German champions, coached by Udo Lattek and captained by Vogts. Rainer Bonhof, Uli Stielike and Jupp Heynckes were also Germany internationals, and their Danish forward Allan Simonsen would beat Keegan to the European Footballer of the Year award for 1977. They had won the UEFA Cup in 1975, and they knew Liverpool, who had beaten them 3–2 on aggregate in that 1973 final.

Neither side had reached a European Cup final before, and neither had found the road easy. Gladbach had won three of their four ties by a single goal, beating Austria Vienna, Torino, Club Brugge and Dynamo Kyiv. Liverpool had beaten Crusaders and Trabzonspor. In the quarter-final they lost 1–0 in Saint-Étienne and won the second leg 3–1 at Anfield, a match Keith described as one of the ground's great nights. They then beat FC Zürich 6–1 on aggregate in the semi-final.

* * *

The players had been told to expect about 8,000 Liverpool supporters in the Stadio Olimpico. LFChistory's account says they found more than 25,000, and the Guardian estimated about 24,000. Many had set off almost as soon as the Wembley final ended. On the day of the match, newspapers reported that 5,000 of them travelling by train faced a wait of up to 11 hours in Rome after the final whistle. Neal remembered fans who had sold nearly everything they owned to get there. One banner celebrated Joey Jones, the Welsh left-back, for having eaten the French in the quarter-final and rolled the Swiss in the semi, and declared that he was now "munching Gladbach".

According to the Guardian, the Liverpool end sang throughout and drowned out the German supporters, leaving the Italian spectators feeling almost like intruders in their own stadium.

Gladbach had the first chance, and it nearly changed everything. After 22 minutes, Yates wrote, Bonhof beat Clemence's dive and the ball came back off the post. Frank McGhee of the Daily Record called it the one moment when Liverpool were lucky. After that the first half belonged to them, and Yates described the Germans as jumpy as kangaroos.

The opening goal came just before the half-hour. Callaghan took the ball off Bonhof near halfway, and Yates noted that the German did not bother to chase. Callaghan's pass went to Heighway, who cut inside and put the ball in front of McDermott, already running at full speed. McDermott beat Wolfgang Kneib as the goalkeeper came out. Paisley had signed him from Newcastle for £170,000. Liverpool led 1–0 at half-time.

* * *

Lattek said afterwards that his team's counter-attacking plan had only come into operation in the second half. It brought them an equaliser within six minutes. Case played a careless ball back towards his own goal, the Daily Record reported, without realising that Neal had moved forward. Simonsen intercepted it and drove a shot from the corner of the box into the top corner. Neal himself later called it a mix-up between him and Case.

For the next few minutes Gladbach were the better side. Yates counted two chances, the better of them when Clemence failed to cut out a cross and Simonsen had the goal at his mercy, but did not score. Lattek, too, remembered Simonsen nearly scoring a second and thought then that his team had a chance. Just short of the hour Liverpool claimed for a penalty when Vogts brought Keegan down, and the referee, Robert Wurtz, waved play on.

Then came the save. Simonsen sent Stielike clear with a pass across the field, and Clemence raced out to meet him. Yates wrote that the goalkeeper beat the shot away with his knees, and the Press Association report said the save ended the German revival. It came around the 63rd minute.

Two minutes later Liverpool were ahead again. Hans Klinkhammer conceded a corner, Heighway took it, and Smith came forward and headed it in. The Guardian noted it was his first goal of the season and judged it "probably the last and certainly the most important" of his Anfield career. Several papers reported that he was retiring. They were wrong about that: by LFChistory's account he turned down the chance to manage Wigan Athletic and stayed at Liverpool.

* * *

The rest of the night was about Keegan and Vogts. Yates wrote that Vogts stuck to Keegan as closely as the shirt on his back, and coped only because the referee took a generous view of his pushes in the back. The Guardian described Keegan's speed and sudden changes of direction wearing Vogts down on a warm, heavy evening. Keith, who was also in the press box, considered it probably the best performance of Keegan's six years at Liverpool. McDermott later compared him to "an electric eel".

Seven minutes from time Keegan ran through and seemed certain to score, according to the Press Association, until Vogts lunged at him inside the area. Wurtz had no choice but to give the penalty. Neal, Liverpool's penalty taker, sent Kneib the wrong way, and it was 3–1. Neal later remembered a newspaper photograph of Callaghan praying as he stepped up.

David Fairclough warmed up as if he might come on, Yates noted, but Paisley left the eleven alone. The Liverpool supporters were already setting off rockets.

The Guardian's verdict on Gladbach was harsh. It named Bonhof as their outstanding player, the one who looked capable of winning the final on his own, but said the defending around him had become increasingly elementary. The West German paper Die Welt reached a similar view the next day, according to agency reports: Gladbach's cover had been weaker than usual and only Simonsen had been sharp in attack. In Rome, Il Messaggero said Keegan had won his duel with Vogts.

At the final whistle, the Guardian reported, the Liverpool supporters greeted the result with jubilation rather than relief, as if they had expected it all along. Liverpool had become the second English club, and the third British club, to win the European Cup.

* * *

Lattek was gracious. He said Liverpool had been too strong and experienced for his side over the last 20 minutes, and he defended his decision to leave Vogts on Keegan all night. He had "nobody else who could play against him", he said, and Keegan had shown what an excellent player he was.

Paisley was matter-of-fact. Liverpool had been trying to pace the game, he said, and were punished for their first mistake. It was their 17th match in six weeks. Keegan confirmed that this had been his last game for Liverpool, and Paisley said talks with Hamburg would follow. Shankly, in the stand, thought the win had been easier than expected.

There was no champagne in the dressing room. Keith described the trophy sitting on a bench while the players drank warm Coca-Cola from paper cups. Paisley drank orange juice, and told Keith he was "just drinking in the occasion".

The supporters found the team hotel and, according to LFChistory, helped themselves to the buffet. Others danced on the steps of the Trevi Fountain until daybreak. The Echo's Steve Oldfield reported that one of them, a nurse from Rainhill Hospital, jumped in, and a Roman policeman helped her out with great politeness. The trouble that had been feared never came, and Oldfield wrote that the Romans would remember the fans singing "Liverpool are magic" through the streets. The next afternoon the trophy was paraded through Liverpool.

Keegan left for Hamburg, and Liverpool paid Celtic a British record fee for Kenny Dalglish to replace him. A year later Dalglish scored the only goal against Club Brugge at Wembley, and Liverpool kept the European Cup. Seven years after that they went back to Rome and won it again, against Roma on penalties.

The Echo printed a photograph of Paisley with Callaghan that night, and the caption gave Paisley's words to him: "It's taken 13 years, Cally, but we've done it."
