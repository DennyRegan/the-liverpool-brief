# Klopp's Liverpool, Part 6: Kings of Europe

*Working title.*

---

On Sunday 12 May 2019, Liverpool beat Wolverhampton Wanderers 2–0 at Anfield and finished second. Sadio Mané scored both goals, but Manchester City won 4–1 at Brighton and took the title by a single point, 98 to 97. Liverpool had lost one league match all season, and they would not be champions. Less than three weeks later, in Madrid, they were champions of Europe.

Told in that order, it sounds like a story of injury and response: a team denied the prize it wanted most, turning the hurt towards the one still available. The reporting of the time does not bear that out, or not on its own. The two campaigns did not run one after the other. They ran side by side, and, looking back, for most of the spring the European one seemed the likelier to end in disappointment. By the time the league was lost, Liverpool were already in the final. They had got there with a comeback that had only two precedents at the semi-final stage of the European Cup, on the night after City had gone back above them in the table. What was left after the Wolves game was a single match, three weeks away.

How they got there involves a team reorganised around control, a home crowd that carried it through dismal away form, a squad worn thin by injury, and a handful of moments that could easily have gone the other way.

Liverpool began the 2018–19 Champions League as beaten finalists. In Kyiv the previous May, Real Madrid had won 3–1. Mohamed Salah went off in the 31st minute after landing on his left shoulder in a challenge with Sergio Ramos, and two errors by Loris Karius decided the night. The summer that followed belongs to the previous chapter. Fabinho was signed less than 48 hours after the final, and Naby Keïta, Xherdan Shaqiri and Alisson Becker followed, Alisson from Roma for what was then a world-record fee for a goalkeeper. The link between Kyiv and the new goalkeeper was drawn at the time: in August 2018 Sports Illustrated called Karius's night a "necessary evil" that had forced Liverpool to solve their goalkeeping problem.

Group C put Liverpool with Paris Saint-Germain, Napoli and Red Star Belgrade. The opening night at Anfield on 18 September set a pattern for the autumn. Roberto Firmino had picked up an eye injury in the win at Tottenham three days earlier and started on the bench. Daniel Sturridge headed in Andy Robertson's cross after half an hour, and James Milner scored a penalty six minutes later after Georginio Wijnaldum was brought down. PSG came back through Thomas Meunier before half-time and Kylian Mbappé seven minutes from the end. Firmino, on as a substitute, won it 3–2 in stoppage time.

Away from home the story was different. In Naples on 3 October, Lorenzo Insigne scored in the 90th minute from José Callejón's low cross. Keïta had gone off injured after 18 minutes and Liverpool did not manage a single shot on target. "No attempts on target," Klopp said afterwards, "I don't remember when that happened last time." Red Star were beaten 4–0 at Anfield, but in Belgrade on 6 November the result went the other way. Klopp rested Firmino and started Sturridge, who missed from six yards in the 17th minute. Milan Pavkov then scored twice in seven minutes, Liverpool hit the woodwork twice and lost 2–0. Asked what had gone wrong, Klopp replied that he only had 10 fingers. He added that Liverpool had lost their mojo in the game, not in general.

Paris, on 28 November, made it three away defeats out of three. Juan Bernat scored after 13 minutes, and Neymar added a second. Milner's penalty just before half-time was Liverpool's only shot on target. Six Liverpool players were booked, and Klopp complained that, "with the number of interruptions in the game, it was just not cool," adding that his side looked "like butchers when you look at the yellow cards".

The arithmetic for the final group game was unforgiving. Liverpool went into it third, on six points. The club's own permutations said they needed to beat Napoli 1–0 or by two clear goals; any draw or defeat would send them into the Europa League. On 11 December Salah scored after 34 minutes, squeezing his shot past David Ospina from a narrow angle. Van Dijk was booked for a challenge on Dries Mertens, which would rule him out of Liverpool's next European game. Then, in stoppage time, the ball dropped to Napoli's substitute Arkadiusz Milik in the six-yard box and Alisson blocked his shot from point-blank range. A goal there would have put Liverpool out. "And the save Ali made, I have no words for that," Klopp said. "That was, of course, a lifesaver tonight." Van Dijk said of the goalkeeper: "He gives confidence to everyone."

Liverpool and Napoli finished on nine points each, level on goal difference and on head-to-head record. Liverpool went through in second place, behind PSG, because they had scored more goals across the group, nine to Napoli's seven. Eight of those nine had come at Anfield. The only away goal was Milner's penalty in Paris.

The round of 16 drew Bayern Munich. When the first leg came round on 19 February, Liverpool's season had changed shape. City had beaten them 2–1 on 3 January, their first league defeat, and draws against Leicester and at West Ham had followed within six days of each other. The defence for Bayern was improvised. Van Dijk was suspended because of the booking against Napoli, and Joe Gomez and Dejan Lovren were injured. The Associated Press described Joel Matip as Liverpool's fourth-choice centre-back, and Fabinho played alongside him. Bayern were missing Thomas Müller, banned for both legs after a red card at Ajax.

The match finished 0–0; ITV said it had "failed to live up to expectations". "It is not a game we will remember in 30 years," Klopp said, and then: "I'm not over the moon but I'm completely OK with the game." "I can't remember that many clubs that have not conceded at Anfield," said Bayern's coach, Niko Kovač. Agence France-Presse noted that Liverpool had lost their last five Champions League games away from Anfield, the Kyiv final included, which made the trip to Munich look daunting. Under the away-goals rule, any scoring draw in Munich would put Bayern through.

The weeks between the legs did little for confidence. Liverpool drew 0–0 at Goodison Park on 3 March.

In Munich on 13 March, Jordan Henderson went off with an ankle injury after 13 minutes and Fabinho replaced him. After 26 minutes van Dijk hit a long ball forward, Mané controlled it, turned away from Rafinha and lifted it over Manuel Neuer, who had come out of his goal. Matip turned Serge Gnabry's low cross into his own net in the 39th minute, so the scores were level at half-time, a result that would have taken Bayern through. In the 69th minute van Dijk rose above Mats Hummels and Javi Martínez to head Milner's corner in at the near post. Six minutes from the end, Mané headed in after Salah crossed with the outside of his boot. Liverpool won 3–1.

UEFA's figures put the result in context. Liverpool had lost their previous four away matches in the competition and were only the second side in five seasons to win a Champions League game at the Allianz Arena. The result recalled 1981, when Bob Paisley's side had also gone through against Bayern after a goalless first leg at Anfield. Klopp said Liverpool had a lot to improve and a lot to learn, but that they were back, where everyone thought the club belonged. The Bayern camp did not dispute it. "It was a deserved defeat today," Kovač said.

In hindsight, Munich showed something that had been building all season. In 2017–18, as Goal's Neil Jones set out that February, Liverpool had scored 47 goals in 15 Champions League games and won nothing. Pep Lijnders had left for NEC Nijmegen in January 2018, returned that summer and was named assistant manager in January 2019. In an interview with The Times, reported in April by the fan site Empire of the Kop, he explained the change: Liverpool had to work on a better movement of the ball and the positioning of their midfielders. He was blunter still: for him, the first way of defending was how a team moved the ball when it had it. Fabinho, who had not started a league game until October, was in Lijnders' words "like a lighthouse" at the base of midfield.

The defensive figures matched what Lijnders described. In Europe, as the campaign would turn out, they conceded two goals in six home matches. Looking back, this was a less spectacular side than the one that had reached Kyiv, and a much harder one to beat at Anfield.

The quarter-final was against Porto, the side Liverpool had beaten 5–0 away a year earlier. Robertson's booking in Munich ruled him out of the first leg on 9 April, so Milner played at left-back. Lovren made his first start in more than three months. Henderson, now fit, played further forward than usual, with Fabinho behind him. Keïta scored after five minutes with a deflected shot from outside the area, and Firmino tapped in a second from Trent Alexander-Arnold's cross. Klopp was pleased with his captain's new role, and apologised for not having used him there sooner.

In the second leg on 17 April Divock Origi was a surprise starter, with Firmino on the bench. Gomez, who had broken his leg in December, was back among the substitutes. Mané's opener was initially flagged offside, but a VAR check showed he was level. "I was thinking it was offside, to be honest," he said afterwards. Salah made it 2–0 on the night, Éder Militão headed one back for Porto, and Firmino and van Dijk added late goals. Liverpool won 4–1 and 6–1 on aggregate. The semi-final would be against Barcelona and Lionel Messi. Asked about Messi that night, Klopp said: "Thank God I don't have to think about that tonight."

Messi had at least 45 goals that season.

At the Camp Nou on 1 May, Firmino was fit only for the bench because of a muscle injury. Gomez played at right-back, his first start since breaking his leg at Burnley, and Alexander-Arnold was among the substitutes. After 24 minutes Keïta went off with a groin injury. Two minutes later Luis Suárez got between Matip and van Dijk to finish Jordi Alba's cross at the near post. Liverpool created plenty. In the 75th minute Suárez hit the woodwork and Messi got to the rebound before van Dijk. Seven minutes later Messi scored from a free-kick about 30 yards out, his 600th goal for the club. Late on, Barcelona cleared a Firmino shot off the line and Salah hit the post from the rebound. In stoppage time Ousmane Dembélé missed a chance that would have made it 4–0.

The verdicts at the time agreed on two points: the score flattered Barcelona, who faced 15 Liverpool shots, and the tie was very probably over. For NBC Sports, Nicholas Mendola wrote that the scoreline "flattered to deceive the hosts" but that Liverpool were now well behind. Klopp made both points himself. "I really think it was the best away game we've played in the Champions League," he said, and then: "It's OK to lose away in the Champions League as long as you score a goal, we didn't and that's a problem." He added: "These boys never give up. I love that. But there's no party in that dressing room."

The squad got thinner over the weekend. At Newcastle on 4 May Salah was hurt in a collision with the goalkeeper Martin Dubravka and carried off on a stretcher. Origi, on as a substitute, headed in Shaqiri's free-kick four minutes from time for a 3–2 win that put Liverpool two points clear. On the Monday night City beat Leicester 1–0 with a long-range goal from Vincent Kompany and went back to the top. At his press conference that day, Klopp confirmed Salah would miss the second leg: "It is a concussion and that means he will not even be allowed to play." Of the second leg itself, he said that if it did not work, Liverpool would "fail in the most beautiful way". Alexander-Arnold said: "It is a slim possibility but the possibility is still there".

On 7 May Liverpool started without Salah, Firmino and Keïta, with Shaqiri and Origi in the attack and Alexander-Arnold back at right-back. After seven minutes Alba's weak header let Mané find Henderson, whose shot was saved by ter Stegen, and Origi scored the rebound. Liverpool needed three more goals and could not afford to concede one. For the rest of the first half, Alisson was the more important goalkeeper. He saved from Messi, parried Philippe Coutinho's shot and blocked Alba at close range. Robertson went off injured at half-time. Wijnaldum, who had been left out of the starting XI, came on in his place.

Wijnaldum changed the tie within two minutes. In the 54th minute he drove Alexander-Arnold's cross low past ter Stegen. In the 56th he headed in Shaqiri's cross, and the aggregate score was 3–3. In the 79th minute Liverpool won a corner at the Kop end while Barcelona's defenders were still organising themselves. Alexander-Arnold made as if to walk away, then turned back and played it to the unmarked Origi, who shot into the top corner. "I think it was just instinctive," Alexander-Arnold told BT Sport. Klopp admitted he had seen the ball in the net without knowing who had taken the corner; it had been too quick for him.

The ball reached Alexander-Arnold quickly because of a ball boy. Oakley Cannonier, an academy player, was by the corner flag. According to Cannonier, the instruction had come down from the first-team staff to the academy. In Cannonier's words, "the assistant manager told our coach Carl that the ball needs to be in rapid for the intensity of the game." Contemporary reports named the coach as Carl Lancaster, who organised the ball boys at the Kirkby academy.

Liverpool held the 4–0 to the end. They were only the third side in the history of the European Cup to overturn a three-goal deficit from the first leg of a semi-final, after Panathinaikos in 1971 and Barcelona themselves in 1986. For Barcelona it was the second year running that a three-goal first-leg lead had been lost, after the defeat in Rome the season before. The Catalan and Spanish papers were harsh. Sport called it "The greatest humiliation in history", and Mundo Deportivo called the fourth goal "the corner of shame". Ernesto Valverde said: "It is a terrible result for our fans and for ourselves but credit is due to Liverpool."

His players, Klopp said, were "really mentality giants", though the version he gave on live television was less printable. Henderson had played on with a knock to the knee, and the plan now was to "collect the bones and go again against Wolves." UEFA's correspondent described players and supporters in tears at the final whistle. Salah, who had watched from the stands, wore a T-shirt that read "Never Give Up".

Tottenham joined them in the final the next evening, through Lucas Moura's second-half hat-trick in Amsterdam. Spurs had reached the semi-final at City's expense, and Harry Kane had damaged his ankle ligaments in the first leg of that quarter-final.

The league ended on 12 May with the Wolves game and second place. Before the final Klopp was asked about the title race, and he did not describe it as a failure. I have found no player or coach who said at the time that losing the league drove the European campaign, and Liverpool had already reached the final while the title was still undecided. If the near miss mattered in Madrid, that is interpretation. What the record does show is that a squad that had played 38 league matches at a very high level got almost three weeks to recover before one game.

The club used the time carefully. In Marbella the coaches worked on tactics against a side set up to play like Tottenham. A week before the final, in heat similar to what Madrid would bring, Liverpool played a friendly behind closed doors against Benfica B. Record reported that Lijnders had arranged it after consulting a former colleague at Porto; a later Goal account said Julian Ward had set it up with Benfica's technical director, Pedro Marques. Liverpool won, and Mané scored early. Firmino, who had missed the last three matches with a thigh strain, trained with the squad in Marbella from 21 May. Keïta was still recovering from his groin injury and was ruled out of the final.

Klopp's record was in the papers too: six finals lost in a row, three with Borussia Dortmund and three with Liverpool. He did not accept that it was a curse. "I am probably at the moment a world-record holder, in the last seven years at least, in winning semi-finals," he said. And: "If I would be the reason for losing six finals in a row then everybody needs to worry really." He was clear on how the team had changed since Kyiv: "we are a completely different side to last year."

The final was played at the Estadio Metropolitano on Saturday 1 June, in heat of around 30 degrees. Mauricio Pochettino started Kane, who had not played for nearly two months, and left Lucas Moura on the bench. Firmino was fit to start for Liverpool.

The decisive moment came almost at once. Roughly 22 seconds in, Mané's effort from close range hit Moussa Sissoko on the shoulder and then the arm, and the referee, Damir Skomina, gave a penalty. A brief VAR check confirmed it, though Sky Sports said the decision "seemed to split opinion". Salah scored. By the club's timing the goal came 1 minute 48 seconds into the match, then the second-fastest in a Champions League final.

Not much happened after that. Tottenham had 61 per cent of the ball over the match but no shot on target until the 73rd minute. Origi replaced Firmino after 58 minutes. In the last half-hour Alisson pushed away a shot from Son Heung-min and held one from Moura. In the 87th minute Matip found Origi, who shot low with his left foot into the far corner. In stoppage time Alisson made a further save from Kane. Liverpool won 2–0.

Contemporary reporting was frank about how poor the match had been. Sky Sports called it "an otherwise turgid affair". Pochettino defended picking Kane: "This wasn't a drama, it was a decision." UEFA named van Dijk man of the match. Sky's own man of the match was Alisson, the goalkeeper bought after Kyiv. According to the club, no cards were shown, a first for a Champions League final.

It was Liverpool's sixth European Cup and their first since Istanbul in 2005. It was Klopp's first trophy as Liverpool manager, and it ended his run of lost finals. Henderson became the fifth Liverpool captain to lift the trophy, after Emlyn Hughes, Phil Thompson, Graeme Souness and Steven Gerrard. Klopp's main feeling was not triumph. "I feel mostly relief to be honest, for my family actually," he said. He said that going on holiday with a silver medal the last six times had not felt too cool. Henderson spoke about the defeats that had come before: "It was nice to know we have come back from so many disappointments and finally get over the line and win a big trophy."

So how did a team that had just lost the league win the European Cup three weeks later? Partly because the question's order of events misleads. Liverpool got through Munich and Barcelona while still fighting for the title; the only match left once the league was gone was the final. Beyond that, the evidence points to Anfield, where Liverpool won five and drew one of six European matches and conceded twice, while away from home they lost three group games and scored once, from a penalty. It points to the change Lijnders set out in the spring, a side built to keep the ball and defend through it, anchored by a goalkeeper and a centre-back who had not been at the club 18 months earlier: van Dijk scored in both away knockout wins, and without Alisson's save from Milik there would have been no knockout rounds at all. And it points to depth under strain. Keïta was injured twice in the competition, Salah and Firmino missed the biggest night, and the four goals against Barcelona came from Origi, a fringe forward, and Wijnaldum, a half-time substitute. Origi then settled the final.

Other readings came later. The part played by Liverpool's analytics department in building the side has since been told at length in books and long-form journalism, but little of it was public in 2019, when the improvement was credited to the coaches and the signings. The contemporary verdicts, too, shifted with time: the away defeats treated as a crisis, the 0–0 with Bayern as a let-down, the 3–0 in Barcelona as very nearly the end. Each looked different by June, not because the run had been inevitable but because Liverpool had come through each by a small margin.

On Sunday 2 June the squad flew into Liverpool John Lennon Airport. At about four in the afternoon an open-top bus marked "Champions of Europe" set off from Allerton Maze, north along Queens Drive and in through West Derby Road and Islington towards the waterfront at Blundell Street. Red flares were lit on top of the Royal Liver Building. ITV reported that Liverpool City Council estimated the crowd at 750,000; the BBC attributed the figure to the police. A month earlier, after the 3–0 in Barcelona, few would have expected the day.
