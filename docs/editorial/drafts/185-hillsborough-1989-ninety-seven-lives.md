# Hillsborough: ninety-seven lives unlawfully lost

Ninety-seven Liverpool supporters were unlawfully killed as a result of the Hillsborough disaster on 15 April 1989. They had gone to Sheffield Wednesday’s ground for an FA Cup semi-final against Nottingham Forest. Hundreds more were injured, and survivors and bereaved families faced consequences that continued for decades.

The fatal crush developed in the central pens of the Leppings Lane terrace. With dangerous congestion outside, police opened exit Gate C to admit supporters. The tunnel opposite led towards pens that were already full; it remained open, without incoming spectators being directed elsewhere. Fences divided the terrace and restricted escape on to the pitch.

Supporters helped people out of the pens and carried casualties on advertising boards. Doctors and nurses attending the match joined attempts to resuscitate the injured. The emergency response lacked effective leadership, co-ordination and equipment, while recognition of the scale of the disaster was delayed.

Lord Justice Taylor’s inquiry identified the principal cause as a failure of police control. Yet families and survivors still had to campaign against an account that shifted responsibility on to supporters, and for the disclosure of evidence held by public bodies.

The Hillsborough Independent Panel’s report, published on 12 September 2012, exposed the alteration of police statements to remove or change criticism of the force. Its examination of medical evidence also undermined the assumption behind the original inquests’ 3.15pm cut-off: that everyone who died had already suffered irreversible fatal injury. The Panel found that a better emergency response had the potential to save lives, while making clear that it could not establish whether a particular individual would have survived.

The original accidental-death verdicts were quashed in 2012. On 26 April 2016, the jury at the fresh inquests concluded that the 96 people whose deaths it had considered were unlawfully killed. It expressly rejected any suggestion that supporters’ behaviour caused, contributed to, or might have contributed to the dangerous situation at the turnstiles.

The jury identified failures in police planning, command and control, alongside defects in the stadium and shortcomings in its safety oversight and preparation. Errors in both the police and ambulance response had contributed to the loss of life.

Those findings were distinct from the outcomes of criminal prosecutions. Match commander David Duckenfield was acquitted of gross negligence manslaughter at his retrial in 2019. Graham Mackrell, Sheffield Wednesday’s former secretary, was convicted of a health and safety offence.

Andrew Devine died on 27 July 2021, aged 55, after living for 32 years with the injuries he sustained at Hillsborough. The following day, Liverpool’s coroner concluded that he had been unlawfully killed, making him the 97th fatality. His family remembered a much-loved son, brother and uncle, supported through those years by relatives and dedicated carers. A lifelong Liverpool supporter, he had continued to attend Anfield when he could.

In December 2025, the Independent Office for Police Conduct published the findings of its investigation and Operation Resolve. It confirmed fundamental failures in South Yorkshire Police’s planning, policing and emergency response, and the force’s subsequent attempt to control evidence and deflect blame. Its conclusions supported the findings that families and survivors had fought for: supporters had not caused or contributed to the disaster.
