*Expensive attackers bought from Germany averaged nearly 30 per cent fewer goals and assists per 90 in their first Premier League campaign. A smaller group bought from Spain, Italy and France dropped by almost as much.*

Florian Wirtz's first season in England fell well short of the expectations that came with his transfer. Signed from Bayer Leverkusen in the summer of 2025 for £100m, potentially rising to £116.5m, he had scored 10 and set up 12 in his final Bundesliga season, according to [Opta](https://theanalyst.com/players/135/florian-wirtz/career). In 33 Premier League appearances for Liverpool, he managed five goals and three assists.

His first direct contribution, an assist at Tottenham on 20 December, came in his 16th league appearance. His first goal followed a week later against Wolves. On [Understat's figures](https://understat.com/player/8397), which supply the rates throughout this piece, his goals plus assists per 90 minutes fell from 0.84 to 0.30.

Seasons like that tend to get filed under a familiar heading: the Bundesliga tax. The argument runs that German football is more open, that defences outside the top few clubs leave more room, and that an attacker's numbers there flatter him as a result. Buy the numbers, the theory goes, and England will take its cut.

That is a claim that can be tested.

## The drop is real

This analysis identified 33 attacking players who moved directly from the Bundesliga to the Premier League between summer 2010 and summer 2026, in deals reported at £25m or more. Two arrived in 2026 and have not completed a campaign, leaving 31 for the main comparison. For each player, the study compared goals plus assists per 90 minutes in their last full Bundesliga season with the same figure for their first Premier League campaign.

Twenty-four of the 31 recorded a lower rate. The average fell from 0.74 to 0.53 per 90, a drop of 29 per cent. Leave out Donyell Malen, whose fee [Sky Sports](https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/13288125/donyell-malen-transfer-aston-villa-complete-signing-of-dutch-forward-from-borussia-dortmund) reported above £25m and [the BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/sport/football/articles/cn4m2n1zl8jo) below it, and the drop is 31.5 per cent.

The falls are not marginal. Timo Werner scored 28 league goals in his last season at RB Leipzig and six in his first at Chelsea; his rate fell from 1.15 to 0.48. Sébastien Haller went from 0.99 to 0.32 at West Ham, and Jadon Sancho from 0.82 to 0.29 at Manchester United. Henrikh Mkhitaryan's output fell by almost two-thirds in his first season at Old Trafford.

The exceptions matter, too. Any theory about Bundesliga attackers has to account for Erling Haaland. Haaland's rate rose slightly, from 1.36 to 1.41, in a first season of 36 league goals. Roberto Firmino and Pierre-Emerick Aubameyang also improved. Seven improvements in 31 do not overturn the pattern. In this sample, expensive Bundesliga attackers usually recorded fewer goals and assists per 90 in their first English campaign, often by a considerable margin.

## More than a finishing slump

One tempting explanation is that this is mostly about finishing: players who caught fire in their last German season simply cooled down. Two checks show the decline extends beyond the recorded goals and assists.

The first uses expected goals and expected assists (xG and xA), which estimate the quality of the chances a player gets and creates, rather than simply counting the goals that result. Across the 30 players for whom Understat has these figures, actual output fell by 28 per cent and expected output by 22 per cent. In absolute per-90 terms, the expected-output fall was roughly three-quarters the size of the actual-output fall. That does not tell us what caused the decline. It does show that their recorded chance involvement fell as well. They shot less often, too: 2.8 times per 90 minutes in Germany, 2.4 in England.

The second check asks whether clubs were buying on the strength of an unusually productive season. Using each player's last two Bundesliga seasons as the starting point instead of one barely changes the picture. For the 28 players with two comparable seasons, the average starting rate is almost identical either way. The decline survives using a two-season baseline.

## Recovery is patchy

If the first season is the problem, perhaps the second is the answer. Some careers suggest so. Leroy Sané's rate at Manchester City fell from 0.54 to 0.40 in his first season, then more than doubled to 0.93 in his second, when he scored 10 and set up 15. Leon Bailey managed one goal in 723 league minutes in his first season at Aston Villa, and ten goals and nine assists in his third.

The group as a whole does not follow them. Take the 19 players who arrived between 2010 and 2023 and have had three seasons since. Their average rate was 0.78 in Germany, 0.52 in their first English season, 0.59 in their second and 0.53 in their third. The second-season improvement is modest, and most of it disappears in the third.

Some had left the league by then. Only 15 of the 19 were still playing Premier League football in their third season: Werner had returned to Leipzig, Haller had joined Ajax halfway through his second. Yet the shape holds even among the 14 who played in all three seasons, so it is not simply a matter of who stayed.

## A different job

Some of the drop may reflect a change of role. Liverpool's own recent history offers examples in both directions.

Firmino was listed by Understat as an attacking midfielder for most of his final season at Hoffenheim. In his first Liverpool season more than half of his minutes came in forward positions, and his rate rose from 0.52 to 0.76.

Dominik Szoboszlai went the other way. More than half of his minutes in his last Leipzig season were logged in attacking midfield; in his first season at Liverpool, after a £60m move, almost all of them were in central midfield. His rate more than halved, from 0.48 to 0.21. The following season he was listed mainly as an attacking midfielder again, and it doubled, to 0.43, with six goals and six assists. Position labels do not show exactly what a player was asked to do, and the timing could be coincidence. Even so, a lower attacking return from a midfielder moved deeper need not mean he has become a worse player.

Wirtz's role is harder to pin down. Understat logged most of his first-season minutes in attacking midfield, with spells on the left and up front. A [Premier League tactical analysis in January](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4567464/talking-tactics-analysis-on-florian-wirtz-improving-form-at-liverpool) described Arne Slot using him as a No 10, on the left, on the right and as a false nine against Arsenal. That variety is plausibly part of his story, but the data cannot say how large a part.

## Is it German?

This is the question the slogan skips. Moving to a new country, a new club and often a new role could be expected to cost a player something wherever he came from. If the Bundesliga is unusual, expensive arrivals from other leagues should hold up better.

That is not what this benchmark shows. A comparison group of 12 expensive attacking signings from La Liga, Serie A and Ligue 1, four from each, fell from 0.83 to 0.60 per 90, a drop of 28 per cent. Eight of the 12 declined. Romelu Lukaku's rate fell from 1.09 at Inter to 0.46 in his second spell at Chelsea; Nicolas Pépé's from 0.89 at Lille to 0.45 at Arsenal. Mohamed Salah, like Haaland, was an exception whose rate rose.

Restricting the Bundesliga sample to the same arrival period, 2015 to 2023, the 18 Bundesliga signings fell by an average of 0.25 goals plus assists per 90 and the comparison group by 0.23.

*Figure 3. The mean decline is 0.248 goal contributions per 90 for 18 Bundesliga entrants in 2015–2023 and 0.234 for the 12-player benchmark. The similar figures cannot establish that the leagues are equivalent.*

The comparison has limits. Twelve players is a small group, chosen to meet the same rules rather than drawn at random, so it cannot show that the leagues are equivalent, or rule out a meaningful additional German effect. The decline appears in both selected groups. This evidence does not establish a special German penalty.

## The team they join matters

The clearest pattern in the data has little to do with Germany. Of the 29 Bundesliga arrivals with comparable team figures, 20 joined a side that scored fewer league goals per game than the one they had left. Their output fell by 0.27 per 90 on average; the other nine fell by 0.07. The comparison group splits the same way: the nine who joined lower-scoring sides fell sharply, while the three who joined higher-scoring ones held steady.

This cannot establish cause and effect. A player's own goals count towards his team's total, and league conditions can affect both at once. But it points to something the slogan obscures. Many of these players left Leverkusen, Dortmund or Leipzig, clubs used to finishing near the top of the Bundesliga, and joined sides that scored less. Leverkusen scored 2.12 league goals a game in Wirtz's last season there; Liverpool scored 1.66 in his first.

## Back to Wirtz

Wirtz's first-season attacking return was poor by his own standards, and the drop was steeper than the fall in the group's mean rate: 65 per cent, compared with 29 per cent. But his expected numbers fell by 31 per cent, from 0.70 to 0.48. The gap between what he was involved in creating and what came of it was one of the widest in the group. That is not proof of bad luck, and it does not explain why his chance involvement fell as much as it did. His underlying numbers nevertheless present a less bleak picture than his goals and assists.

The first six games of 2026/27 offer little more to go on. Under [Andoni Iraola](https://www.liverpoolfc.com/teams/mens-team/andoni-iraola), who replaced Slot in the summer, Wirtz has made six league appearances, all starts coded by Understat as attacking midfield, with no goals and one assist. Six games are not a trend in either direction.

None of this can tell anyone what Wirtz will become. Sané and Bailey are reminders that a first season can mislead; Werner and Haller that sometimes it does not. What the record does show is that a first-season drop is common in this Bundesliga sample, and appears in the smaller benchmark from other leagues too. Calling it a Bundesliga tax explains less than it seems to.

## How this was worked out

The starting list covered reported direct moves from Bundesliga clubs to Premier League clubs between summer 2010 and summer 2026 involving centre-forwards, wingers, second strikers, attacking midfielders and central midfielders with a substantial attacking role in Germany. The reported package, including add-ons, had to reach £25m. Defenders and primarily deeper midfielders, including Thiago Alcântara, were excluded, as were players who passed through another club first, such as Alexander Isak. Players were not chosen for success or failure; Son Heung-min, for example, falls below the fee threshold.

The main measure is goals plus assists per 90 minutes of league football, from [Understat](https://understat.com) ([Opta](https://theanalyst.com/players/6432/edin-dzeko/career) for Edin Džeko's older seasons). Each player's last complete Bundesliga season was compared with his first Premier League campaign; for mid-season arrivals, only the part of the season after the move was counted. Averages give every player equal weight. Expected-goals figures come from Understat's model throughout and are never mixed with another provider's.

The comparison group of 12 players from Spain, Italy and France was chosen to meet the same fee, position and direct-move rules. It is a benchmark, not a complete list. The reported percentage declines compare the groups' mean rates; they are not the average of each player's percentage change. There is no minimum-minutes threshold in the main sample. All figures run to 16 October 2026.
