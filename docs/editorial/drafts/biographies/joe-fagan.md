# Joe Fagan: a lifetime behind Liverpool’s treble

Joe Fagan was 62 when he became Liverpool manager. Within a year, his team had won the League Cup, the league championship and the European Cup. No English club had previously collected three major trophies in one season. The achievement came late in his working life, after years spent helping other managers build and maintain successful Liverpool teams.

His two seasons in charge are only the final part of a much longer Anfield career. Fagan joined the coaching staff in 1958, before Bill Shankly arrived, and remained through the transformation of Liverpool from a Second Division club into European champions. When he succeeded Bob Paisley in July 1983, he was taking responsibility for an institution whose daily work he had helped shape for 25 years.

That experience matters as much to his biography as the speed with which he won his own honours. Fagan knew Liverpool from the reserves upwards. He had taught players, worked alongside successive managers and helped younger coaches find their way. His promotion brought a familiar figure to the head of a familiar staff, but the responsibility for selection and results was now his.

## The coach before the manager

Born in Liverpool on 12 March 1921, Fagan spent the main part of his playing career with Manchester City, whom he joined in 1938. The war interrupted his progress. He served in the Royal Navy and played wartime football before establishing himself in City's side after the resumption of the Football League.

His move into management began away from the professional game's leading clubs. In 1951 he left City to become player-manager of Nelson. A spell at Bradford Park Avenue followed, and he subsequently worked as a trainer at Rochdale under Harry Catterick. These were years of playing and teaching before his long association with Liverpool began.

Fagan arrived at Anfield in 1958 and worked with the reserves. Shankly's appointment the following year placed him within the coaching group associated with the Boot Room. Liverpool's success would make that room famous, but Fagan's contribution was practical: preparing footballers, discussing their progress and helping turn the manager's requirements into work on the training ground.

He remained as Shankly rebuilt the club, and stayed when Paisley took over in 1974. In 1979 he became Paisley's assistant. By then his experience extended across different generations of players and the changing demands of domestic and European football. The eventual succession was a continuation of that work, rather than the arrival of a manager learning an unfamiliar club.

The recollections of colleagues help explain how he exercised authority. Roy Evans remembered that Fagan would tell a player privately why he had been left out before announcing the team. Graeme Souness described a usually quiet speaker whose anger, when it came, carried considerable force. Both accounts came from men who had worked closely with him, and neither suggests that his kindness amounted to an absence of standards.

Evans's account also shows a coach attentive to the consequences of a decision. A player could lose his place without being left to guess the reason or feel excluded from the group. Fagan's manner allowed personal warmth and professional judgement to coexist. For players accustomed to him over many seasons, the move from assistant to manager did not require a new relationship to be invented.

## Three trophies in one season

Fagan inherited considerable strength. Souness captained the side, Kenny Dalglish and Ian Rush formed its central attacking partnership, and experienced defenders included Phil Neal, Alan Kennedy and Alan Hansen. Yet maintaining results through a change of manager was an achievement in itself. Liverpool's players had to sustain their standards across a season that eventually required 67 matches.

The first trophy came against Everton. The League Cup final at Wembley on 25 March 1984 finished goalless after extra time. Three days later, at Maine Road, Souness scored the only goal of the replay. Liverpool had won the competition for a fourth successive season, extending a run begun under Paisley.

Fagan publicly acknowledged the contribution of his predecessor after the victory. He had been part of the staff that developed these players, but he recognised whose managerial era had assembled the side. That acknowledgement did not diminish his own work: he had carried the team through two closely contested matches to secure the first part of a season that was still being fought on several fronts.

The championship was confirmed by a 0–0 draw at Notts County on 12 May. It was Liverpool's third league title in succession. Rush's scoring gave the campaign a remarkable individual dimension: he finished with 47 goals in all competitions, the most productive season of his Liverpool career. His partnership with Dalglish combined finishing with creation, while Rush's work without the ball helped defend from the front.

Liverpool's European campaign demanded success away from Anfield as well as at home. Rush scored the only goal of the tie against Athletic Bilbao at San Mamés. He also scored in the away victories over Benfica and Dinamo Bucharest as Liverpool advanced to meet Roma in the final. These results carried Fagan's team into a match played on their opponents' own ground.

At the Stadio Olimpico on 30 May, Neal gave Liverpool the lead and Roberto Pruzzo equalised before half-time. Neither side scored again during the second half or extra time. The European Cup would be decided by a penalty shoot-out for the first time.

Liverpool won it 4–2, Alan Kennedy converting the decisive kick. Fagan had completed a treble in his first season as manager, and Liverpool had won their fourth European Cup. The conclusion is often remembered through Bruce Grobbelaar's movements on the goal-line and Kennedy's penalty; the route there also required the side to withstand Roma for two hours in Rome.

Fagan's use of an established team was particularly striking across that campaign. Grobbelaar, Hansen, Kennedy and Sammy Lee appeared in all 67 matches. The continuity gave Liverpool an experienced core through the different competitions. It also placed substantial demands on a small group: success required the same players to keep producing after one trophy had already been secured.

## Change, retirement and Heysel

The following season brought a major alteration. Souness left for Sampdoria after the European Cup victory. Fagan had to replace the captain's presence in midfield as well as his playing contribution. Jan Mølby arrived from Ajax in the summer of 1984, while Paul Walsh and Kevin MacDonald were other additions to the squad. John Wark, signed during the previous season, became Liverpool's leading scorer in 1984–85.

Mølby's later importance should not be read backwards into his first campaign. He made 24 appearances and scored once that season; his outstanding contribution to the league and FA Cup double came under Dalglish in 1985–86. Fagan had recruited a player who would become important to Liverpool's future, but his own final season involved a less settled transition.

An injury kept Rush out until October, and Liverpool began the league campaign poorly. They recovered to finish second to Everton and again reached the European Cup final. Fagan had already decided to retire before that final against Juventus at Heysel in Brussels on 29 May 1985.

Thirty-nine people died before the match. Liverpool supporters charged towards Juventus supporters, and people were crushed against or fell through a retaining wall. The match subsequently went ahead, with Juventus winning 1–0 through a Michel Platini penalty. The deaths, and the suffering of those injured and bereaved, take precedence over the sporting result.

Heysel was the last match of Fagan's managerial career. It is inaccurate to present his retirement as a decision made because of the disaster: the decision preceded it. Equally, the events cannot be reduced to an unhappy ending to a football story. They require recognition in their own right whenever his final season is recalled.

Fagan continued to visit Melwood after retirement. He died on 30 June 2001, aged 80. The official club record gives him 131 matches as manager and three major honours, all won in 1983–84.

Those figures identify an exceptional season within a short managerial tenure. His wider place at Liverpool also rests on the years before it: a coach entrusted with developing players, an assistant who helped sustain Paisley's teams, and a colleague whose influence was remembered by men who later led the club themselves. The treble made his managerial record distinctive. The long service behind it explains why Liverpool entrusted him with the job.
