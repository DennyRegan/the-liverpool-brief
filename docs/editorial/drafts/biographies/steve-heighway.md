# Steve Heighway: from the wing to the academy

Steve Heighway helped Liverpool win their first European Cup by supplying the passes for two of their three goals in Rome. He had already scored in two FA Cup finals, won league championships and helped establish the side Bill Shankly built in the early 1970s. Years later, he returned to oversee the development of players who would carry Liverpool into another era.

His playing career and his work with young footballers each deserve a substantial place in the club’s history. Together, they account for an association that extended far beyond his 475 competitive appearances.

Born in Dublin on 25 November 1947, Heighway reached professional football by an unusual route. He completed an economics degree and played for non-league Skelmersdale United before joining Liverpool. Having initially signed as an amateur in February 1970, he became a professional on 19 May, aged 22.

Liverpool were rebuilding under Shankly, and Heighway soon became part of that process. An injury to Bobby Graham helped open a place in the side. By the end of his first season, he had made 49 competitive appearances and scored at Wembley.

His direct running gave Liverpool a way of advancing quickly from the left. He could beat a defender and cross, but he was also prepared to continue towards goal himself. An early demonstration came against Everton at Anfield on 21 November 1970. Liverpool were 2–0 down when Heighway drove in from the left and scored from a tight angle. He then crossed for John Toshack to head the equaliser. Chris Lawler completed a 3–2 victory.

Those two contributions showed the choices Heighway presented to a defence. A full-back had to protect against the delivery into the middle while also dealing with a runner willing to take the ball into the penalty area. Against Everton, both routes produced goals.

The 1971 FA Cup final brought another example, followed by a defeat. Early in extra time against Arsenal, Heighway cut in from the left and beat Bob Wilson at his near post. Liverpool led, but Eddie Kelly equalised and Charlie George scored the winner. Heighway’s first season as a professional ended with a Wembley goal and a runners-up medal.

The major honours followed. Liverpool won the league championship and UEFA Cup in 1973, then returned to Wembley to face Newcastle United in the 1974 FA Cup final.

Heighway’s involvement in the 3–0 victory went beyond scoring the second goal. With Liverpool already ahead through Kevin Keegan, he ran through to finish low into the far corner. Late in the match, he passed into the right side of the penalty area for Tommy Smith, whose ball across goal gave Keegan his second. Heighway had contributed both a direct finish and a pass that opened the way for a teammate to create another goal.

Shankly resigned that summer. Under Bob Paisley, Heighway remained an important part of the team as Liverpool won another league and UEFA Cup double in 1976. He started both legs of the European final against Club Brugge. His role also developed: although associated above all with the left wing, he increasingly operated in central areas.

That ability to create from different positions mattered in the 1977 European Cup final. Against Borussia Mönchengladbach in Rome, Heighway played Terry McDermott through for Liverpool’s opening goal. After Allan Simonsen equalised, his corner supplied Smith’s header to restore the lead. Phil Neal’s penalty completed the 3–1 win.

Liverpool had lost the FA Cup final to Manchester United just days earlier. They finished the season as league champions and European champions, with Heighway directly involved in the goals that twice put them ahead in the European final. It was a precise illustration of his value: Liverpool could use his passing in open play and his delivery from a dead ball.

He collected a second European Cup winners’ medal in 1978, coming on as a substitute against Club Brugge at Wembley. His place was becoming less secure, though. He made 28 league appearances in the championship season of 1978–79, followed by nine in 1979–80 and six in 1980–81.

He left Liverpool in 1981 with 76 goals from his 475 competitive matches; the league figures were 50 goals in 331 appearances. His subsequent playing career included Minnesota Kicks and Philadelphia Fever in the United States, where he also developed his coaching career.

In 1989, Kenny Dalglish brought him back to Liverpool as youth development officer. Across 18 years leading the club’s youth development work, Heighway helped prepare Robbie Fowler, Jamie Carragher, Michael Owen and Steven Gerrard for senior football.

The job grew from a small youth department at Melwood into responsibility for the purpose-built Kirkby academy, opened in 1999. It involved organising a development programme as well as coaching. Heighway argued that the new base allowed better planning, rather than youth sessions being interrupted whenever the reserves needed players. Under-18 coach John Owens and reserve manager Gary Ablett liaised over those calls, illustrating that the route towards senior football depended on several coaches, not the academy director alone.

His approach joined high expectations to personal engagement. Describing it in 2016, he used the sequence “engage, confront, challenge and inspire”: talk to a youngster, address what needed improving, then give him the belief to improve it. Discipline and respect had to begin early, but toughness without the ability to inspire was insufficient. In his earlier defence of the academy, he also resisted the overplaying of boys across school, Sunday-league and club football, and the disruption to schooling and family life that speculative recruitment could cause.

Preparing boys for the next stage could mean exposing them to it before they were ready to play there. Hughie McAuley recalled Heighway bringing promising younger players, including Gerrard, into the experience of tournaments and cup finals alongside older boys. The purpose was to make representing Liverpool on those occasions less unfamiliar when their own opportunity arrived. McAuley and Dave Shannon shared the longer work of developing that generation.

Liverpool’s first FA Youth Cup success came in 1996, with Owen and Carragher in the team that beat West Ham United. Further victories followed in 2006 and 2007. After the latter final against Manchester United, Heighway described leaving the players to decide their penalty takers between themselves. Their successful shoot-out was, in his account, a demonstration of the group’s maturity rather than simply another trophy for its coach.

Cup success did not settle the harder question of first-team progression. Heighway distinguished the exceptional talent immediately recognisable in Gerrard from players such as Carragher who grew through senior opportunities. He maintained that the academy could prepare a player but could not establish his ultimate level without those chances. Benítez was himself calling for more competitive reserve football for 18-to-21-year-olds. Their concerns overlapped, even though their preferred arrangements did not.

Heighway left in 2007, saying after the Youth Cup final that it was his decision and that Liverpool had not asked him to go. Looking back in 2016, he described a disagreement over youth development, believing that Benítez’s recruitment of teenagers for Melwood reduced the Kirkby players’ opportunities. He also said he had needed a break from running the academy. Those were his explanations of the departure, not proof that one man alone accounted for the difficulties of producing first-team players.

He returned to the academy in 2015, initially part-time and then as a full-time consultant. This was a different responsibility: under Alex Inglethorpe, he concentrated particularly on the 15- and 16-year-olds, offering individual and group work without managing a team or the academy. He could also advise parents and coaches. Barry Lewtas later described how exchanging ideas with Heighway helped his own development as U16s coach. Liverpool announced Heighway’s retirement from coaching in December 2022, shortly after his 75th birthday.

Heighway’s Liverpool record has two distinct measures: the goals and chances he created for Shankly and Paisley, and the players he later helped prepare for the first team. His place in the club’s history rests on substantial work in both.
