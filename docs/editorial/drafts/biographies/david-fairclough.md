# David Fairclough

David Fairclough scored 55 goals for Liverpool, started a European Cup final and helped win a league championship in his first season. Yet the name attached to him was always “Supersub”. His most famous goals explain why it stuck. The rest of his career explains why he found it frustrating.

Across 154 competitive appearances, Fairclough started 92 times and came off the bench on 62 occasions. He could change a match in a few minutes, but he also wanted the chance to shape one from the beginning. That tension runs through his Liverpool years: decisive contributions, periods of regular football and repeated returns to the margins.

Born in Liverpool on 5 January 1957, Fairclough joined the club in 1972 and became a professional in January 1974. Bill Shankly was still manager when he signed, but Bob Paisley gave him his first-team opportunity. On 1 November 1975, he started a 1–0 league victory at Middlesbrough. Three days later, he came on against Real Sociedad at Anfield and scored his first senior Liverpool goal in a 6–0 UEFA Cup win.

His speed and direct running offered Paisley another way to attack. Kevin Keegan and John Toshack were established ahead of him, although Toshack’s injury problems created openings. Fairclough finished the 1975–76 league campaign with seven goals from 14 appearances, nine of them as a substitute. For a teenager entering a side chasing the championship, it was a substantial contribution.

The clearest demonstration came against Everton at Anfield that spring. Introduced from the bench, Fairclough carried the ball from around halfway, worked his way through the defence and beat Dai Davies to settle the derby late on. Liverpool won 1–0. The official club profile records it as Fairclough’s own favourite goal: a run and finish which showed how much he could create for himself.

His place in the European Cup story followed on 16 March 1977. Liverpool were leading Saint-Étienne 2–1 in the quarter-final second leg, but the French champions still held the advantage on away goals. With time running out, Paisley replaced Toshack with Fairclough.

In the 84th minute, Ray Kennedy sent the ball beyond the visiting defence. Fairclough controlled it, held off the challenge and finished low past Ivan Ćurković. Liverpool’s third goal secured their place in the semi-finals. The move depended on the qualities which made him dangerous: acceleration, control at speed and the composure to finish with a defender close by.

The celebrations did not lead to a starting place in either of Liverpool’s major finals that May. Fairclough had started both FA Cup semi-final matches against Everton, but was left out of the final team against Manchester United. He then remained an unused substitute when Liverpool won their first European Cup in Rome. In a later LFChistory interview, he described his disappointment at those decisions. The player who had supplied one of the campaign’s essential goals watched its conclusion from the bench.

There was more sustained involvement in 1977–78. Fairclough made 42 appearances, including 34 starts, and scored 15 goals across all competitions. His scoring included a goal in each leg of the European Super Cup against Hamburg. This was his busiest Liverpool season, with responsibilities extending well beyond late appearances.

Even then, he continued to provide the sort of intervention which reinforced his nickname. Against Derby County in the League Cup in October 1977, he entered with about 20 minutes remaining and the score 0–0. Within five minutes he had scored twice. Liverpool went through to the next round.

At Wembley in May 1978, however, Fairclough was in the starting side as Liverpool retained the European Cup. Kenny Dalglish scored the only goal against Club Brugge. After his exclusion from the previous year’s final, Fairclough had taken part in the occasion itself.

That season did not establish him permanently. Steve Heighway’s selection ahead of him restricted his opportunities in 1978–79, and an injury against Everton in March further interrupted his progress. Fairclough still produced 13 goals in 26 appearances the following season, but injuries increasingly affected his availability. He made no competitive first-team appearances for Liverpool in 1981–82, as Ian Rush established himself in attack.

Looking back, Fairclough believed he could have contributed more. “I had a lot more to offer,” he told LFChistory. That was his assessment of an unfulfilled opportunity, rather than proof that a different selection policy would have brought a different career. His record nevertheless gives the complaint substance: 37 of his Liverpool goals came in matches he started, compared with 18 as a substitute.

Fairclough left for the Swiss club Luzern in 1983. His subsequent career included Norwich City, Oldham Athletic, Beveren, Tranmere Rovers and Wigan Athletic. His Liverpool totals remained 154 appearances and 55 goals, including 34 in 98 league games.

Saint-Étienne will always dominate the recollection of Fairclough at Liverpool. It deserves its place. But the derby winner, the goals in the 1976 title campaign and his participation in the 1978 European Cup final belong alongside it. Together, they give a fuller account of a forward whose opportunities were uneven and whose contribution was considerable.
