# Chris Lawler: the quiet full-back who kept finding the goal

Six minutes from the end of the Merseyside derby at Anfield on 21 November 1970, Chris Lawler scored the goal that completed Liverpool’s recovery from 2–0 down. Alec Lindsay’s free-kick was headed on by John Toshack, and the right-back arrived to strike the winner past Andy Rankin. Steve Heighway and Toshack had already brought Liverpool level. Lawler’s intervention made the final score 3–2 and gave an unforgettable expression to a talent that was easy to overlook until he appeared in the penalty area.

He finished his Liverpool career with 61 goals in 549 appearances, an exceptional return for a defender whose scoring did not depend on a regular allocation of penalties or direct free-kicks. The goals were the most surprising feature of his football, but they were built upon a much less surprising virtue: he was repeatedly available, repeatedly selected and trusted to do his defensive work. The player nicknamed the Silent Knight helped Liverpool through both the establishment of Bill Shankly’s first successful side and the rebuilding that produced another.

Lawler was a Liverpool boy, born on 20 October 1943, who came through local schoolboy football and joined the club’s junior ranks in 1959. His upbringing in a family of Liverpool supporters made the choice of club a personal one as well as a sporting opportunity. He had already represented England Schoolboys, so there was recognised ability to develop. Joining Liverpool did not immediately mean spending every day on the training pitch, however. The groundstaff routine included work around the stadium and training with the amateurs in the evenings.

In his later account, Shankly changed that arrangement after asking about his day. Morning training with the professionals replaced a routine in which football had been fitted around the work. The change gave the youngster regular access to experienced players and their advice. It was a practical example of Shankly treating development as part of rebuilding the club. Lawler signed professional forms on his seventeenth birthday in October 1960, but the first-team opportunity would take considerably longer to arrive.

Initially a centre-half, he faced an obvious obstacle in Ron Yeats. Liverpool’s captain occupied the position in a team moving upwards, and Lawler’s openings came when Yeats was unavailable. His debut on 20 March 1963 was a 2–2 draw with West Bromwich Albion at Anfield. He wore the number five, while Gerry Byrne and Ronnie Moran were the full-backs. A contemporary assessment praised his direction of headers and his passing. Those qualities would remain important after Liverpool found him a different place in the defence.

He made six league appearances in that first season and another six during the championship campaign of 1963–64. Liverpool were winning, but Lawler still lacked the regular role he wanted. He recalled asking Shankly for a transfer because he could see little future behind Yeats. The manager’s solution was positional: Byrne moved to the left as Moran’s career approached its end, and Lawler was tried at right-back. A player who had been waiting for somebody else’s absence now had a route into the normal team.

The adjustment transformed his career. Right-back gave him defensive responsibility and opportunities to arrive from an unexpected direction when Liverpool needed a goal. His first came in a 5–1 win at Burnley on 5 December 1964, the last of Liverpool’s five at Turf Moor. The lineup showed the new arrangement plainly: Lawler on the right, Byrne on the left, Yeats still at the centre of the defence. The positional change had made room for another talented player without requiring Liverpool to abandon their captain.

By the end of 1964–65, Lawler was established. On 1 May he started the FA Cup final against Leeds United, the match that ended Liverpool’s long wait to win the competition. After a goalless ninety minutes, Roger Hunt put them ahead in extra time. Billy Bremner equalised, before Ian St John headed the winner from Ian Callaghan’s cross. Lawler was one of the eleven who completed that 2–1 victory. For the local youngster who had struggled to find a permanent place, the first Liverpool FA Cup was an achievement experienced from the pitch.

The league championship followed in 1965–66, with Lawler a regular member of the side. European progress brought a second major final, against Borussia Dortmund at Hampden Park on 5 May 1966. Roger Hunt cancelled out Sigfried Held’s opener, but Stan Libuda scored in extra time and Dortmund won 2–1. Lawler was again in Liverpool’s starting defence. The distinction between domestic success and the European disappointment matters: Liverpool’s first generation of winners was learning how difficult it could be to complete its ambitions beyond England.

Lawler’s defensive method depended upon anticipation. He read a developing attack and preferred to solve a problem before a desperate challenge became necessary. Ron Yeats later remembered the quality of his passing and heading, as well as that ability to read the game. On the right, familiarity with Tommy Smith and Callaghan helped the system work. Lawler explained that one of them would cover when he went forward. The goals were therefore part of an understood relationship between teammates, rather than permission for a defender to forget the area he had left.

His movement towards goal was often unobtrusive. Ian Callaghan remembered him appearing in places opponents did not expect. Lawler himself said he went forward when Liverpool needed him, especially at corners when they were behind. The value lay in choosing the moment and handling the chance once he reached it. He did not need to be an attacking player by position to possess an attacker’s judgement. His best scoring season was 1969–70, when he contributed eleven goals across the competitions.

Availability made that talent useful over time. From October 1965 to April 1971, Lawler played 316 consecutive matches. Across the eight league seasons from 1965–66 to 1972–73, he missed only three games. These figures describe more than physical durability. A manager could keep selecting the same player because he remained able to meet the side’s requirements. Lawler gave Shankly continuity on the right while changes elsewhere gradually became unavoidable. The quietness of his manner did not make the responsibility small.

Liverpool went seven years between the league titles of 1966 and 1973. Shankly’s first great side aged, and the team required renewal. Yeats, St John, Hunt and Tommy Lawrence gave way as younger players took on their responsibilities. Ray Clemence, Larry Lloyd, Emlyn Hughes, Heighway, Toshack and Kevin Keegan helped define the emerging side. Lawler remained, alongside Smith and Callaghan. He was part of the connection between the generations, still contributing to the team rather than simply carrying memories of its earlier successes.

The 1970 derby winner caught that transition particularly well. Everton were two goals ahead after Alan Whittle and Joe Royle scored in the second half. Heighway’s goal revived Liverpool, and Toshack’s first for the club brought them level. The new attacking players had changed the match before the established full-back finished it. Lawler’s late arrival joined old and new in the same move. A contemporary report described a crowded Everton penalty area as Liverpool pressed to recover; the final shot belonged to someone whose starting position was at the other end.

Another opportunity to win the FA Cup came in 1971. At Wembley on 8 May, Lawler started against Arsenal in a Liverpool team containing many of the players central to the rebuild. Heighway put Liverpool ahead early in extra time, but Eddie Kelly equalised and Charlie George scored the winner. The 2–1 defeat left Lawler with another final in which a promising position had turned into disappointment. Reaching the occasion showed the renewed side’s possibilities. Winning its next major honours would require further progress.

Four days after that final, he made his senior England debut against Malta at Wembley. England won 5–0, and Lawler scored the fifth goal. It was an appropriately characteristic introduction for a defender with his Liverpool record. His international career was brief: four appearances, all in 1971, and that single goal. The larger achievement remained at club level, where he was regularly required across a much longer period. International recognition acknowledged his quality without matching the scale of his service at Anfield.

The fullest reward for the rebuilt team came in 1972–73. Liverpool regained the championship and won the UEFA Cup, their first European trophy. Lawler played all 66 competitive matches. Approaching thirty, he was still supplying the same dependable presence he had offered in the 1960s, now behind an attack with different strengths. The season joined the familiar league challenge to a long European campaign. His part cannot be confined to one celebrated goal or one final; he was present throughout the work that made both trophies possible.

In the UEFA Cup final’s first leg at Anfield on 10 May, Liverpool won 3–0 through two Keegan goals and one from Lloyd. Clemence saved a penalty from Jupp Heynckes, preserving the clean sheet. The advantage looked substantial, but the second leg at Mönchengladbach demonstrated the danger of treating it as decisive. Heynckes scored twice before half-time on 23 May. Liverpool had to protect a single-goal aggregate lead for the remainder of the match. Lawler’s job on that occasion was to help prevent another goal, rather than provide one himself.

They held on to win the tie 3–2. Liverpool historian Stephen Done later singled out Lawler’s defensive contribution in that difficult away leg. It is a useful counterpart to the memories of his finishing: a player with a striker’s instinct still had to do a full-back’s work when the opposition threatened to overturn the final. Liverpool’s first European trophy followed the earlier defeat at Hampden by seven years. Lawler had been there for both, retaining his place as the club’s continental experience developed.

He continued to score into the next season. His final Liverpool goal came against Red Star Belgrade at Anfield on 6 November 1973. After Toshack’s contribution, he equalised late in the second leg, but the visitors scored again and won 2–1. Liverpool were eliminated 4–2 on aggregate. Even a familiar Lawler intervention could not always change the outcome. The goal was the last addition to a total that had grown through a decade of choosing when to leave the defence and make himself available farther forward.

A knee injury against Queens Park Rangers that November changed his situation much more profoundly. He underwent a cartilage operation and struggled to regain his earlier fitness. The player whose availability had seemed almost automatic now faced prolonged absence and a reduced role. Shankly selected him as substitute for the 1974 FA Cup final, but he did not come on. Smith played at right-back while Liverpool beat Newcastle United 3–0 through Keegan, twice, and Heighway. Lawler collected another FA Cup medal from the bench, a very different involvement from 1965.

Shankly retired that summer, and Lawler’s appearances remained limited under Bob Paisley. He played sixteen times in 1974–75 and once the following season. The last match was a 1–0 UEFA Cup defeat by Hibernian at Easter Road on 17 September 1975. Phil Neal was also in the side, part of the next defensive generation. Lawler moved to Portsmouth in October, joining a club managed by his former teammate St John. His long Liverpool playing career had ended through a gradual loss of availability and place, rather than a single decision that erased his earlier value.

Football continued beyond Anfield. He had a loan spell with Miami Toros and subsequently played for Stockport County and Bangor City before becoming player-manager at Raufoss in Norway. Management and coaching offered another use for his experience, and in 1982 he returned to Liverpool to work with the reserve team. That return extended his association with the club beyond the twelve years of first-team appearances. He had first learnt from Liverpool’s professionals as a youngster; later, he could help players approaching the team themselves.

Lawler’s league record was 406 appearances and 41 goals, within the overall totals of 549 and 61. Those numbers describe an unusual combination of continuity and intervention. The dependable defender could remain almost unnoticed during the ordinary work of a match, then arrive to supply its decisive act. Liverpool benefited from both qualities across Shankly’s changing teams. The derby winner remains an especially vivid example, but the career behind it is the larger achievement: a local player finding his role, keeping it through renewal and making his quiet presence count.
