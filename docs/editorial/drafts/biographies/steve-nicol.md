# Steve Nicol: the complete footballer Liverpool could keep moving

Steve Nicol's first Liverpool goal was a winner at Queens Park Rangers in October 1983. He had been at Anfield for two years, but was still trying to establish himself when Joe Fagan sent him on for Craig Johnston. With seven minutes remaining, Nicol scored the only goal at Loftus Road. Liverpool had bought him with the eventual succession to Phil Neal in mind. His first decisive intervention arrived while Neal was still in the team, and it helped explain how the younger player could find his place without waiting for a vacancy at right-back.

That capacity became central to a Liverpool career of 468 competitive appearances and 46 goals. Nicol could defend either flank, move into the middle or contribute further forward. Managers could change his position and retain a high level of assurance. His goals made the versatility spectacular on occasion; the larger achievement was to keep supplying what the side needed across more than a decade of changing teams. Four league championships, three FA Cups, a European Cup and a League Cup marked the journey.

Bob Paisley signed Nicol from Ayr United for £300,000 in October 1981, following the recommendation of Geoff Twentyman. He was nineteen, with experience of part-time Scottish football and work as a building labourer. Liverpool offered a different professional world. He entered a club already accustomed to winning in Europe, where senior players had established both the standard and the competition for places. The immediate task was to learn how to become useful within it.

It was a patient beginning. Nicol made 26 appearances for the reserves in his first season and no competitive first-team appearances. The absence from the senior side should not obscure the purpose of that stage: Liverpool had recruited a possible successor to a firmly established international, rather than somebody guaranteed an immediate job. His first months were spent developing behind players who had already proved themselves in the club's most demanding matches.

His competitive debut finally came on 31 August 1982, a goalless draw at Birmingham City. Paisley's team at St Andrews included Neal, Alan Kennedy, Phil Thompson, Graeme Souness and Kenny Dalglish. Nicol had reached the first team, but made only four appearances in total that season. The gap between signing and regular selection was considerable. It gives the later longevity a different shape: his Liverpool career began with waiting and preparation, before it became associated with near-constant usefulness.

Fagan's first season as manager brought the breakthrough. Johnston's absence opened an opportunity on the right of midfield, allowing Nicol to play while Neal continued in defence. The QPR winner gave an early demonstration of his ability to affect an attack. A contemporary Shoot! interview recorded his ambition for 1984 as securing a regular Liverpool place. He was still describing the essential first step, even as the team around him was heading towards an extraordinary collection of trophies.

The same interview identified his first Merseyside derby as his most memorable match. Liverpool had beaten Everton 3–0 at Anfield the previous November, with Nicol scoring the third. It was an understandable choice for a young player entering the side: a derby goal made his progress visible to supporters as well as coaches. The career's later landmarks were still to come. At that point, belonging to Liverpool's first team was itself an achievement worth measuring.

By the end of 1983–84 he had played 38 times. Liverpool won the championship and League Cup before travelling to Rome for the European Cup final. Nicol had become part of the senior group quickly enough to share its greatest night of the season. His involvement at the Stadio Olimpico, however, would carry a difficulty that a simple list of medals cannot convey.

He replaced Johnston after 73 minutes of the final against Roma. The score remained 1–1 through extra time, and Nicol took Liverpool's first penalty in the shoot-out. He put it over the bar. The young player had accepted a responsibility that exposed him immediately to the possibility of failure, in the opposition's home stadium, with the European Cup at stake. Liverpool subsequently won the shoot-out 4–2, with Alan Kennedy converting the decisive kick.

Paisley's later assessment remembered the willingness to volunteer as much as the miss. That distinction captures something of Nicol's value. Liverpool had not needed a player who would avoid every mistake; they needed somebody prepared to take part when the match demanded it. Rome could have left him defined by a failed penalty. Instead, he left as a European champion whose first substantial senior season had helped bring three major trophies.

Nicol's football gave successive managers reasons to keep trusting him. Being comfortable with both feet allowed him to operate on either side without reducing every move to the same preferred foot. He could tackle and compete in the air, then take an opponent on or deliver a cross. These were connected abilities. A defender who could use possession constructively helped Liverpool move out of their own half, while the same confidence let him join an attack when the opportunity appeared.

His versatility also required judgement. Moving forward from a defensive position changes the balance of a team; moving between positions changes which teammate needs support and which space must be protected. Nicol could offer the attacking intervention without making it his entire identity. Liverpool's managers were able to move him because his game contained the necessary defensive work, rather than because he merely possessed the energy to run through several areas of the pitch.

The following season took Liverpool back to a European Cup final, with Nicol starting against Juventus at Heysel on 29 May 1985. Thirty-nine people died before the match. It was a disaster with consequences far beyond the players' competitive records.

Dalglish became player-manager that summer. Nicol's relationship with him continued from the dressing room into a new managerial arrangement, and the younger Scot remained part of the side that achieved Liverpool's first league and FA Cup double in 1986. The change of manager did not interrupt his progression. A recruit brought in for the future was now helping sustain Liverpool's success as the future arrived.

He started the FA Cup final against Everton on 10 May. Liverpool were behind to Gary Lineker at half-time before two Rush goals and one from Johnston brought a 3–1 victory. Mølby's passing and Rush's finishing supplied the most conspicuous moments of the recovery. Nicol's role belonged to the wider work of a team which had to survive a difficult first half and then find the means to reverse it. The double was another major achievement shared by a player who had become established across the side's different requirements.

Progress was not uninterrupted. Nicol played 21 competitive matches in 1986–87, with his last appearance of that campaign coming in December. A hernia and stomach operation preceded his return the following season. Liverpool's familiar utility player had experienced a lengthy absence, and Dalglish regarded having him back as something close to acquiring another player. His return therefore mattered within a team already undergoing a significant change in attack.

Barnes and Beardsley made their debuts at Arsenal on the opening day of 1987–88, joining an attack in which Aldridge had begun replacing the departed Rush. Nicol played at left-back at Highbury. Dalglish's account of the afternoon explicitly connected the result with the benefit of recovering a player who could fill several positions. Amid attention on the new arrivals, Liverpool were also regaining somebody whose knowledge of the team had been acquired over several seasons.

With the game level late on, Tony Adams headed away a Barnes free kick. Nicol met the clearance from the edge of the penalty area and headed it past John Lukic to give Liverpool a 2–1 win. It was an unusual finish, demanding sufficient power and accuracy to threaten goal from a position where a headed return might ordinarily begin another phase of attack. On the opening afternoon, the returning defender supplied the decisive goal.

A month later, on 20 September, he scored a hat-trick in a 4–1 win at Newcastle. The individual finishes differed: a first-half opener, a close-range second after Beardsley's unselfish square pass, and a chip over Gary Kelly to complete the treble. They showed a player capable of reading the opening, following an attack and choosing a finish. Aldridge scored Liverpool's other goal, but the afternoon belonged unusually to somebody best known for defending.

Nicol later selected Newcastle as the game he remembered most fondly. His account was characteristically less polished than the highlights: he admitted that the apparently simple second finish had almost gone wrong. He also recalled the fixture's move to Sunday television disrupting plans to give away his twin sister at her wedding on Arran. The signed match ball became a keepsake from a day whose complications had produced his most remarkable scoring performance.

That performance needs its proper place in the wider season. Nicol was one of the players who enabled Liverpool's new attacking combinations to flourish, and he was capable of becoming the finisher himself. The hat-trick did not turn him into a permanent centre-forward. It demonstrated how much an attack could gain from a teammate who recognised where to arrive. The celebrated forwards had another useful player moving with them, beyond the men opponents expected to occupy those positions.

Liverpool won the 1987–88 championship, but the season ended with defeat by Wimbledon in the FA Cup final. Nicol started at Wembley as Lawrie Sanchez's goal and Dave Beasant's save from Aldridge's penalty prevented another double. The contrast with the season's earlier attacking freedom was severe. Even a side with so many ways to make chances could encounter an afternoon when the necessary finish would not come.

The next campaign demanded a different expression of Nicol's adaptability. Hansen's long absence created a need in central defence, and Nicol could move inside to meet it. His contribution earned the Football Writers' Association Footballer of the Year award in 1989. For a player often remembered through the broad label of utility, it was an unusually clear individual recognition: the work performed across different positions had become visible as an outstanding season in its own right.

Liverpool's 1988–89 season was devastated by Hillsborough. Ninety-seven people died as a result of the disaster at the FA Cup semi-final against Nottingham Forest on 15 April 1989. The football which resumed afterwards belonged to a club and community grieving for its supporters. Nicol's individual award and Liverpool's remaining fixtures form part of the record, but cannot measure the experience or the losses of that spring.

He started the FA Cup final against Everton on 20 May and helped create Rush's first goal in extra time. Aldridge had given Liverpool an early lead before Stuart McCall's late equaliser took the game beyond ninety minutes. Nicol supplied Rush as Liverpool went ahead again; further goals from McCall and Rush completed a 3–2 result. His attacking contribution remained available even in a match whose pressure had already extended into extra time.

Six days later, Nicol was in the Liverpool side beaten 2–0 by Arsenal at Anfield. Michael Thomas's late second goal transferred the championship to the visitors. Liverpool and Arsenal finished level on points and goal difference, with Arsenal's higher goals total deciding the title. The match became one of English football's most enduring endings, and Nicol's record includes its disappointment alongside the Cup win. His strongest individually recognised season contained both outcomes.

The championship returned in 1989–90. One especially vivid evening came against Crystal Palace at Anfield on 12 September, when Nicol opened and completed Liverpool's scoring in a 9–0 win. There were eight different scorers, with Nicol the only player to score twice. He also supplied McMahon's goal. The variety across the scoresheet made the team's attacking strength plain; Nicol's presence at both ends of it was another reminder of how broadly he could contribute.

That final league championship under Dalglish was Nicol's fourth. His Liverpool story had now taken him from a young reserve behind the European champions to an experienced member of another title-winning side. The attacking personnel had changed, and his own place on the pitch could change with them. What persisted was the ability to participate in Liverpool's football without requiring the team to be redesigned around him.

Scotland also recognised that ability. Nicol won 27 senior caps and was part of the squad at the 1986 World Cup in Mexico. International football broadened his career beyond the club which supplied its longest and most successful stretch. For Liverpool, it was another indication that the player originally recruited from Ayr had developed into somebody trusted at a demanding level, after the careful beginning in the reserves.

Dalglish's departure in 1991 brought further change, with Moran temporarily in charge before Souness returned as manager. Nicol remained involved. At the 1992 FA Cup final he started alongside a mixture of established teammates and newer players, including Rob Jones and McManaman. Liverpool beat Sunderland 2–0 through Michael Thomas and Rush. The victory brought Nicol a third FA Cup, six years after the first.

McManaman's contribution made the changing generation particularly visible. Nicol was now an experienced link to earlier Liverpool teams within a side looking for a new direction. The Cup could not restore the previous championship pattern by itself, but it showed that his usefulness had survived another managerial transition. The old teammate who had once praised his potential was now selecting him in a winning final as manager.

His later involvement was more substantial than a retirement narrative might suggest. He started 39 competitive matches in 1992–93 and made 35 appearances the following season. Liverpool were changing around him, but he remained a working first-team player. The sharp reduction came in 1994–95, when he appeared only five times. His capacity to fill several positions had extended his involvement for years; eventually even that flexibility could no longer secure a regular place.

His final competitive Liverpool appearance was at Burnley on 5 October 1994. Roy Evans's side won the League Cup tie 4–1, with Nicol starting alongside Fowler, McManaman and Redknapp. The lineup illustrates the distance travelled since his debut beside Neal, Souness and Dalglish. Liverpool's new attacking generation had become established, while one of the players who connected it to the previous era was making his last appearance.

He left in January 1995 for Notts County. Further spells with Sheffield Wednesday, West Bromwich Albion on loan and Doncaster Rovers preceded a move to the Boston Bulldogs in 1999. Leaving Anfield did not end his playing life, and the American move provided a route into coaching. His association with Liverpool had lasted more than thirteen years, but he still had another substantial part of his football career ahead.

At New England Revolution he reached four MLS Cup finals, in 2002, 2005, 2006 and 2007. Winning the championship remained beyond him, but the club won the US Open Cup in 2007 and SuperLiga in 2008. His decade in charge ended in 2011 after two seasons outside the play-offs. The later record combined sustained competitive success with frustration at the final hurdle, giving his American career its own achievements and limits.

Nicol's Liverpool legacy rests on a similarly complete view of the footballer. The Rome miss belongs alongside the courage to take the penalty; the long absence belongs alongside the Highbury return; the Newcastle goals belong alongside the seasons spent protecting the defence. Liverpool could keep moving him because the different jobs were supported by genuine ability. His career supplied spectacular moments, but its enduring value was the depth of football he could offer wherever the team required him.
