# Peter Thompson: the winger who gave Shankly's Liverpool another way through

Liverpool's route to their first FA Cup passed through Peter Thompson's feet at Villa Park. On 27 March 1965, with the semi-final against Chelsea still goalless, the left-winger scored the opening goal. Willie Stevenson's penalty completed a 2–0 victory and sent Liverpool to Wembley. The player who had once knocked them out of the competition in Preston North End colours had become one of the men taking them towards its most important moment.

Thompson's place in Bill Shankly's first great Liverpool side rested on much more than that goal. He could unsettle a defender with pace, keep possession close enough to change direction and supply an attack that contained Roger Hunt and Ian St John. Alongside Ian Callaghan on the opposite flank, he gave Liverpool width and contrasting forms of invention. His skill was conspicuous; his extraordinary availability made it useful week after week.

He finished with 416 competitive appearances and 54 goals, two championship wins and the 1965 FA Cup. The career also contained disappointment with England, defeats in major finals and an unhappy last period at Liverpool when knee trouble separated him from the football he wanted to play. The confident winger in the championship team and the injured player struggling to find a future were the same man. Both belong in his history.

## Preston and the burden of a comparison

Born in Carlisle on 27 November 1942, Thompson attracted attention as a schoolboy before joining Preston. His senior debut came against Arsenal on 30 August 1960, when he was seventeen. He entered a club adjusting to Tom Finney's retirement, an awkward inheritance for a young attacker. The possibility of discovering another great winger generated an expectation that personal talent alone could not satisfy.

Thompson later remembered being compared with Finney and feeling the pressure. He respected the older player's ability and recognised that he was somebody different. The comparison supplied recognition and an impossible standard simultaneously.

Preston were relegated in 1961, and Thompson continued playing in the Second Division. When Liverpool faced them in the FA Cup fifth round in February 1962, the tie required two replays. Thompson scored the winner in the second, at Old Trafford, after the first two matches had ended goalless. Shankly saw enough of his speed and skill to remember the opponent who had ended Liverpool's run.

The eventual transfer took another eighteen months. Thompson joined Liverpool on 14 August 1963 for £37,000, at the age of twenty. Shankly was adding him to a side promoted in 1962 and already established back in the First Division. The signing came at a point when Liverpool needed to turn a successful return into a serious challenge for the championship. Thompson was being recruited to help complete an attack, with a clear first-team opportunity available.

## Immediate influence in a championship team

His Liverpool debut came in a 2–1 win at Blackburn on 24 August 1963. There was no prolonged wait for a settled place: he played all 42 league matches in his first season. Liverpool won the championship by four points from Manchester United, securing their first title since 1947. Thompson scored six league goals, but his importance also came from giving the forwards a dependable source of attacking possibilities.

His place on the left complemented Callaghan on the right. Thompson's own description distinguished Callaghan's direct movement and early delivery from his greater willingness to hold the ball and beat a defender. They could swap sides when the particular opponent made one approach less effective. The partnership was useful because the two men could present different difficulties, rather than merely reproduce the same action on opposite wings.

A full-back facing Thompson had to judge whether to confront him immediately, retreat or protect the route inside. Close control and changes of direction made that decision uncomfortable. Liverpool could use the space he created for a cross, a pass or a shot. The pleasure he gave spectators was connected to a practical football purpose: a player who could beat his immediate opponent could disturb the organisation behind that opponent too.

He worked to expand those possibilities. Thompson was naturally right-footed but practised crossing with his left at Melwood. Playing on the left therefore offered more than a permanent invitation to turn infield onto his stronger foot. The practice helped him go outside as well. The capacity remembered as instinctive trickery contained training, repetition and an attempt to make his next move harder to anticipate.

The durability was equally important. In his first seven seasons he played 284 of a possible 294 league games. Shankly could build an attack knowing that Thompson would usually be available to occupy his place in it. An inventive winger who missed only ten fixtures across such a span was an unusually stable component of the team. Liverpool gained both the unpredictability of his play and the predictability of his presence.

## The first FA Cup and another title

The 1964–65 season offered Liverpool a different achievement. Thompson's opening goal against Chelsea brought them closer to a trophy the club had never won. The semi-final was still level at half-time; his breakthrough came in the 63rd minute. In a competition where a single mistake or opening could settle a team's season, he had supplied the intervention Liverpool needed before Stevenson added the penalty.

At Wembley on 1 May, Leeds United proved harder to overcome. Thompson played the whole match, including extra time. Hunt headed Liverpool ahead from Gerry Byrne's cross, Billy Bremner equalised and St John scored the winner from Callaghan's delivery. The principal finishing moments belonged to others, but Thompson was part of the side that endured the whole afternoon and ended the long wait.

His FA Cup achievement was not dependent on being the centre of every memorable scene. Liverpool needed different players to take responsibility as each tie demanded. Thompson had helped secure the Wembley opportunity and then played his part when the most celebrated goals came from Hunt and St John.

In 1965–66 Liverpool regained the championship, with Thompson appearing in 40 of the 42 league games. Two titles and the FA Cup in his first three seasons made the transfer's value unmistakable. These were substantial achievements in their own right, rather than preliminary steps whose importance depends on the European trophies Liverpool later won. Thompson belonged to the team that established Shankly's club as winners at the highest domestic level.

Europe brought a different conclusion that spring. Thompson started the Cup Winners' Cup final against Borussia Dortmund at Hampden Park on 5 May 1966. Hunt equalised after Dortmund had gone ahead, but Stan Libuda's extra-time goal gave the German side a 2–1 victory. Liverpool's first European final ended in defeat. The winger's honours describe the domestic successes accurately; they do not conceal that the team had encountered a limit beyond England.

## A wider role and international frustration

Thompson continued to be heavily involved after the trophy sequence ended. His appearance totals remained high as Liverpool sought another way back to the top. A contemporary report in the Liverpool Echo in August 1968 described Shankly planning to give him more freedom to move inside and attack through the centre. A pre-season performance at Hull had offered an encouraging example of the intended change.

Shankly wanted to vary where his speed and shooting could be used, allowing the game's pattern to determine his movements.

England had recognised his ability soon after the Liverpool move. Thompson made his senior international debut in a 4–3 win over Portugal in Lisbon on 17 May 1964. He eventually won sixteen caps without scoring, with his last appearance against Scotland in April 1970. The career included opponents and occasions of considerable stature, but no place in a final World Cup squad.

He was omitted from both the 1966 and 1970 tournament parties after appearing in the initial selections. Ramsey's movement towards a side without conventional wingers restricted the role for which Thompson was most naturally equipped. England's success in 1966 did not make his personal disappointment disappear; nor did it establish that Liverpool had overvalued him. Club football and international selection were asking different things of an accomplished player.

## A final contribution, then a painful separation

The change in Liverpool's team became more pronounced in the early 1970s. Thompson's 1970–71 campaign produced 39 appearances in all competitions, fewer than in any of his preceding seasons. Injury interrupted the extraordinary run of availability, while younger players helped reshape the attack. Thompson remained capable of affecting an important match, but his presence was becoming less automatic.

The 1971 FA Cup final supplied one last major illustration. He replaced Alun Evans after 68 minutes against Arsenal and provided the pass for Steve Heighway's goal early in extra time. Liverpool led briefly before Eddie Kelly equalised and Charlie George scored the winner. A player associated with the first Cup success had helped Liverpool threaten another, six years later, within a substantially different side.

The pass also connected two stages of Shankly's Liverpool. Thompson's own opportunities were diminishing, yet his skill could still help a newer attacking player score on the largest domestic stage. The defeat prevents the moment becoming a neat farewell triumph. It remains evidence that his earlier importance did not vanish at the first appearance of a successor.

His last senior Liverpool match was the goalless draw at Wolves on 22 January 1972. No competitive first-team appearance followed, although he remained a Liverpool player for nearly two more years. The distinction explains why the familiar playing dates end in 1972 while his permanent departure came in 1974. His employment outlasted his usable place in the senior team.

In his later account, Thompson described knee operations, uncertainty about playing again and a painful deterioration in his relationship with Shankly. He remembered the manager's treatment during injury as markedly different from their successful years together.

The long record of availability makes that ending particularly stark. Liverpool had been able to rely on him through hundreds of matches; now a damaged knee made his immediate usefulness uncertain. He was trying to decide whether football could continue at all. The contribution already made did not provide an easy answer to what he should do next.

## Bolton and the value left behind

Jimmy Armfield offered a route back with Bolton Wanderers, initially on loan in December 1973. A permanent transfer followed in January 1974. The move took Thompson away from Anfield and into the Second Division, but it gave him another opportunity to play.

He stayed until 1978 and ended his career as Bolton won promotion to the First Division. The recovery gives his story a more substantial conclusion than the image of a winger stranded in Liverpool's reserves. His first-team Liverpool career had finished, but his professional football had not. Another club could benefit from a player whose usefulness at Anfield had become exhausted.

Thompson died in December 2018, aged 76. Liverpool remembered a central figure in the achievements of the mid-1960s, while Bolton remembered their own years with him. His strongest Liverpool legacy remains unusually clear: the dependable left-wing presence in two championship sides, the breakthrough against Chelsea and the partnership that gave Shankly's attack two different ways to threaten.

The 54 goals are only one measure of that contribution. The dribbling created situations for other players; the endurance allowed Liverpool to repeat their attacking patterns; the skill could still influence a final when he was no longer a regular starter. Thompson helped make the first great Shankly team both effective and enjoyable to watch. The difficulty of his departure does not diminish that achievement, and the achievement does not make the departure less difficult.
