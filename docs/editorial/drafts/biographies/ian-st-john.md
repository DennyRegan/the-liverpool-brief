# Ian St John: at the front of Liverpool's revival

Ian St John's diving header at Wembley in 1965 gave Liverpool the FA Cup for the first time, but his part in the club's revival had begun four years earlier. He arrived from Motherwell while Liverpool were still in the Second Division and helped make Bill Shankly's ambition visible on the pitch. In ten years at Anfield he scored 118 goals in 425 appearances, combining his own finishing with the invention that helped Roger Hunt prosper. The Wembley winner was the most celebrated expression of a contribution that extended through promotion, two league championships and Liverpool's first European campaigns.

Born in Motherwell on 7 June 1938, St John had already become a significant player before Liverpool bought him. As a boy he watched his home-town club win the Scottish Cup in 1952; he later emerged in the Motherwell side remembered as the Ancell Babes. His record there included 80 goals in 113 league appearances. A rapid League Cup hat-trick against Hibernian in 1959 brought wider attention, but his sustained scoring was the more substantial evidence of his quality. That year also brought his first Scotland cap. Liverpool were recruiting an established international forward, rather than asking an untested youngster to transform their attack.

Shankly had seen St John and Ron Yeats while managing Huddersfield Town and retained an interest in both. Liverpool signed St John on 2 May 1961 for £37,500, a club-record fee; Yeats followed that summer to strengthen the defence. St John made an immediate impression with a hat-trick against Everton at Goodison Park in the Liverpool Senior Cup final, although Liverpool lost 4–3. That local competition appearance is distinct from the league debut recorded in August. Together, the two Scottish recruits gave Shankly experienced central figures at opposite ends of the team he was building.

Promotion followed in their first season. St John scored 18 league goals in 1961–62, with his total across the league and FA Cup reaching 22, as Liverpool won the Second Division title. His partnership with Hunt gave the attack both a consistent goal threat and different ways of creating openings. This was the first essential achievement of his Liverpool career: a club that had repeatedly fallen short could finally begin testing itself against the leading teams again. The transfer fee was attached to a player whose influence could be seen immediately in the change of division.

St John's aerial ability was especially striking because he was not a tall centre-forward. Timing and spring helped him compete with bigger defenders, and he could turn a delivery into a chance for someone else as well as finish it himself. His creative work mattered alongside his scoring. The technical quality was accompanied by an appetite for physical competition that sometimes became a liability. In a later interview with LFChistory, St John acknowledged his quick temper and recalled being sent off at Fulham and Coventry. His own account resists a softened portrait in which determination had no cost.

His production held up in the First Division. Nineteen league goals in 1962–63 became 21 in 1963–64, when Liverpool won the championship. That progression is an important part of assessing the signing: St John's effectiveness had survived the move to a higher level, then helped Liverpool win there. He offered Shankly a scorer and a creator in the same forward line, contributing to a team that had advanced from promotion to the title in two seasons. Liverpool's revival now had a top-flight championship to confirm it.

The following season supplied the goal that would define his public memory. On 1 May 1965, Liverpool faced Leeds United in the FA Cup final. Gerry Byrne played despite a broken collarbone and supplied the cross for Hunt's opening goal in extra time. Billy Bremner equalised, leaving the final level again. In the 111th minute Ian Callaghan crossed and St John headed past Gary Sprake to settle the match 2–1. The decisive movement was characteristic of a forward whose heading depended on judgement and timing. Liverpool had won a competition that had resisted them throughout their history, and St John had provided its final intervention.

The European Cup brought another stage for his finishing. He scored five times in Liverpool's first campaign in the competition, including twice in the opening 6–1 victory over KR Reykjavik. Three days after the Wembley final, he scored Liverpool's third goal in the 3–1 first-leg semi-final win over Inter Milan at Anfield, following up after Hunt's effort was saved. Liverpool subsequently lost the tie, but St John had been directly involved in both the domestic breakthrough and the European challenge. The two matches placed his contribution at the centre of a remarkable week in the club's development.

Liverpool won the league again in 1965–66, with St John contributing ten league goals. They also reached the European Cup Winners' Cup final, losing 2–1 to Borussia Dortmund in Glasgow. The defeat denied that side a European trophy, but the appearance marked another advance in the scope of Liverpool's ambitions. For St John, five years had taken him from a Second Division transfer to two English championships, an FA Cup and a European final. Those achievements gave substance to Shankly's belief in the players he had brought south.

St John's later Liverpool seasons required a different contribution. He moved deeper, using his understanding of the game in midfield as the attack changed around him. His reduced scoring did not mean an immediate disappearance from the side: the appearance record shows that he remained heavily involved through the later 1960s. Eventually, however, Shankly began replacing the players of his first successful team. St John made only three competitive appearances in 1970–71. His last, in January, also brought his final Liverpool goal, closing the playing record before his departure later that year.

He played for Hellenic in South Africa during 1971 and subsequently joined Coventry City, then Tranmere Rovers, where Yeats was manager. His Scotland career had produced nine goals in 21 appearances. These later moves and the international record form part of a wider career, but Liverpool remained the setting for his major club achievements. The partnership with Yeats had also outlasted their years in the same Anfield side: the defender signed alongside him in the rebuilding period was now overseeing one of his final playing spells.

Management took St John back to Motherwell in 1973. There he brought together Bobby Graham and Willie Pettigrew in attack before leaving for Portsmouth after little more than a year. Broadcasting eventually provided a more enduring second career. With Jimmy Greaves he presented ITV's Saturday lunchtime programme *Saint and Greavsie* from 1985 to 1992. It introduced him to viewers too young to have watched the Wembley winner or the promotion campaign. The familiar television figure thus belonged to a later football audience as well as to the Liverpool supporters who had known him first as a player.

Liverpool recognised St John and Yeats together with lifetime achievement awards in 2015. Looking back at the period, St John stressed the contribution of the younger players around them and the difference between the club they had joined and the one it became. It was a useful perspective from someone so closely identified with one goal: the change involved a team and the manager who directed it. His own importance lay partly in helping that group acquire the confidence and quality to make its first advances.

St John died on 1 March 2021, aged 82. The Wembley header remains the clearest single image of his Liverpool career, yet the record around it explains why he mattered. He had helped win promotion before he won a cup final, scored consistently as Liverpool became champions and continued contributing after his role changed. His Liverpool was the club moving upwards under Shankly, and he was one of the players who made that movement possible.
