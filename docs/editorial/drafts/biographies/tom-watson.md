# Tom Watson: the manager of Liverpool’s first champions

Liverpool won the Second Division in 1905 and the First Division in 1906. No club had previously taken the two championships in successive seasons. Tom Watson had already managed Liverpool to their first league title in 1901; now he had led them out of relegation and straight back to the summit. The sequence captures both the scale of his achievement and the instability he had to confront during almost nineteen years at Anfield.

Watson managed Liverpool from 1896 until his death in May 1915. His tenure encompassed the club’s first two top-flight championships and its first FA Cup final, while his recruitment supplied many of the players around whom its early history developed. Liverpool’s current record credits him with 742 matches. The length of that service matters, but so does its timing: he took charge of a club only four years old and helped make it a national champion.

Born in Newcastle upon Tyne in 1859, Watson arrived with achievements that Liverpool could not yet match. He had worked at Newcastle’s East End and West End clubs before becoming Sunderland’s secretary in 1889. Sunderland entered the Football League the following year and won the championship in 1892, 1893 and 1895. Liverpool were recruiting a man who had already demonstrated that he could organise a successful side in the expanding professional game.

John McKenna moved into the boardroom as Watson came to Anfield in the summer of 1896. Liverpool had just won promotion back to the First Division after relegation at their first attempt to remain there. The new appointment gave responsibility for football affairs to an experienced specialist. At 37, Watson was young enough for a long future, but his Sunderland record made the move an unmistakable statement of ambition.

The job combined duties now divided between several departments. As secretary, Watson arranged fixtures, maintained records and supplied information to newspapers. His responsibilities also extended to selecting and recruiting players and directing their preparation. Understanding that breadth is essential to his Liverpool career: building a team involved administrative work, contacts and travel as well as judging what happened on the pitch.

Watson’s own account of recruitment, published in 1899, described the change from his earlier expeditions into Scotland to dealings through the transfer system at Liverpool. He recalled resistance to English clubs taking Scottish players and the relationships made through those rivalries. The stories were his recollections, often told for entertainment, but his closing distinction was practical: at Liverpool, he could negotiate for players without repeating the hazardous journeys of his earlier career.

His contacts remained valuable. In a 1924 recollection, the Liverpool Echo’s Victor Hall described a network of friends who would provide information about a player’s experience and availability. That account helps explain the human work behind Watson’s reputation as a recruiter. His success depended on obtaining useful assessments and bringing players together, rather than simply assembling a collection of celebrated names.

Preparation was equally deliberate, although its methods belonged to the football of his time. Watson introduced a regulated diet and exercise routine. The first league match of his tenure, against The Wednesday on 1 September 1896, also marked Liverpool’s appearance in red shirts and white shorts. A 2–1 victory began a season in which the promoted team finished fifth and reached the FA Cup semi-final, losing 3–0 to Aston Villa.

There were early signs of progress without a smooth upward line. Liverpool finished ninth in 1897–98, a season that included their first league victory over Everton, 3–1 at Anfield. Watson continued to strengthen the side. Jack Cox came from Blackpool, Tom Robertson and John Walker from Hearts, and Alex Raisbeck from Hibernian. Raisbeck’s authority at centre-half would become one of the defining features of Watson’s Liverpool.

By 1898–99, Liverpool were challenging for both major domestic prizes. Their FA Cup semi-final against Sheffield United became a prolonged ordeal: two draws were followed by an abandoned match at overcrowded Fallowfield, then defeat in another meeting at Derby. The championship remained within reach. Liverpool went to Aston Villa for their final league match needing a draw to finish above their opponents, but lost 5–0.

A contemporary view of Watson’s preparation survives in an Evening Express report from March 1899. Liverpool were based at St Anne’s-on-the-Sea, where the reporter found the secretary catching up with paperwork. Walking, sprinting, baths supervised by trainer James Chapman and quiet evening recreation formed the routine. Watson explained that the players did not practise with a football during the week. The report presents an approach concerned with condition and freshness, rather than a programme resembling modern technical training.

The near miss did not lead immediately to a title. Liverpool opened the following season with eight successive league defeats and eventually finished tenth. They also lost George Allan, an important forward in the earlier challenge, who died in October 1899. Watson recruited Sam Raybould from New Brighton Tower in January 1900. The next championship side emerged through further work after a season in which the previous momentum had disappeared.

Liverpool began 1900–01 with three wins. Their 5–1 victory over Aston Villa at Anfield in November offered a particularly striking demonstration of their attacking quality. Contemporary reporting praised the speed of their forward passing and the way it unsettled Villa’s defence. Raybould supplied the finishing power, with Walker and Robertson among the players helping develop the attacks, while Raisbeck gave the team its central authority.

Yet the championship was secured through a sustained recovery rather than an uninterrupted procession. A 1–0 victory at Sunderland began a twelve-match unbeaten league run. By the last fixture, Sunderland had completed their programme and Liverpool needed a point at West Bromwich Albion. On 29 April 1901, Walker scored the only goal at The Hawthorns. Liverpool finished two points clear of Watson’s former club and became champions of England for the first time.

The title was the clearest vindication of the appointment made five years earlier. It also demonstrated the value of a balanced side: Bill Perkins was ever-present in goal, Billy Dunlop provided long service at full-back, and Raisbeck organised from centre-half. Contemporary accounts of the team’s return described a musical welcome for the champions. Liverpool’s success had become something the city could celebrate publicly, beyond the club’s immediate circle.

Sustaining it proved much harder. Liverpool finished eleventh in 1901–02, and relegation followed in 1904. The manager of the club’s first champions therefore also presided over a return to the second tier only three years later. That reversal belongs alongside his trophies. Watson’s early Liverpool were capable of reaching the top, but had not established the consistency that would keep them there.

His response combined established leadership with further recruitment. Raisbeck remained at the centre of the side, and Watson brought in Ned Doig, the experienced goalkeeper from his Sunderland years. Doig played every league match in 1904–05. Liverpool conceded only 28 goals across their 36 fixtures, beat Manchester United 4–0 in the penultimate match and finished two points above Bolton Wanderers to win the Second Division.

Promotion was followed by another difficult beginning. Liverpool lost their first three league matches in 1905–06, conceding eleven goals. Jack Parkinson broke his wrist in the opening defeat at Woolwich Arsenal, and Dunlop also suffered an injury. A 2–1 win at Sunderland brought the first points, but five defeats in the opening eight games showed how much improvement was still required.

Watson had signed Sam Hardy from Chesterfield, and the younger goalkeeper replaced Doig after those eight fixtures. Hardy’s first Liverpool season ended with a championship, and he subsequently held the position for years. The change was part of a wider recovery: the season’s results show Liverpool climbing from sixteenth after eight games to first by mid-December, with victories at Newcastle and against Aston Villa among the important steps.

The pursuit of two trophies again ended at the FA Cup semi-final, where Everton beat Liverpool 2–0. In the league, Preston North End remained the principal challenger. Liverpool lost 3–2 at Bolton on 16 April, but Preston’s defeat at Sunderland meant they could no longer catch Watson’s side. Liverpool were champions again. Their rise from relegation to successive divisional titles had required both the retention of key players and the willingness to change the team.

At the club’s meeting after the season, a contemporary report described Watson acknowledging Liverpool’s fluctuations and urging continued progress. The championship was accompanied by improvements to Anfield, including the new terrace that became the Kop. These developments gave the second title a place in the physical growth of the club as well as its football record. Watson’s tenure encompassed the emergence of familiar features of Liverpool’s identity.

There would be no third championship under him. Liverpool were runners-up to Aston Villa in 1909–10, when Parkinson scored thirty First Division goals. Other seasons were less convincing. In 1911–12, Watson entrusted the inexperienced Kenneth Campbell with the goalkeeping position during a relegation struggle. Three wins in the last four matches, with three clean sheets from Campbell, helped Liverpool survive. Long service had continued to involve rebuilding and difficult decisions.

The FA Cup finally offered a new opportunity in 1914. Liverpool beat Aston Villa in the semi-final through two Jimmy Nicholl goals to reach their first final. The achievement came during a league season that ended in sixteenth place. As in earlier phases of Watson’s career, the cup run and the league position told different stories about the same team.

At Crystal Palace on 25 April, Burnley won 1–0 through Bert Freeman’s second-half goal. Liverpool had lost their regular captain Harry Lowe to injury before the match, but still could not find an equaliser. Watson’s long pursuit of the FA Cup had ended one match short. Four days later, a charity fixture he had proposed brought the finalists to Anfield, where Liverpool won and Burnley paraded the trophy. The return match did not alter the final’s result, but showed another part of the secretary’s work beyond competition.

The First World War was under way during Watson’s last season. He remained in charge through the closing league fixtures of April 1915 and died of pneumonia on 6 May, aged 56. The Liverpool Echo’s funeral report recorded former players including Raisbeck, Doig and Arthur Goddard carrying his coffin, with representatives of numerous clubs present. He was buried at Anfield Cemetery, close to the ground where most of his working life had been spent.

In 2015, Liverpool and Sunderland jointly supported a headstone for his previously unmarked grave. The gesture connected the two clubs at which he had won five top-flight championships. His Liverpool record is more demanding than a simple list of honours: first titles, relegation, recovery, near misses and repeated changes across a long formative period. Watson gave a young club the organisation and players to become champions, then did the work again when that first success had fallen away.
