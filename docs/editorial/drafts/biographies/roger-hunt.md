# Roger Hunt: the goals that carried Liverpool upwards

Roger Hunt’s Liverpool career can be followed through the changing significance of his goals. They helped the club escape the Second Division, made it champion of England and opened the scoring in its first victorious FA Cup final. By the time he left Anfield in December 1969, he had scored 285 times in 492 competitive appearances. The record was extraordinary; so was the transformation of the team in which he achieved it.

Hunt belonged to Liverpool before Bill Shankly arrived. Phil Taylor signed him from Stockton Heath in July 1958, after former Liverpool player Bill Jones had spotted him in amateur football. Born on 20 July 1938 near Warrington, Hunt came to Anfield through a local route into the professional game. Shankly would give his career its defining setting, but the striker was among the talents the new manager inherited.

His senior debut came against Scunthorpe United at Anfield on 9 September 1959. Liverpool won 2–0 and Hunt scored. It was an early demonstration of the quality the club had found, although the side remained in the Second Division. The arrival of Shankly later that year brought a wider reconstruction. Hunt survived the changes because he offered both the ability to finish chances and the willingness to work for the team creating them.

The effort was part of his effectiveness. Hunt was strong, quick and persistent, prepared to chase, tackle and practise. His own account of establishing himself emphasised the work he put into training and improving his ball skills. He could score with force, but his contribution did not depend on a single kind of finish. Liverpool had a forward whose appetite for the less conspicuous work helped sustain his threat in front of goal.

The arrival of Ian St John in 1961 gave Hunt the partner with whom he became most closely associated. Together they led the attack through Liverpool’s first great Shankly years. In the promotion season of 1961–62, Hunt scored 41 goals in 41 league matches. That return supplied a large part of the scoring behind the Second Division championship and gave the partnership a formidable starting point. Liverpool were going up, with Hunt already central to their identity.

Promotion did not diminish his importance. Hunt was Liverpool’s leading scorer in eight successive seasons, from 1961–62 through 1968–69. That sequence spans the climb out of the second tier, the major trophies that followed and the less successful later years of the first Shankly team. His value was sustained across changing circumstances. Liverpool could rely on him for goals while the standard of the opposition and the demands on the club increased.

The first top-flight championship arrived in 1963–64. Hunt scored 31 league goals as Liverpool won the title only two years after promotion. In 1965–66, when they became champions again, he contributed 29. Those figures belong specifically to the league: his work in domestic and European cups added further goals. The championship seasons established his finishing at the highest domestic level, beyond the exceptional scoring run that had carried Liverpool out of Division Two.

Between those titles came the FA Cup. At Wembley in May 1965, Liverpool and Leeds United reached extra time without a goal. Hunt broke the deadlock with a low header. Leeds equalised through Billy Bremner before St John headed the winner, making the final another expression of the partnership’s importance. Liverpool’s 2–1 victory ended the club’s wait for the trophy. Hunt had helped secure a place in its history that could never be displaced by a later scoring record.

The opportunities now extended across Europe. Hunt scored Liverpool’s equaliser against Borussia Dortmund in the 1966 European Cup Winners’ Cup final at Hampden Park, although the match ended in a 2–1 extra-time defeat. He had progressed from the Second Division to a European final with the same club. The result also supplies a necessary limit to the story: Liverpool’s first European trophy would come after his departure, with a different attacking partnership.

His goals reached another audience through television. Against Arsenal at the start of the 1964–65 season, Hunt scored the first goal shown on the BBC’s Match of the Day. It was a small distinction alongside championships and cup finals, but an enduring one. His career coincided with Liverpool becoming a much more prominent national football presence, and one of his finishes became part of the programme’s beginning.

England had recognised him while Liverpool were still in the Second Division. Walter Winterbottom gave Hunt his international debut in 1962, and he travelled to that year’s World Cup as a squad member without playing. Four years later, under Alf Ramsey, his role was much larger. The strength, running and commitment that helped Liverpool also made him valuable to an England side requiring substantial work from its forwards.

Hunt played in all six matches as England won the 1966 World Cup and scored three goals in the group stage. Against Mexico, he followed up after the goalkeeper could only push away Jimmy Greaves’s shot, scoring England’s second in a 2–0 win. The finish showed the practical value of being ready for the next chance. Over the tournament, his contribution combined those goals with the work that helped other players function around him.

He finished his England career with 18 goals in 34 appearances. Liverpool supporters knew him as ‘Sir Roger’, an affectionate title rather than a formal knighthood. His place in the World Cup-winning team added a national achievement to the club honours, but the bond at Anfield had been built over years of watching him play. The combination of scoring and dependable effort made him a defining player of the era.

In November 1967 Hunt passed Gordon Hodgson’s total of 241 goals to become Liverpool’s leading scorer. He ultimately left a record of 285, including 244 in the league. Ian Rush overtook the overall total in 1992. Hunt’s league tally has its own place in Liverpool’s record books, accumulated in the two divisions through which he travelled with the club. The numbers preserve the scale of his achievement beyond the individual finals and championships.

His departure belonged to the difficult ending of Shankly’s first great side. During 1969–70 he lost his regular place, and in December 1969 he moved to Bolton Wanderers. The separation was painful despite Bolton being the club he had supported as a boy. His daughter Julie later recalled how much he missed Liverpool in the early weeks after the transfer, and the letters he continued to receive from supporters.

Nat Lofthouse signed him for Bolton, where Hunt spent three seasons and scored 24 goals in 76 league appearances. He was their leading scorer in 1970–71, but could not prevent relegation to the Third Division. He also had a brief loan spell with Hellenic in South Africa. The closing period of his career therefore brought a different task from his championship years at Anfield. His scoring continued, without the team success that had accompanied his best Liverpool seasons.

Anfield’s response when he returned for his 1972 testimonial demonstrated the affection that remained. More than 55,000 attended. His family remembered that Hunt had worried supporters might have forgotten him after his departure; the size of the crowd moved him deeply. The evening brought together memories of Liverpool’s first FA Cup winners and England’s World Cup-winning generation, two teams to which he had contributed so much.

After football, Hunt worked in the family haulage business. In 2000 he received an MBE, and in 2006 he entered the National Football Museum’s Hall of Fame. He died on 27 September 2021, aged eighty-three. His place in Liverpool’s history rests on more than a single famous afternoon: he supplied goals and work throughout the years in which Shankly’s first team changed the club’s position in the game. ‘Sir Roger’ was the supporters’ name for a contribution they had seen, week after week.
