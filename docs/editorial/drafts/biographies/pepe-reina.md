# Pepe Reina: the goalkeeper who made Liverpool his home

When David Ngog scored in front of the Kop to settle Liverpool's match against Manchester United in October 2009, Pepe Reina ran almost the length of the pitch to join him. The goalkeeper's arrival surprised the scorer. Years later, Ngog remembered the celebration as an entirely spontaneous expression of happiness: a teammate hurtling towards him, a famous opponent beaten, relief shared with the supporters. Liverpool had won 2–0, and their goalkeeper wanted to be at the centre of it.

It remains an unusually revealing image of Reina. For most of a match his job demanded restraint, judgement and an understanding of what might happen next. Here he abandoned the distance between goalkeeper and attack and joined the moment. The technical assurance and the exuberance belonged to the same player. Across eight seasons in Liverpool's first team, both helped make an arrival from Spain feel like an enduring part of the club.

## Replacing a hero

Reina's introduction in July 2005 required considerable nerve from Rafael Benítez. Jerzy Dudek had just helped Liverpool win the European Cup in Istanbul, his extraordinary final saves and penalty shootout already secured in club folklore. Yet the next campaign began with another goalkeeper. In the Champions League qualifier against Total Network Solutions at Anfield on 13 July, Reina started as Liverpool won 3–0 through Steven Gerrard's hat-trick. It was a quiet competitive debut, with little serious work, but an unmistakable statement about the manager's plans.

The newcomer cost £6 million from Villarreal. He was only 22 but had already played first-team football for Barcelona and accumulated substantial experience in Spain. Liverpool were therefore buying a young goalkeeper who had already encountered the demands of senior competition. They offered him a new setting and an immediate first-team place. Benítez's selection made the scale of that opportunity clear.

That decision helps explain Reina's importance to this Liverpool side. The manager was prepared to change a position that had delivered the most celebrated moment of his first season. The emotional pull of Istanbul was enormous; selection still had to address the next match. Reina became one of the clearest examples of Benítez shaping a team according to its future requirements.

## More than the last save

Reina's qualities extended beyond preventing goals. John Achterberg, who worked with Liverpool's goalkeepers, later singled out his distribution and ability to read the game. Those attributes explain why his involvement could be important even when he faced few shots. A goalkeeper who recognises danger early can intervene before a save becomes necessary; one who uses the ball accurately can influence the next attack rather than simply return possession to an opponent.

Liverpool had employed accomplished keepers before him. Reina's distinction was the combination: assured handling and anticipation alongside the ability to make possession useful. A match could move from defence towards attack through the man wearing number 25, and opponents had to consider what he might do with the ball as well as how to get it past him.

The collective organisation around him mattered too. In December 2005 Liverpool reached a club record of eleven successive clean sheets when they beat Deportivo Saprissa at the Club World Championship. It was a team achievement, involving the defenders and midfielders who limited opponents' chances as well as the goalkeeper who dealt with what remained. Reina's arrival nevertheless coincided with a striking increase in the defensive reliability of the European champions.

He also acquired silverware quickly. Liverpool beat CSKA Moscow 3–1 after extra time in the UEFA Super Cup in August 2005, with Djibril Cissé's intervention turning a match that had been slipping away. Reina was already participating in the club's habit of making difficult occasions end successfully. His most memorable contribution to that habit came at the end of the season.

## Cardiff: recovering in public

The FA Cup final against West Ham United on 13 May 2006 tested Reina's capacity to recover. Liverpool fell two goals behind through a Jamie Carragher own goal and Dean Ashton's finish. Djibril Cissé and Gerrard brought them level, only for Paul Konchesky's cross to find its way into Reina's net. The goalkeeper had endured a troubled afternoon. Liverpool were approaching defeat when Gerrard's stoppage-time strike made it 3–3.

That equaliser is the final's defining goal, but it did not win the trophy. Extra time left the sides level, and the shootout shifted responsibility towards Reina. He saved from Bobby Zamora, Konchesky and Anton Ferdinand. Liverpool won the penalties 3–1, and a goalkeeper who had been implicated in his team's difficulties became decisive in resolving them.

Reina's success did not erase the mistakes of the preceding two hours. It made his recovery part of the final's story, alongside Gerrard's rescue act. Cardiff gave Liverpool an FA Cup and gave supporters a compelling early reason to trust their goalkeeper under pressure.

The season's league record supplied a different kind of evidence. Reina won the Premier League Golden Glove with twenty clean sheets. He retained the award with nineteen in 2006–07 and eighteen in 2007–08. Three consecutive awards turned the impression of assurance into a sustained achievement. Cup finals offered concentrated drama; league football demanded that he repeat the ordinary work over many months.

## Europe, from Anfield to Athens

In May 2007 the penalty specialist again helped take Liverpool towards a major prize. Chelsea arrived at Anfield for the second leg of the Champions League semi-final with a one-goal advantage. Daniel Agger's first-half finish levelled the tie, while Reina blocked a chance for Didier Drogba. No further goal survived through extra time, and another shootout followed.

Reina stopped Arjen Robben's attempt and later denied Geremi. Dirk Kuyt converted the decisive kick as Liverpool won 4–1 on penalties. The result made the goalkeeper an essential figure in a second European Cup final appearance for Benítez's team. His Liverpool reputation now included two separate occasions on which shootout saves had changed the outcome of a defining match.

The final in Athens brought a different ending. Milan won 2–1 on 23 May, with Filippo Inzaghi scoring both their goals. The first came when Andrea Pirlo's free-kick struck the forward; the second followed a run through and a finish after he had gone past Reina. Kuyt's late reply could not recover the trophy.

For Reina, Athens also carried a family connection: his father, Miguel, had played in a European Cup final for Atlético Madrid. Both finished on the losing side. That parallel adds poignancy without explaining the result. Liverpool's disappointment arose from a tightly contested match, and Reina's earlier contribution to reaching it remained substantial. European football had given him triumph at Anfield and frustration at the final hurdle within the same month.

## A team capable of challenging

The 2008–09 campaign brought Liverpool close to the league championship. Reina kept twenty Premier League clean sheets, matching his first-season total, as Benítez's side pursued Manchester United. Their 4–1 victory at Old Trafford in March demonstrated how formidable they could be. United ultimately held them off, leaving the challenge as an achievement accompanied by an obvious absence.

That absence matters to Reina's biography. His strongest years at Liverpool did not culminate in a league winner's medal. Judging him only by that outcome would overlook the contribution that helped make the challenge possible; describing the period solely through clean sheets would overlook how much remained unfulfilled. His career sits between those two truths, a goalkeeper of high quality in a side whose ambition exceeded its eventual collection of trophies.

International football presented another contrast. Reina started Spain's 2–1 win over Greece at Euro 2008, sharing the side with Liverpool teammates Álvaro Arbeloa and Xabi Alonso, who captained it that evening. Yet Iker Casillas remained Spain's first-choice goalkeeper. Reina belonged to the squads that won the 2008 and 2012 European Championships and the 2010 World Cup, while his status for his country differed from his almost automatic selection at Anfield.

## Remaining through change

In April 2010 Reina signed a new six-year Liverpool contract. He spoke of his family's happiness and his children's developing Scouse accents. The agreement was a public commitment at a time when another major European occasion was approaching: Liverpool had reached the Europa League semi-finals. Later that month, after a 1–0 defeat at Atlético Madrid, Reina assessed the difficulty of reversing the tie and the danger of leaving space for counterattacks. Affection for the club coexisted with a realistic view of its immediate problems.

Liverpool supporters voted him their Player of the Season for 2009–10. That recognition marked his personal importance in a campaign that had fallen below the team's recent standards. Benítez's departure then changed the managerial relationship under which Reina had established himself, and the goalkeeper continued through Roy Hodgson's tenure and Kenny Dalglish's return.

The period contained mistakes as well as loyalty. In Hodgson's first league match, against Arsenal in August 2010, Reina's late own goal turned a prospective Liverpool victory into a 1–1 draw. Ngog had put ten-man Liverpool ahead after Joe Cole's dismissal, but the keeper fumbled the ball over his line after it came back from the post. It was a painful ending for a player whose previous consistency had made him a source of reassurance.

Dalglish's side won the League Cup in February 2012. Cardiff City forced the Wembley final to penalties after a 2–2 draw following extra time. Liverpool prevailed as Cardiff missed three of their kicks. This time Reina watched opponents send their attempts wide or against the woodwork, and celebrated another trophy with teammates who had endured a tense afternoon at Wembley.

A little over a month later, frustration had consequences. Reina was sent off during a 2–0 defeat at Newcastle after an altercation with James Perch. With Liverpool's substitutes used, José Enrique finished in goal. Reina's three-match ban included the FA Cup semi-final against Everton. A player usually associated with dependable availability had made himself unavailable for an important occasion.

## An unsettled farewell

Reina remained Liverpool's goalkeeper during Brendan Rodgers' first season, but the summer of 2013 brought Simon Mignolet and a change in direction. On 20 July the club confirmed Reina's season-long loan to Napoli, where he would work with Benítez again. His Liverpool playing career had ended, although his permanent departure was still a year away.

His farewell letter made clear that he was disappointed by how the move had been handled. In Reina's account, Liverpool agreed the loan before telling him. He acknowledged having asked the club to consider Barcelona if an offer came, but said he had also been willing to stay and extend his contract if that opportunity failed to materialise. These were his publicly stated views of a contested departure, rather than a complete record of the discussions behind it.

The letter also expressed enduring affection for the club and city. His family regarded Liverpool as home, and the difficult ending had not extinguished that attachment. On 8 August 2014 the club confirmed his permanent transfer to Bayern Munich. The definitive Liverpool total was 394 competitive appearances.

Reina's later career continued elsewhere until his retirement in 2025. At Liverpool, his legacy rests on a combination of repeatable excellence and moments that are still easy to picture: three saves in Cardiff, two against Chelsea, a goalkeeper racing towards Ngog in front of the Kop. The club records 177 clean sheets from his appearances. Behind that figure was a player who helped make Liverpool secure, helped them use the ball, recovered on some of their most demanding afternoons and came to feel their victories personally.
