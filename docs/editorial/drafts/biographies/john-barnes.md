# John Barnes

John Barnes gave Liverpool an attacker who could beat a defender, make the next pass and finish the move himself. At his best, those abilities helped Kenny Dalglish build a side that won two league championships in three seasons. When injury took away his acceleration, Barnes found another way to contribute. His ten years at Anfield encompassed both the attacking football of the late 1980s and the less successful attempts to recover that supremacy in the following decade.

Born in Kingston, Jamaica, on 7 November 1963, Barnes moved to England with his family in 1976. He established himself at Watford under Graham Taylor, helping them win promotion in 1982 and finish second to Liverpool the following season. By the time Dalglish signed him for £900,000 in June 1987, he was an experienced England international. Liverpool were buying a player already proven in the First Division.

Ian Rush’s departure for Juventus required a different attack. John Aldridge was already at Anfield, and Peter Beardsley joined during the same summer as Barnes. Their understanding developed quickly. Within nine minutes of his debut against Arsenal at Highbury, Barnes had supplied Aldridge with a goal.

His starting position on the left did not confine him to the touchline. In a later LFCHistory interview, Barnes described moving inside while Beardsley went wide, with Ray Houghton helping to preserve the midfield’s balance. Barnes could run at a full-back, but he could also receive the ball closer to goal and combine with the forwards. He recalled taking greater pleasure in creating a goal than scoring one.

The individual quality was evident against Queens Park Rangers in October 1987. Barnes scored twice in a 4–0 victory, including a solo goal begun around halfway. The collective quality was demonstrated against Nottingham Forest on 13 April 1988. Liverpool won 5–0; Barnes did not score, but supplied the goals for Houghton and Beardsley. He could be central to a heavy victory without appearing among the scorers.

Liverpool remained unbeaten through their first 29 league matches and finished nine points clear of Manchester United. Barnes contributed 15 league goals and won both the Professional Footballers’ Association and Football Writers’ Association player-of-the-year awards. The season ended with a disappointment: Wimbledon’s 1–0 FA Cup final victory prevented Liverpool completing the Double.

His achievements came amid persistent racism. At Goodison Park in 1988, he was photographed back-heeling a banana thrown onto the pitch. He had also experienced abuse from Liverpool supporters while playing for Watford, as he recalled in a 2018 interview with This Is Anfield. Barnes resisted the idea that being accepted as a successful Liverpool player demonstrated a wider defeat of prejudice. In his account, supporters applauding a black footballer who helped their team win was not proof that their attitudes towards black people had changed.

The following season brought the Hillsborough disaster and a return to Wembley against Everton. Liverpool won the 1989 FA Cup final 3–2 after extra time. Barnes supplied the left-wing cross from which Rush headed the winning goal. It was a decisive contribution to a match played only weeks after a disaster that ultimately claimed 97 lives.

Six days later, Arsenal took the league title at Anfield. Barnes lost possession attempting to beat Kevin Richardson before the move that ended with Michael Thomas scoring the decisive goal. In his later account to LFCHistory, Barnes acknowledged Ronnie Moran’s criticism that he should have taken the ball to the corner. He rejected, however, the notion that a championship could fairly be explained by one player’s decision in one match.

Barnes responded with his most productive scoring season. In 1989–90 he scored 22 league goals and 28 in all competitions, finishing as Liverpool’s leading scorer as they regained the championship. His work increasingly included playing through the middle alongside Rush. The Football Writers’ Association named him Footballer of the Year for a second time.

The early 1990s brought managerial change and serious injuries. Barnes contributed to Liverpool’s 1992 FA Cup campaign, scoring a hat-trick against Crewe Alexandra, but a calf injury kept him out of the final against Sunderland. That absence matters when describing his honours: he helped win the competition without playing in the Wembley victory.

Worse followed in June 1992, when he ruptured his right Achilles tendon playing for England against Finland. The injury reduced the acceleration on which so much of his attacking game depended. His return did not restore the winger Liverpool had signed, and the difficult seasons under Graeme Souness were followed by a change of role under Roy Evans.

In central midfield, Barnes used his close control and passing to keep possession and direct play. Jamie Redknapp operated alongside him, with Steve McManaman further forward. This arrangement gave Liverpool a use for Barnes’s judgement and technique after his ability to escape defenders at speed had diminished.

There was another trophy in 1995. Barnes passed to McManaman before the first of his two goals in the 2–1 League Cup final victory over Bolton Wanderers. It was Barnes’s last major honour at Liverpool. As captain, he led the team into the 1996 FA Cup final, which Manchester United won 1–0.

His place eventually became uncertain. Evans left him out of the second leg of the European Cup Winners’ Cup semi-final against Paris Saint-Germain in April 1997. That August he joined Newcastle United, working again with Dalglish. He later played for Charlton Athletic and managed Celtic, Jamaica and Tranmere Rovers.

Barnes left Liverpool with 108 goals in 407 competitive appearances; his league record was 84 in 314. Those totals encompass two distinct contributions. He helped decide championships as an attacking player, then adapted sufficiently to remain an important midfielder. The later years brought fewer trophies, but they demonstrated how much of his football survived the loss of his pace.
