# John Toshack: the intelligence behind Liverpool's famous partnership

The rain that stopped Liverpool's UEFA Cup final first leg in 1973 gave John Toshack an opportunity he had not been offered at kick-off. Bill Shankly had left him out against Borussia Mönchengladbach. When a waterlogged pitch forced an abandonment and the sides returned the following evening, Liverpool's manager changed his team. Toshack came in, and the German defence encountered a different problem.

His aerial presence helped Liverpool win 3–0. Kevin Keegan scored twice, with Toshack creating both goals, before Larry Lloyd added the third. A selection change had altered the shape of the final and brought Liverpool within reach of their first European trophy. For Toshack, it was a particularly clear demonstration of his worth: when Liverpool needed another way through, his ability to win a ball and make it useful could change the contest.

## Joining a team being remade

Toshack arrived at Anfield in November 1970, as Shankly's first great Liverpool side was giving way to its successor. The manager had begun replacing or phasing out familiar figures, including Roger Hunt, Ian St John, Tommy Lawrence and Ron Yeats. Players such as Ray Clemence, Emlyn Hughes and Larry Lloyd were becoming central to the new team. Toshack joined that process while the outcome was still uncertain.

He cost a club record £110,000 from Cardiff City. At 21, he brought a reputation developed in his home city and experience that extended beyond domestic football. Cardiff's Welsh Cup successes had taken them into Europe, and Toshack had played in the Cup Winners' Cup before moving to England's top division. Liverpool's contemporary annual presented their expensive new recruit as a young forward already accustomed to travel and senior competition.

The fee represented a significant investment in a player whose contribution would be judged immediately. His debut, at home to Coventry City on 14 November, ended 0–0. A week later the circumstances were far less forgiving: Everton came to Anfield, and Liverpool fell two goals behind in the second half. The new signing's introduction to the Merseyside derby became an introduction to the club's capacity for recovery.

Steve Heighway pulled a goal back before Toshack headed the equaliser towards the Kop. It was his first Liverpool goal, on his second appearance, and a direct expression of the aerial strength Shankly had bought. Chris Lawler then scored the winner, with Toshack involved in creating it. Liverpool had turned 0–2 into 3–2, and their new centre-forward had contributed as both scorer and provider.

That afternoon gave the relationship with the supporters a powerful beginning. It also established a theme of his career. Toshack could be the man at the end of an attack, but an equally valuable intervention might be the touch that allowed somebody else to finish it.

## Finding Keegan

Kevin Keegan's arrival from Scunthorpe United in the summer of 1971 created the partnership most closely associated with Toshack. Their different physical characteristics were obvious, but the understanding was more subtle than a tall forward simply knocking balls towards a smaller one. Keegan knew that Toshack could win the high ball; Toshack learned where his partner would move to receive it. The first contest could become the beginning of another chance.

In later recollections, Toshack described an immediate chemistry when Shankly paired them in training. Keegan likewise remembered anticipating the direction of Toshack's header and admiring his honesty as a teammate. Their mutual confidence made a familiar attacking pattern difficult to defend. An opponent might recognise the cross and the knock-down while still struggling to stop the runner arriving where the ball would fall.

Toshack's part in Liverpool's passing football was important too. He was powerful and imposing, but the team's play did not require him to spend every attack waiting beneath a high delivery. The club's retrospective assessment emphasises how naturally he fitted into its movement and combination play. His value lay in the judgement behind the touch as well as the strength required to win it.

Liverpool did not become champions as soon as the partnership formed. Toshack's first season had ended in defeat against Arsenal in the FA Cup final. He started at Wembley as Liverpool took the lead in extra time through Heighway, only for Arsenal to come back and win 2–1. The next season brought a narrow failure in the league, with the final match at Arsenal leaving Shankly's side short of the title. The new team had promise and painful evidence of what still separated promise from success.

## The first double

The breakthrough came in 1972–73. Liverpool ended a seven-year wait for a trophy by winning the league championship, then completed the season with the UEFA Cup. Toshack's availability was constrained by injuries, yet his contribution could still be decisive. His first honours were part of the wider confirmation that Shankly's reconstruction had produced another winning side.

The abandoned European final captured the particular threat Toshack offered. Shankly had been able to observe Mönchengladbach before choosing him for the restart. His introduction made Liverpool more dangerous in the air, and the partnership with Keegan converted that advantage into two goals. Liverpool also needed Clemence to save a Jupp Heynckes penalty to preserve the 3–0 lead. The attack had supplied a commanding score; the goalkeeper protected it from a potentially damaging away goal.

The return in Germany showed how necessary that margin had been. Heynckes scored twice before half-time, leaving Liverpool's aggregate advantage at a single goal. Shankly's side survived the second half and won the tie 3–2 overall. Toshack belonged to a first European triumph achieved through both the attacking force of the opening match and the resolve required to endure the second.

For the club, the double was a landmark. For the striker, it placed his partnership at the centre of a successful team rather than a promising one. The new Liverpool had acquired trophies to accompany the memories of near misses.

## Wembley and a threatened departure

Liverpool won the FA Cup the following year, beating Newcastle United 3–0 on 4 May 1974. Toshack had scored three times during the cup run. The final again illustrated how much his contribution could exceed the number beside his name on the scoresheet.

After Keegan put Liverpool ahead, Toshack's headed touch from Clemence's long kick allowed Heighway to run through and score the second. Keegan added a third after an accomplished passing move. Toshack did not score at Wembley, but he helped make an emphatic result possible, linking the goalkeeper's delivery with a teammate's finish. The attack could combine direct service and flowing football within the same performance.

It proved to be Shankly's last trophy as Liverpool manager. His resignation that July brought Bob Paisley into the job and changed the circumstances for Toshack. Ray Kennedy had also arrived from Arsenal. Competition, injury and the transition in management made the months that followed difficult for a player who had recently helped Liverpool win another major honour.

A proposed transfer to Leicester City in 1974 then collapsed when Toshack failed a medical because of a muscle injury. In his later account, he recalled being told his playing career might have little time left. The concern was personal as well as professional: he was a young man with a family, confronting uncertainty about the work on which his future depended.

He returned to Liverpool and changed his preparation, describing specialised training, swimming and shorter, sharper sessions. He also recalled feeling that Paisley was not entirely convinced about him. The failed move did not lead straight to the end of his career. Instead, Toshack found a way to continue, and the season that would become his most productive at Liverpool was still ahead.

## The best scoring season

In 1975–76 Toshack scored 23 goals in all competitions, his highest return for the club. Six came in the UEFA Cup, including a winner whose significance extended far beyond the early stage of the match in which it arrived.

Liverpool visited Barcelona's Camp Nou for the first time on 30 March 1976, in the first leg of the semi-final. Johan Cruyff and Johan Neeskens were among their opponents. Thirteen minutes into the game, Keegan supplied Toshack and the Welshman struck low past goalkeeper Pere Mora. This time the familiar providers' relationship worked in the opposite direction: Keegan made the opportunity, and Toshack finished it.

Liverpool defended the lead to win 1–0. The second leg finished 1–1 at Anfield, with Phil Thompson scoring before Barcelona replied, and Paisley's side reached the final 2–1 on aggregate. Toshack's Camp Nou goal had supplied the difference across two matches. His reputation as a target man could accommodate a winner on the ground against one of Europe's most prestigious opponents.

The league championship was settled on another demanding away night. At Wolves on 4 May, Liverpool trailed for much of the match as Queens Park Rangers waited for the outcome of the title race. Toshack helped create Keegan's equaliser with fourteen minutes left, then scored himself before Ray Kennedy completed a 3–1 victory. The partnership had supplied both the breakthrough and the assurance that followed it.

The result secured Paisley's first league title as manager. Toshack, who had feared for his future after the Leicester medical, was contributing at the point where an entire season could be decided. His recovery had delivered more than continued employment: it had helped Liverpool finish above their challengers.

The UEFA Cup final brought a reminder that his importance did not make his selection untouchable. Club Brugge led 2–0 at Anfield, and Paisley replaced Toshack with Jimmy Case at half-time. Liverpool turned the first leg round to win 3–2, with Case among the scorers. A 1–1 draw in Belgium completed a 4–3 aggregate victory and another league and UEFA Cup double.

Toshack had been essential to reaching that final, but Liverpool's response within it required a different arrangement. The contrast with 1973 is striking. Shankly's change had brought him into a final and transformed the attack; Paisley's change took him out and helped transform another. Both seasons ended with the same pair of trophies.

## The final he could not play

Liverpool's progress continued in 1976–77, with Toshack contributing thirteen goals as they pursued success in several competitions. They retained the league championship and won the European Cup for the first time. The crowning match in Rome, however, took place without him.

Toshack later identified missing the 1977 European Cup final as his greatest disappointment at Liverpool. An Achilles tendon injury prevented him from playing. His teammates defeated Mönchengladbach 3–1, reaching a summit towards which he had helped the club advance, while he could only watch. It was Keegan's final Liverpool appearance, so a partnership that had shaped six seasons ended with its two members experiencing Rome very differently.

That absence gives the ending of his Liverpool career its particular sadness. The European Cup belonged to the progress of the side he had joined during its rebuilding years, yet the player could not experience the defining match from the pitch. Success for Liverpool and frustration for Toshack arrived together.

His appearances became limited in the following season. The last came against Bristol City at Anfield on 19 November 1977, a 1–1 draw in which he created Kenny Dalglish's goal. Even at the end, the record contained the familiar contribution: somebody else's finish made possible by Toshack.

Early in 1978 he left for Swansea City as player-manager. The role opened a different career and carried him back to south Wales. Swansea rose from the Fourth Division to the First, winning promotions in 1978, 1979 and 1981. The footballing education Toshack had received at Liverpool now informed his work with another team.

## Returning with Liverpool beneath the tracksuit

On 3 October 1981 Toshack returned to Anfield as Swansea's manager, soon after Shankly's death. Before a match that ended 2–2, he revealed a Liverpool shirt beneath his Swansea tracksuit during the tribute to his former manager. He later explained the gesture as a personal acknowledgement of the man to whom he owed so much.

It was an enduring expression of a connection that had survived his departure. Toshack's managerial career would take him well beyond Wales, but he continued to credit the grounding supplied by Shankly and Paisley. Liverpool remained part of the way he understood the game.

His playing record for the club was 247 appearances and 96 goals. Those figures describe a substantial scorer; they cannot fully describe the spaces he opened, the balls he redirected or the understanding he shared with Keegan. Toshack helped Liverpool turn a rebuilding project into a team that could win at home and in Europe. His strength was visible. The intelligence that made it useful was just as important.
