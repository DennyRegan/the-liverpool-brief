# Gordon Hodgson: the goals that sustained Liverpool between the wars

Gordon Hodgson scored four times at Hillsborough on 14 February 1931, turning a difficult afternoon against Sheffield Wednesday into a 5–3 Liverpool victory. Three of his goals came after half-time, including a penalty, and the last settled a match in which the home side had twice drawn level. It was the outstanding away performance of a season that brought him 36 league goals. Liverpool finished ninth; their leading scorer had reached a standard the club would not surpass for three decades.

That difference between individual achievement and the team’s position runs through Hodgson’s Liverpool career. He scored 241 goals in 377 competitive appearances, 233 of them in the First Division. He won no major trophy at Anfield. His contribution lay instead in sustaining an attack through changing personnel and uneven seasons, sometimes lifting Liverpool towards the top of the table, sometimes providing urgently needed points much lower down it.

Hodgson was born in South Africa on 16 April 1904, to English parents. He played for several clubs there before joining Transvaal, and visited Britain with a South African touring team in 1924. On 1 October the visitors beat Liverpool 5–2 at Anfield. Hodgson’s performance attracted attention. The tour also introduced Liverpool to goalkeeper Arthur Riley; both would become substantial figures in the club’s interwar team.

He joined Liverpool in December 1925 and made his competitive debut under Matt McQueen on 27 February 1926, in a 1–1 draw with Manchester City at Maine Road. His first goals followed on 10 March at Old Trafford, where he scored twice in a 3–3 draw with Manchester United. Four goals in 12 league appearances constituted the opening instalment of a career whose scoring soon became much more regular.

The side he entered still contained players from Liverpool’s consecutive championship teams of 1921–22 and 1922–23. In attack, Dick Forshaw and Harry Chambers offered considerable experience and goals of their own. Hodgson could play in the centre, as he did on his debut, but inside-right became a familiar position. The distinction matters: his prolific record was not simply the work of a player stationed at centre-forward throughout his Liverpool years.

His first Liverpool hat-trick came against Sheffield United at Anfield on 11 September 1926, in his seventeenth appearance. Liverpool won 5–1, with Forshaw supplying the other two goals. The line-up placed Hodgson between Jimmy Walsh on the right wing and Forshaw in the middle, with Chambers at inside-left. It was an attack with several routes to goal, and Hodgson had already shown how decisively he could contribute from his place within it.

Another treble, against Derby County on 22 January 1927, demonstrated the speed with which he could change a match. Hodgson scored in the third, fifteenth and twenty-fifth minutes, giving Liverpool a 3–0 interval lead. Derby recovered two goals in the second half, leaving his early burst to account for the whole of Liverpool’s winning score. Eighteen goals in all competitions in 1926–27 became 23 the following season and 32 in 1928–29.

Liverpool finished fifth in that third full campaign, scoring 90 league goals. Hodgson supplied 30 of them. The team’s improvement did not mean every attacking problem had been solved. A Daily Mail assessment before the next season praised Liverpool’s purposeful play but described Hodgson’s use at centre-forward as a successful temporary expedient. It still wanted a specialist for the position. His adaptability helped cover a need; it did not remove the wider question of how Liverpool should arrange their forwards.

The following campaign brought a fall to 14 goals. Hodgson’s output could fluctuate, and the recovery was emphatic rather than inevitable. In 1930–31 he scored 36 times in 40 league appearances, all his goals that season coming in the league. Three home hat-tricks and the four-goal afternoon at Wednesday made this a year of exceptional peaks as well as sustained production. His league total established a Liverpool record, later exceeded by Roger Hunt’s 41 in 1961–62.

At Hillsborough, Dave Wright put Liverpool ahead before Wednesday equalised. Hodgson restored the lead before the interval, but the hosts drew level again early in the second half. Two more Hodgson goals, the second from the penalty spot, made it 4–2. Wednesday reduced the deficit before his fourth completed Liverpool’s scoring. The sequence gives the performance its force: he supplied new leads, a margin of safety and the final answer to another attempt at recovery.

He ended the season with both goals in a 2–0 home win over West Ham United on 2 May. Yet Liverpool’s final league figures were 86 scored and 85 conceded. Ninth place reflected a team whose attacking success did not consistently translate into control of matches. Hodgson’s record belonged within that uneven collective performance. The importance of his goals was clear without pretending that one forward could repair every weakness elsewhere.

His own explanation of shooting gives a more useful account of his craft than numbers alone. Writing in the Exeter and Plymouth Gazette in September 1930, Hodgson argued that a player with a clear opening should be prepared to shoot instead of passing responsibility to someone else. The decision required judgement, but hesitation could waste the opportunity. He favoured placing the ball effectively over trying to strike it with the greatest possible force.

He also stressed composure and the ability to use either foot. A shot from distance might fail, but it could still trouble the goalkeeper or produce a rebound; the mere possibility of failure was insufficient reason to abandon a good chance. These were practical observations from a player approaching his most productive season. They describe a forward concerned with recognising an opening, making a decision and giving the shot its best chance of success.

The same period brought his England recognition. Hodgson had represented South Africa before moving to Liverpool, and his English parentage also qualified him for England. He earned three England caps between October 1930 and March 1931, scoring against Wales in a 4–0 victory in November. All three appearances came while he was a Liverpool player. It was a brief international career beside the scale of his club service, but it coincided with his exceptional scoring season.

He remained productive after that peak. His next four campaigns brought 27, 24, 25 and 29 goals in all competitions. Most of his Liverpool matches were played under George Patterson, who succeeded McQueen, and the dependable scoring continued as the earlier championship generation gave way. Hodgson’s value was increasingly that of an established forward around whom an attack could be assembled, with the record showing substantial contributions year after year rather than one isolated great season.

In March 1934, the urgency was different from a championship challenge. Liverpool were nineteenth after beating Birmingham 4–1 at Anfield on the seventeenth; Birmingham stood twenty-first. Hodgson scored all four. He cancelled an early visiting goal, put Liverpool ahead just after half-time and then added two more. The performance came in a struggle near the relegation places, giving his finishing immediate practical importance beyond its place in the season’s totals.

He scored twice more in Liverpool’s final match of that campaign, a 3–2 home victory over Manchester City on 2 May. Before the game, Elisha Scott addressed the supporters as he prepared to leave the club. Hodgson and Riley, the two players Liverpool had recruited following the South African tour, were in the starting side as another part of the earlier team’s history came to an end. Liverpool’s fortunes had changed, but Hodgson was still supplying goals.

His sporting life extended well beyond football. Between 1928 and 1933 he played 56 first-class matches for Lancashire, taking 148 wickets as a fast bowler. Those summers overlapped his Liverpool career. In May 1935 the Dundee Courier reported his arrival as Forfarshire’s professional and described his continued practice at Old Trafford. The footballer’s substantial physical presence and the bowler’s attention to speed and spin belonged to the same unusually accomplished sportsman.

Hodgson’s final Liverpool appearance came in the 3–2 home defeat by Chelsea on 28 December 1935. In January 1936, aged 31, he moved to Aston Villa. The departure closed a decade in which his competitive total had reached 241, with eight FA Cup goals added to his 233 in the league. Aston Villa and then Leeds United provided further work in English football; a contemporary report announced his Leeds signing in March 1937 and noted his ability to fill the inside-forward positions.

After the Second World War he became Port Vale’s manager. He was still in charge when he died on 14 June 1951, aged 47. The contemporary Daily Mail obituary identified a former Liverpool forward and England international whose football career had carried on from playing into management. His life had ended early, after achievements in two professional sports, but his Liverpool scoring record would continue to supply a point of comparison for later generations.

Hodgson recorded 17 Liverpool hat-tricks, alongside the repeated seasonal contributions that made his career more than a collection of spectacular afternoons. The 36-goal league season and the performances against Wednesday and Birmingham show different uses of the same ability: scoring at a record pace, winning an open away match and strengthening a team under pressure near the bottom. Liverpool’s interwar history contained little silverware during his service, but it contained a forward whose work gave it many of its outstanding moments.
