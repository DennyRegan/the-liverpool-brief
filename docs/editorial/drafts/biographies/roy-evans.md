# Roy Evans: the Liverpool apprenticeship and the unfinished return to the top

When Liverpool appointed Roy Evans as reserve-team trainer in August 1974, chairman John Smith identified a future beyond the immediate vacancy. He believed the young coach might eventually become Liverpool's manager. Evans was 25, had made only eleven competitive first-team appearances and had required persuasion to stop playing. The prediction was remarkable because the coaching career on which it depended had barely begun.

Almost twenty years later, on 31 January 1994, Evans succeeded Graeme Souness. Between those appointments lay the most productive period in Liverpool's history, much of it spent learning and working beside men whose names became inseparable from the club. Evans reached the manager's office with an unusual qualification: he understood Liverpool from the reserves, the training ground, the treatment room and the bench. His story cannot be adequately measured by the trophy he won after taking charge.

That trophy, the 1995 League Cup, nevertheless matters. So do the attacking teams that followed, the championship opportunities they missed and the failed joint-management arrangement that ended his Anfield employment in November 1998. Evans helped Liverpool recover a compelling way of playing without recovering the domestic supremacy he had known as a coach. The distance between those two achievements defines his years as manager.

## A Liverpool boy learning his place

Born in Bootle on 4 October 1948, Evans supported Liverpool before he represented them. He later remembered visiting the Boys' Pen while his father stood on the Kop, watching a club still in the Second Division. His own football took him through Bootle Boys, Lancashire Boys and England Schoolboys. Clubs outside Merseyside were interested, but his preference was already established. Liverpool was a sporting ambition and a family attachment before it became an employer.

He entered the club's youth system under Bill Shankly and signed professional forms in October 1965. The route to first-team football was slow. Liverpool had experienced defenders, and Evans spent years in the reserves before an injury to Geoff Strong created an opening. His debut came at Anfield on 16 March 1970, in a 3–0 victory over Sheffield Wednesday. He had reached the team he had watched as a child; remaining there proved more difficult.

His eleven appearances comprised nine league games, one League Cup tie and one Fairs Cup match. Alec Lindsay's conversion to left-back helped close the route to a regular position. There were no senior appearances in 1971–72 or 1972–73. It would be misleading to describe Evans as a central playing figure in Shankly's championship-winning renewal. His place in that wider Liverpool world was real, but the first-team contribution was small.

A loan with Philadelphia Atoms in 1973 offered a different experience. Evans recalled being selected when coach Al Miller visited Merseyside looking for players and spending four months in America with a side that won the North American Soccer League championship. He returned to Liverpool and played twice that December. The Boxing Day visit to Burnley was his last competitive appearance for the Reds, although a much longer career at the club was about to begin.

## The decision that made the career

Paisley offered Evans the reserve-team job after succeeding Shankly in 1974. Evans did not immediately accept. He still wanted to play, and moving elsewhere might have allowed him to do so. Conversations with Paisley, Joe Fagan, Ronnie Moran and Tommy Smith helped persuade him that coaching offered a future at Liverpool. It was a decision about what kind of football life he wanted, taken before there was evidence that he could succeed in the new role.

The contemporary announcement gives a clearer account of that opportunity than hindsight alone. Paisley praised Evans' understanding of the game and ability to guide players; Smith stressed the importance of keeping changes within the club. Liverpool were making an investment in somebody they knew, rather than rewarding a decorated first-team career. Evans' limited playing record had not prevented his colleagues from recognising qualities that might be more useful off the pitch.

The reserves won seven Central League titles in nine years under him. Those championships had a practical purpose beyond adding silverware: the reserve team gave younger players a demanding environment in which to develop and senior players somewhere to regain fitness. Evans later recalled having control of the side while accommodating Paisley's need to give returning first-team players match practice. Different stages of a player's career could meet within the same team.

That arrangement also made learning reciprocal. Youngsters could play alongside accomplished seniors instead of encountering them only in training. Evans identified Sammy Lee as a particularly successful player to emerge from his reserves. The coach's contribution was to help prepare players to meet Liverpool's standards, with neither the reserves' results nor the first team's reputation sufficient on its own. Winning below senior level mattered most when it supported the strength of the club above it.

## Work inside the Boot Room

The Boot Room became shorthand for a successful system of succession, but Evans' descriptions emphasised the people and their differences. Moran could be forceful and demanding; Fagan was direct and trusted; Paisley was an exceptional judge of players. Evans' own manner was often more reassuring. These were complementary ways of communicating, held together by a manager who made the final decision and colleagues who could contribute to it.

Evans regarded Fagan as his most important football mentor. In a later Liverpool interview, he described a man whose authority came from honesty and the respect he commanded. A telling-off carried weight because players understood that it was deserved. Evans took from him an approach to management that allowed firmness and personal consideration to coexist. The quieter member of a coaching team could still be somebody players listened to carefully.

His recollections of Moran offer another useful detail. Coaches were expected to perform their own jobs well, rather than manoeuvre for somebody else's position. The spirit of cooperation was sustained through daily responsibilities, not merely a shared affection for Liverpool. Evans could offer encouragement while Moran insisted on standards, and both could serve successive managers. Continuity required people willing to contribute without receiving the largest share of public attention.

When Paisley retired in 1983, Evans moved into first-team coaching under Fagan. Liverpool won the league, League Cup and European Cup in 1983–84. Evans was part of the staff behind that achievement; Fagan was the manager. The distinction is essential. His later managerial honours do not include the European Cups and championships won while he worked under others, even though those years are indispensable to understanding his importance to Liverpool.

Kenny Dalglish's appointment in 1985 brought another change in authority. Evans remained, working through the period that produced the 1986 league and FA Cup double and further championships. He had already experienced a senior colleague becoming manager under Paisley and Fagan. Dalglish's move from the pitch placed a different kind of leader above him, but the accumulated coaching experience continued to support the team.

The same long service placed him within Liverpool during Hillsborough and its aftermath. In his own later account, Evans described the journey home in numbness and the meetings with bereaved families at Anfield, alongside attendance at funerals. His recollection was about people and responsibilities beyond football. The relationship between club and supporters demanded care when ordinary sporting concerns had lost their significance. That experience belongs to his Liverpool life without being turned into an explanation for results or a device for embellishing them.

## Taking responsibility for the first team

Evans continued on the staff when Souness arrived in 1991 and became his assistant before succeeding him. The appointment restored a manager whose career had been formed inside Liverpool. It did not restore the conditions under which Paisley or Fagan had inherited winning teams. Liverpool had lost ground, and Manchester United had become the leading domestic force. Evans had to improve the side in front of him rather than simply reproduce the achievements of his teachers.

His first match was a 2–2 draw at Norwich on 5 February 1994. The line-up contained senior figures such as Ian Rush, John Barnes and Ronnie Whelan alongside Steve McManaman and Dominic Matteo. This mixture captured the transitional nature of the task. Evans knew the players and the institution intimately, but familiarity did not tell him automatically which combination could make Liverpool successful again.

The team finished eighth that season. In the summer he began making more substantial changes, especially in defence. Phil Babb arrived from Coventry and John Scales from Wimbledon in September 1994, joining Neil Ruddock among the central-defensive options. The spending was significant, but the intended change was also structural. Evans wanted three centre-backs and introduced a 3-5-2 system that gave the wide players different responsibilities.

Scales later recalled feeling comfortable on the right or in the middle of that defensive line, while stressing that the team had to work to understand it. Rob Jones similarly described the demands of wing-back: more movement along the flank and less of the narrow positioning associated with conventional full-back duties. Evans' football was therefore not simply a release of gifted attackers. He was trying to build a shape in which they could flourish and the defence could receive sufficient protection.

That balance remained elusive over the longer term. Scales started well but was subsequently troubled by declining form and injury, leaving for Tottenham in 1996. The early promise of a signing and its eventual contribution were different questions. Evans' defensive purchases demonstrated that he recognised the problem; they did not ensure that he would solve it permanently.

## Wembley gave the revival substance

The first full season produced fourth place and a League Cup final against Bolton Wanderers on 2 April 1995. Liverpool had beaten strong top-flight opponents on the way, including Arsenal and Blackburn. Bolton, though then playing in the division below, had reached Wembley on merit and would soon be promoted. The final was an opportunity Liverpool were expected to take, which brought a pressure of its own.

McManaman supplied the decisive quality. Before half-time he received possession from Barnes and worked through the defence to score. His second came from a longer run after the interval, carrying the ball into a position from which he could finish low beyond Keith Branagan. The freedom and confidence with which he moved gave Evans' attacking ambition a particularly clear expression. Liverpool had a player capable of deciding a major match through individual invention.

Redknapp's involvement in the second goal provides a useful corrective to remembering the final as a completely solitary performance. He beat an opponent deep in Liverpool's half and passed to McManaman. The subsequent run was extraordinary, but possession first had to reach the player who could exploit it. Alan Thompson's reply for Bolton then required Liverpool to protect a 2–1 advantage rather than celebrate prematurely.

They did, securing the club's fifth League Cup. For Evans, the reserve-team championships and years of supporting other managers had now been followed by a major senior trophy won with him in charge. The result gave his restoration project substance. It also raised the standard against which the following seasons would be judged: Liverpool supporters could now look beyond improvement towards a sustained challenge for the championship.

## Goals, exhilaration and an uncomfortable comparison

Stan Collymore joined from Nottingham Forest in the summer of 1995 for a club-record £8.5 million. The intention was ambitious and readily understood: pair his pace and scoring with Fowler's exceptional finishing. Collymore scored 19 goals in his first Liverpool season and 16 in his second. Across those two campaigns, he and Fowler contributed 102 goals between them. The attacking partnership delivered far more than a memorable transfer announcement.

Its most famous evening came against Newcastle at Anfield on 3 April 1996. Fowler scored twice and Collymore twice in a 4–3 victory, with the final goal arriving in stoppage time. Liverpool led, fell behind, recovered and had to recover again. It remains a defining image of the Evans team because its best qualities were on such vivid display: movement, enterprise and the refusal to stop seeking another goal.

The exhilaration also contained a warning. Liverpool had needed four goals to win. A performance could be irresistible as an occasion and still leave a manager questioning how reliably it could support a championship campaign. Evans' later assessment of his team acknowledged that attacking strength sometimes encouraged them to believe they could outscore any opponent. His responsibility included the choice to pursue that approach, not only admiration for the players who made it exciting.

Third place represented another improvement in 1995–96, but the season ended with a 1–0 FA Cup final defeat by Manchester United. Eric Cantona's late goal settled an unproductive afternoon for Liverpool. The cream suits worn before kick-off became an enduring shorthand for a team whose presentation appeared more conspicuous than its performance. They remain part of the memory because Liverpool lost a match in which the stakes were so clear.

The clothes do not explain how the final was lost. Nor does the label attached to the players establish that they never cared about winning. Evans defended their desire to succeed while acknowledging that their promise had not been fully realised. The stronger criticism concerns the football: a talented side had failed to impose itself when an important trophy was available. Its image made that failure easier to caricature, but the failure itself required no caricature.

## The challenge that Liverpool could not finish

In 1996–97 Liverpool again moved into serious contention, then lost the chance to make their strengths decisive. On 6 April, Coventry won 2–1 at Anfield after Fowler had put Liverpool ahead. Noel Whelan equalised and Dion Dublin scored at the end. Liverpool had created opportunities, including a late opening for Collymore, but neither their attacking talent nor their lead insulated them from a damaging defeat.

The opportunity was concrete. Manchester United had lost to Derby the previous day; a Liverpool win would have put the two clubs level on points, with Liverpool holding the superior goal difference. Instead, the gap remained three points. The frustration came from a position available to be taken and an opponent Liverpool might reasonably have expected to beat. It was a championship chance lost within an ordinary home fixture.

Europe exposed another weakness that month. Paris Saint-Germain defeated Liverpool 3–0 in the Cup Winners' Cup semi-final first leg. Evans' contemporary response criticised the whole side for failing to attack or defend adequately. The criticism was forceful, complicating the familiar picture of a manager too mild ever to confront his players. His complaint also acknowledged something more serious than an unlucky scoreline: Liverpool had been outplayed.

The return at Anfield brought a 2–0 win, through Fowler and Mark Wright, but no aggregate equaliser. Liverpool recovered pride and threatened a comeback without reaching the final. Manchester United's 3–1 league victory at Anfield between the two European legs further damaged the title challenge. The season ended in fourth place. Liverpool's capacity to produce a response had survived; their capacity to avoid first needing one had not become dependable enough.

## New players, divided authority and departure

Evans' final full season offered both renewal and further disappointment. Paul Ince arrived from Internazionale in 1997 and became captain, adding an experienced central midfielder with a different presence from Liverpool's creative players. Youth continued to matter. Carragher had made his debut in January 1997; Owen scored on his first appearance that May and then produced 23 goals in all competitions during 1997–98. Evans was giving opportunities to players who would shape Liverpool beyond his tenure.

Third place in 1997–98 kept Liverpool among the leading sides without delivering the championship. The board sought a different structure. On 16 July 1998 Gérard Houllier joined Evans as joint-manager, bringing experience from French football into an arrangement Liverpool had never previously used. The prospect of another experienced figure helping the club did not resolve the fundamental problem of sharing the final authority.

In his later account, Evans regretted accepting the joint title. He described players hearing different answers and decisions becoming difficult even when the two managers had initially found agreement. Their problem was not adequately explained as personal hostility. A football team and its staff needed to know who had the deciding voice, while the structure offered two men occupying the same position.

Results deteriorated after a promising opening to the league campaign. On 10 November Tottenham won 3–1 at Anfield in the League Cup, with former Liverpool defender Scales among the scorers. Contemporary reporting recorded Evans' concern about the team's situation and its repeated habit of conceding an early advantage. That was the last of the eighteen competitive matches played under joint management.

Evans left on 12 November. At the departure press conference he explained that another position at Liverpool had been offered, but he wanted to give Houllier the freedom to proceed. He did not present the change as a quarrel between two personalities. The arrangement had failed, and remaining around the club in a different capacity could complicate the next manager's work. Walking away was painful precisely because Liverpool had supplied almost his entire professional life.

## The value of the whole service

Evans' managerial record comprises 226 competitive matches in sole charge and eighteen alongside Houllier, a total of 244. His only major trophy as Liverpool manager was the 1995 League Cup. Those facts establish the limit of the senior managerial achievement without reducing his long service to it. The reserve-team championships, first-team coaching and work alongside Paisley, Fagan and Dalglish belong to a different category of contribution.

His later career included work at Fulham, Swindon and with Wales under John Toshack. Liverpool remained the association by which he was chiefly known. The players who developed during his management, the quality of the attacking football and the affection surrounding the man endured beyond the disappointment of his departure. None requires pretending that the championship had been won or that one trophy met Liverpool's ambitions.

Evans' career reveals how much a club can owe to somebody whose most celebrated job was also his most exposed. As a coach he helped sustain success whose public credit went principally elsewhere. As manager he accepted the responsibility for converting promise into trophies and did not convert enough of it. Remembering both gives him a more substantial place in Liverpool's history than either the romance of the Boot Room or the missed chances of the 1990s can offer alone.
