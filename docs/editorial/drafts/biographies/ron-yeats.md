# Ron Yeats: the captain at the beginning

Ron Yeats arrived at a Liverpool still trying to escape the Second Division. Within four years he had captained the club to promotion, the championship of England and its first FA Cup. Those achievements explain his importance more clearly than the stories about his size. Bill Shankly needed a defence on which he could build; Yeats became its centre and the captain who represented the team as Liverpool recovered its place in the game.

Born in Aberdeen on 15 November 1937, Yeats came through Aberdeen Lads Club before joining Dundee United in December 1957. His early working life included an apprenticeship as a slaughterman. Professional football took him into another demanding routine: during national service, he travelled back to play for United, whose manager Jerry Kerr secured his release from military duties whenever possible.

That arrangement mattered during United’s promotion season of 1959–60. Yeats missed only four matches across the campaign and followed it with a season in Scotland’s top division. There had been an interruption through injury the previous year, but by the time Liverpool approached he had experienced both the work of winning promotion and the demands of staying in a higher division.

Shankly signed him in July 1961, the same summer that Ian St John arrived from Motherwell. The two Scots gave the rebuilding team strength at opposite ends of the pitch. The manager’s description of Yeats as a “colossus” became inseparable from his name, but Liverpool still had to turn that confidence into results. Promotion had repeatedly eluded the club before their arrival.

Yeats played 41 league games in 1961–62 and took over the captaincy during that first season. Liverpool won the Second Division championship. His influence continued after promotion: by 1963–64, the team had won the First Division title, finishing four points ahead of Manchester United. The change from a club trying to get out of the second tier to champions had taken only three seasons of his Liverpool career.

His defending was direct, and his own description of it was modest. In a later interview with LFChistory, Yeats emphasised his left foot, his ability to read the game and the way he and Tommy Smith complemented one another. Smith supplied the right-sided balance. Yeats did not present himself as a complete footballer; he understood the work he could do and the help a partner provided. That practical account is more revealing than treating his physical presence as an explanation for everything.

The championship opened European football to Liverpool, and Yeats was involved in some of the first campaign’s defining moments. He modelled the red shorts with which Shankly wanted to change the team’s appearance. Liverpool wore them with red shirts against Anderlecht at Anfield on 25 November 1964, when Yeats was among the scorers in a 3–0 win. White socks remained that night. Red socks completed the transformation for the return match in Belgium on 16 December.

In March 1965, the European Cup quarter-final against Cologne required a different kind of contribution from the captain. After two goalless matches, a replay in Rotterdam finished 2–2 after extra time. A toss decided the tie. Yeats recalled that the first attempt left the coin standing in the turf and that a second fell in Liverpool’s favour. Accounts differ over the precise object used, but the outcome is clear: Liverpool advanced to the semi-finals. Their first European season had carried them within one round of the final.

The central achievement of that spring came at Wembley on 1 May. Liverpool’s previous FA Cup finals, in 1914 and 1950, had ended in defeat. Against Leeds United, another goalless 90 minutes took the match into extra time. Roger Hunt scored, Billy Bremner equalised and St John headed in Ian Callaghan’s cross for a 2–1 victory. Yeats became the first Liverpool captain to receive the trophy. It was a new honour for the club, won by a side whose captain had joined while it was still outside the top division.

He was ever-present across the 42 league matches as Liverpool regained the championship in 1965–66. European success proved less straightforward. On 5 May 1966, Yeats led the team against Borussia Dortmund in the Cup Winners’ Cup final at Hampden Park. Hunt cancelled out Sigfried Held’s opening goal, but Dortmund won 2–1 after extra time. Reinhard Libuda’s decisive effort struck the post and went into the net off Yeats as he tried to recover. The German records credit Libuda with the goal. For Liverpool’s captain, the first European final ended in an especially painful piece of defending.

The years after that championship brought continued service rather than another major trophy. Yeats made at least 48 competitive appearances in each of the next four seasons. His international record was much shorter: he won two Scotland caps. At Liverpool, the length of his involvement allowed the first Shankly side to remain recognisable even as the next generation began to arrive.

Eventually that continuity became harder to sustain. The FA Cup defeat at Watford in February 1970 exposed the need for renewal, and Yeats was among the established players gradually displaced. Back trouble was a further difficulty, while Larry Lloyd provided a younger option in central defence. Yeats appeared only 16 times in 1970–71. The reduced role was part of a wider change involving other players who had helped Liverpool win its earliest Shankly honours.

He left for Tranmere Rovers late in 1971 after 454 competitive Liverpool appearances and 16 goals. More than 400 of those games had been as captain. Tranmere gave him the responsibilities of a player-manager, and his side beat Arsenal at Highbury in the 1973 League Cup. Mark Palios, whom Yeats signed as a professional, later remembered his importance in giving young players their start. His work across the Mersey therefore had a lasting value of its own, beyond the distinguished Liverpool name on the touchline.

Further playing spells took him into non-league and American football. In 1986, Kenny Dalglish brought him back to Liverpool as chief scout. That second career at Anfield lasted until 2006, twice as long as his decade as a player. John Aldridge later credited Yeats with watching him at Oxford United and helping make his move to Liverpool possible.

Sami Hyypia was another significant recommendation. Yeats explained that he watched the defender after receiving a recommendation, admired his performance and asked for a manager or coach to see him. The account describes a scouting process involving several people, with Yeats providing an informed assessment at an important stage. His contribution to Liverpool’s defending had moved from playing alongside colleagues to helping identify their successors.

Yeats died on 6 September 2024, aged 86, after living with Alzheimer’s disease. The club mourned a former captain whose service had extended well beyond the years of the first Shankly team. He had helped Liverpool make the essential first steps: promotion, a league title and an FA Cup. His later scouting work made his connection to what followed practical as well as ceremonial.
