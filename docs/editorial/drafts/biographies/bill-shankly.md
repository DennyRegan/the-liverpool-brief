# Bill Shankly: the making of modern Liverpool

Liverpool’s modern history is inseparable from Bill Shankly because his achievement reached into almost everything the club did. The promotion, three league championships, two FA Cups and UEFA Cup provide its clearest record. Behind those honours lay the reconstruction of a team, the improvement of its training ground and the development of a relationship with supporters that outlasted his employment. When Shankly left in 1974, Liverpool possessed both a successful side and people capable of carrying the work forward.

He had arrived on 1 December 1959 with Liverpool in the Second Division. His first match in charge, against Cardiff City at Anfield on 19 December, ended in a 4–0 defeat. The distance between that beginning and the football Liverpool played in the 1974 FA Cup final explains more about his importance than any collection of celebrated sayings. That distance had to be covered through recruitment, coaching and difficult decisions, over nearly fifteen years.

Shankly was born in Glenbuck, Ayrshire, on 2 September 1913. Coal mining and football shaped the village in which he grew up, and he followed his four brothers into the professional game. Those origins remained central to his public identity. The conviction that people could accomplish more by working together found a practical expression in the teams he built and in his attachment to the people who watched them.

His playing career took him to Carlisle United in 1932 and Preston North End the following year. A right-half, he helped Preston gain promotion and played in the FA Cup finals of 1937 and 1938, losing to Sunderland before beating Huddersfield Town. He also became a Scotland international. War interrupted his career; he served in the RAF, and by the resumption of League football in 1946 he was already in his thirties. The next phase of his working life began at Carlisle in 1949.

Management gave his energy a wider outlet. Carlisle improved sufficiently to finish third in the Third Division North in 1951. Grimsby Town followed, then Workington, where the responsibilities extended well beyond selecting a side. At a small club short of resources, Shankly involved himself in administration, scouting and fundraising. He was learning how much of a football club a manager might have to organise before the team could improve.

At Huddersfield he initially worked under Andy Beattie, coaching the reserves and young players, among them Denis Law. He succeeded Beattie in November 1956. These years brought experience of developing talent and repeated frustration over the resources available to strengthen his teams. Liverpool were appointing a man with a decade of managerial work behind him, rather than a proven collector of major trophies. The methods that became famous at Anfield had been tested in less celebrated surroundings.

Nor did Liverpool provide an instant release from financial argument. Shankly wanted stronger players and had to persuade the board to back him. His early signing Sammy Reid never played a competitive first-team match. The decisive arrivals were Ian St John from Motherwell and Ron Yeats from Dundee United in 1961. Their recruitment supplied authority at opposite ends of the pitch: St John in the attack and Yeats at the centre of the defence.

There was also valuable material already at the club. Roger Hunt and Gerry Byrne were among the players whose development would reward the new manager’s judgement. Shankly retained the coaching knowledge of Bob Paisley, Joe Fagan and Reuben Bennett, whose collaboration became associated with the Boot Room. His influence was unmistakable, but the work depended on a group. Recognising what Liverpool already possessed mattered as much as identifying what had to change.

Melwood offered a particularly clear expression of that approach. The training ground was in poor condition, with uneven pitches and inadequate facilities. Shankly saw space that could be put to better use. The pavilion was improved, the playing surfaces received attention and the ground gradually became a proper working base. This was an essential part of the rebuilding: the standards demanded on matchdays needed to be supported by the conditions in which players prepared for them.

The daily routine changed too. Players assembled and changed at Anfield, travelled together to Melwood, then returned after training. Shankly attached importance to the interval between exercise, bathing and eating. The arrangement also kept the squad together and maintained its connection with the home ground. The practical details of a working day became part of the manager’s attempt to organise the whole club around football.

Training itself was carefully planned. Warm-ups, circuits, ball work and small-sided games gave sessions a structure. Boards kept the ball returning to players in demanding control-and-passing exercises; the celebrated sweatbox combined technique with physical effort. Shankly could observe how individuals responded, pushing some and restraining others who were already working hard enough. Fitness and football were trained together. His insistence on apparently simple tasks rested on the belief that repeating them well would improve the team’s play.

The first great breakthrough came in 1961–62. Liverpool scored 99 league goals and finished eight points clear at the top of the Second Division, with 62 points in the two-points-for-a-win system. Hunt contributed 41 goals in 41 league appearances. The scale of the scoring made the season more than a narrow escape from a division in which the club had spent eight years. Shankly had put together a side capable of imposing itself on opponents.

Promotion was secured by a 2–0 home win over Southampton in April 1962, Kevin Lewis scoring both goals. Lewis was deputising for St John, a useful reminder that the rise depended on contributions beyond the names that came to define the era. Liverpool had cleared the obstacle on which so much else depended. They could now test the methods, confidence and players that had won promotion against the strongest sides in the country.

After finishing eighth in their first season back, Liverpool became champions in 1963–64. Peter Thompson had been added on the left wing, and the title was secured with a 5–0 victory over Arsenal at Anfield. The club had travelled from promotion to the championship in two seasons. Liverpool’s first league title since 1946–47 also opened the door to the European Cup, giving Shankly a new field in which to measure his team.

The growing confidence acquired a visual identity in the move to an all-red strip during his reign. Shankly associated the change with the impression his players made on opponents. The familiar account has Yeats trying the colours as the manager assessed their effect. It was characteristic of Shankly to consider how a team felt about itself and how it appeared to others. The kit became an enduring part of the Liverpool he was helping to create.

The FA Cup carried a different significance. Liverpool had never won it when they faced Leeds United at Wembley in May 1965. After a goalless ninety minutes, Hunt put Liverpool ahead in extra time. Billy Bremner equalised, then St John headed the winner from Ian Callaghan’s cross. The two forwards whose partnership had helped drive the promotion season had delivered another landmark. Liverpool’s 2–1 victory supplied a first in the club’s history as well as a new honour for its manager.

Three days later, Liverpool beat Internazionale 3–1 at Anfield in the first leg of the European Cup semi-final. Hunt, Callaghan and St John scored. The return in Italy brought elimination, but the campaign had established how quickly Liverpool could become competitive in Europe. A club recently confined to the Second Division was now contesting the closing stages of the continent’s leading competition. European football became a recurring part of the job Shankly had undertaken.

A second championship followed in 1965–66, achieved with only fourteen players used in the league. That stability reflected the strength of the side he had assembled. Liverpool also reached the European Cup Winners’ Cup final at Hampden Park. Hunt equalised against Borussia Dortmund, but Liverpool lost 2–1 after extra time. Domestic success had become repeatable; winning a European trophy was proving more difficult.

The following European Cup campaign brought a heavier setback. Ajax beat Liverpool 5–1 in Amsterdam in December 1966, in a match played in thick fog. A 2–2 draw at Anfield left Liverpool out by 7–3 on aggregate. The defeat belongs in Shankly’s history alongside the celebrated victories. His side had made rapid progress, but that did not remove the need to improve against different opponents and different ways of playing.

At home, the team that had achieved so much was growing older. Shankly later acknowledged that loyalty had complicated the task of replacement. The balance was difficult: these were players who had vindicated his confidence and changed the club’s fortunes. Yet the next league championship did not arrive until 1973. His career at Liverpool required a second major act of construction, after the momentum of the first great side had faded.

The FA Cup quarter-final at Watford on 21 February 1970 became a defining point in that process. Second Division opposition won 1–0 through Barry Endean. Shankly subsequently identified the defeat as the moment when he accepted the obligation to change his team. The symbolism is clear, although the rebuilding did not begin and end that afternoon. Some new players were already at Anfield, while Hunt had left for Bolton before the Watford match.

Ray Clemence, Emlyn Hughes, Larry Lloyd, Steve Heighway and Brian Hall were among the players who became important to the renewed side. They joined men such as Tommy Smith, Chris Lawler and Callaghan, who connected the two generations. The changes required Shankly to judge when experience remained useful and when a different player could take the team forward. The second successful side grew through that combination of retained knowledge and new ability.

John Toshack, signed in November 1970, offered strength in the air as well as the ability to bring others into the game. Kevin Keegan arrived from Scunthorpe United in 1971. Their partnership gave the attack a fresh shape, with Toshack’s service helping to release Keegan. Liverpool again had forwards whose qualities worked together, alongside the pace and invention Heighway provided. Recruitment was making a team out of complementary players.

Success took time to return. Liverpool lost the 1971 FA Cup final 2–1 to Arsenal, and the following season missed the league title by a single point. The renewed side was coming close before it began collecting the honours that would define it. Those disappointments give the eventual breakthrough its proper setting: Shankly had remained at work through the interval between his two great teams.

In 1972–73 Liverpool won the league and the UEFA Cup. The European campaign took them past Eintracht Frankfurt, AEK Athens, Dynamo Berlin, Dynamo Dresden and Tottenham Hotspur before a final against Borussia Mönchengladbach. By then the club had accumulated years of continental experience. This time it finished with a trophy, adding European success to the domestic championship for the first time in an English club’s history.

The final also illustrated Shankly’s capacity to adjust. Rain caused the first leg at Anfield to be abandoned, and he brought Toshack into the line-up for the rearranged match the next day. The German defence struggled with the Welsh forward’s aerial presence. Keegan scored twice and Lloyd once in a 3–0 victory, while Clemence saved a penalty from Jupp Heynckes. Those details became crucial when Liverpool lost the return 2–0. They won the trophy 3–2 on aggregate.

The second team then gave Shankly another FA Cup. Liverpool beat Newcastle United 3–0 at Wembley in 1974, Keegan scoring twice and Heighway once. Their passing in the move for the final goal offered a particularly vivid demonstration of the football the manager and his staff had worked to develop. The team that won this final bore the results of his rebuilding, just as the 1965 winners had borne the results of his first.

Throughout those changes, Shankly sustained an unusually direct relationship with supporters. Callaghan remembered the time he gave to talking with people and signing autographs. Letters to fans preserved another side of the manager, away from the performance of press conferences and matchdays. In them, the attention belonged to the recipient. His belief in collective effort, associated with his socialism and Glenbuck upbringing, extended beyond the dressing room to the people paying to watch.

That relationship helps explain the shock when his retirement was announced on 12 July 1974. He was sixty, and Liverpool had just produced another outstanding cup-winning side. Chairman John Smith delivered the news at Anfield. Shankly’s wife Ness wanted the family to see more of him, and he spoke of stepping away from the demands of management. The announcement interrupted an apparent continuation of success, rather than following the collapse of a team.

Paisley succeeded him, carrying forward knowledge accumulated during their years together. Shankly still led Liverpool out for the Charity Shield against Leeds in August, a final ceremonial connection with the side he had built. Retirement nevertheless proved difficult. His relationship with the club became strained, and his continuing desire to be involved did not settle into a lasting formal role. The affection of supporters endured through that uneasy separation.

Shankly died on 29 September 1981, aged sixty-eight. The gates and statue at Anfield give his memory a permanent physical presence, but the strongest evidence of his influence remains the club he left behind. Liverpool had regained the top division, won at home and in Europe, renewed an ageing team and developed the coaching continuity that made the next era possible. His career established that success could be built, rebuilt and sustained. Modern Liverpool began to take its recognisable shape through that work.
