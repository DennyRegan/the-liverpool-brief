# Rafael Benítez: Liverpool's European restoration and the pursuit of the league

In March 2009, Liverpool defeated Real Madrid 4–0 at Anfield and Manchester United 4–1 at Old Trafford within five days. Rafael Benítez's team could dismantle the most imposing opposition in Europe and England. The side that had won the European Cup four years earlier had changed substantially, acquiring a goalkeeper, a midfield structure and a striker capable of making Liverpool look like champions over more than a single extraordinary evening.

They finished that league season with 86 points, four behind United. Liverpool scored more goals than any other team in the division and lost only twice, yet eleven draws left them short. Benítez had brought the club close enough to make the missed opportunity painful. The difficulty of explaining that combination — exceptional performances, genuine progress and an unfulfilled ambition — runs through his Liverpool biography.

Istanbul remains its defining scene. Across six seasons and 350 matches, however, Benítez did much more than win one final. He restored Liverpool's standing as formidable European opponents, won the FA Cup and UEFA Super Cup, added a Community Shield and built a side capable of challenging for the championship. He also made transfer decisions that failed, fought increasingly public battles inside the club and left after a season in which the progress of the previous year had unravelled. Both the achievement and the disappointment belong in the story.

## A coach with a substantial past

Liverpool appointed Benítez on 16 June 2004, giving him a five-year contract to succeed Gérard Houllier. He arrived from Valencia with evidence of sustained success: Spanish league championships in 2002 and 2004, followed in that second title-winning season by the UEFA Cup. His reputation had been established in a league containing Real Madrid and Barcelona. Liverpool were hiring a coach who had already demonstrated that organisation and careful construction could overcome more glamorous rivals.

Born in Madrid on 4 April 1960, Benítez developed as a coach within Real Madrid's youth structure. Early senior jobs at Valladolid and Osasuna ended quickly; promotions with Extremadura and Tenerife subsequently gave his career firmer foundations. He arrived with experience of failure and recovery as well as Valencia's recent success.

Liverpool's starting position also needs perspective. Houllier had won important trophies, and the side Benítez inherited had finished fourth in 2003–04. This was a team entering Champions League qualifying, rather than one that required rescue from the bottom of the table. But fourth place and past cup success had not settled the question of how Liverpool could compete consistently with England's strongest sides. Benítez's Valencia record offered a credible answer without guaranteeing that his methods would transfer easily.

He began reshaping the team during a summer in which Michael Owen left for Real Madrid. Xabi Alonso arrived from Real Sociedad and Luis García from Barcelona, joining other Spanish recruits. Alonso gave Liverpool a midfielder who could receive possession deep, judge the next pass and change the direction of an attack. García offered movement and invention around the forwards. Their importance would exceed the simple fact that a Spanish manager had brought Spanish players with him.

There was also a decisive reassessment of someone already at Anfield. Benítez placed Jamie Carragher alongside Sami Hyypiä in central defence, ending the predominance of full-back duties in Carragher's earlier career. Recalling the decision later, he stressed Carragher's reading of the game and the way the two defenders complemented one another. It was a revealing act of management: improvement could come from identifying the right task for an existing player as well as buying a replacement.

## Learning to survive, learning to win

The first domestic season was uneven. Liverpool finished fifth and lost the League Cup final to Chelsea after extra time. Those results matter because the European triumph can otherwise make the entire campaign appear a continuous ascent. Benítez had not immediately solved the weekly demands of the Premier League, and Chelsea's superiority in domestic competition made Liverpool's eventual European victory over them all the more striking.

Even the road towards Istanbul came close to ending in December. Against Olympiacos at Anfield, Rivaldo's free-kick left Liverpool behind and needing three goals to produce the required two-goal victory. Benítez sent on Florent Sinama-Pongolle at half-time; the substitute equalised. Neil Mellor, another replacement, scored the second before Steven Gerrard supplied the unforgettable third. The finish belonged to the captain, but the recovery also depended on changes that brought Liverpool renewed attacking possibilities.

That distinction is central to assessing Benítez. His strongest European performances were collective achievements in which a carefully chosen intervention could create the setting for an individual player to decide the match. A substitution did not guarantee a goal. It could, however, alter the problem Liverpool were asking their opponents to solve. Olympiacos offered an early example of the manager finding useful contributions beyond the most obvious names.

Against Juventus in the quarter-finals, the challenge was different. Liverpool took a 2–1 advantage to Turin and defended it with a goalless draw. Alonso made his first start since breaking an ankle in January; Gerrard was missing through injury. UEFA's contemporary report described the work of Alonso and Igor Bišćan in interrupting Juventus attacks, while also recording Liverpool's opportunities to score on the counter. This was purposeful resistance, rather than ninety minutes spent merely waiting to concede.

The Chelsea semi-final required another carefully maintained advantage. A goalless first leg at Stamford Bridge was followed by Luis García's early, disputed goal at Anfield. The officials awarded it; Liverpool protected the 1–0 lead and reached the final. The controversy endured, but the awarded goal had given Liverpool an advantage they defended to the final whistle.

Anfield's atmosphere supplied a powerful part of that occasion. It did not remove the need for concentration from Carragher, Hyypiä, goalkeeper Jerzy Dudek and the players defending ahead of them. The crowd and the team helped sustain each other through a match in which Chelsea had to attack and Liverpool had to resist. Benítez's work belonged within that relationship, bringing an organised side to an arena capable of amplifying its efforts.

## Istanbul and the limits of a single explanation

On 25 May 2005, Milan exposed Liverpool's weaknesses with alarming speed. Paolo Maldini scored almost immediately, Hernán Crespo added two more before half-time and Harry Kewell's injury forced an early substitution. Benítez had started without Dietmar Hamann. Whatever the merits of his route to the final, his initial arrangement had not contained Milan. The manager's great night included a first half that required urgent correction.

Hamann replaced Steve Finnan at the interval. His presence helped Liverpool recover control in the centre and allowed Gerrard to move forward with greater freedom. In later accounts, both Hamann and Gerrard remembered the need to restore belief through an early goal. A practical alteration, a less defeated outlook and the continuing support from the stands gave Liverpool a chance to compete again.

Gerrard headed in, Vladimír Šmicer struck from distance and Alonso followed up his saved penalty to complete the recovery from 3–0 to 3–3. Those goals came in six minutes. The speed of the transformation made it extraordinary, but the match then demanded a different kind of performance. Liverpool still had to survive the remaining second half and extra time against opponents whose ability had not disappeared.

Benítez continued adjusting. When Milan introduced Serginho to widen their attack, Gerrard was moved to the right to help meet the threat. Carragher's defensive interventions and Dudek's saves were indispensable. Liverpool eventually won the shoot-out 3–2, with Dudek saving Andriy Shevchenko's final penalty. The fifth European Cup had been secured through tactical correction, attacking inspiration, defensive endurance and goalkeeping, all within a final that had repeatedly threatened to move beyond Liverpool's reach.

No single explanation adequately describes it. Calling Istanbul only a miracle understates the work that allowed the comeback; calling it merely a tactical masterpiece understates Milan's first-half dominance and the contingent moments that followed. Benítez deserves credit for recognising the need to change and finding a workable response. His players deserve credit for making that response matter under the most severe pressure of their careers.

The victory changed what Liverpool could aspire to under him. It also created an awkward benchmark. A European Cup in the first season was an achievement that years of later progress might struggle to equal emotionally. Benítez's remaining task was to build a team whose strength could be reproduced more regularly, especially away from the singular conditions of a European knockout tie.

## Building beyond the first triumph

The summer of 2005 brought Pepe Reina, Mohamed Sissoko and Peter Crouch. Each addressed a different need. Reina became first-choice goalkeeper despite Dudek's Istanbul heroics; Sissoko added energy in midfield; Crouch offered a forward who could hold the ball and bring others into play. When explaining the Crouch signing, Benítez specifically connected those qualities with Liverpool's difficulties in away matches during his first season.

Replacing a European Cup-winning goalkeeper showed that gratitude and team planning could pull in different directions. Reina's subsequent record justified the choice. Liverpool put together eleven successive clean sheets later in 2005, and the goalkeeper would win the Premier League Golden Glove in each of his first three seasons. Benítez was constructing a more dependable defensive base while seeking ways to improve the attack.

The UEFA Super Cup in August supplied another trophy. Liverpool trailed CSKA Moscow until substitute Djibril Cissé equalised late in normal time, then scored again and created García's goal in a 3–1 extra-time victory. Once more a player introduced from the bench changed the match. The result continued the success of the spring, although it was the coming league campaign that offered a better test of whether Liverpool had become more consistent.

They finished third with 82 points in 2005–06, one behind Manchester United and nine behind Chelsea. That was a substantial domestic improvement, even though it did not constitute a title challenge carried to the final day. European progress moved in the opposite direction: Benfica won 3–0 on aggregate in the last sixteen. Liverpool created chances in the Anfield return but failed to take them. Organisation and momentum could not substitute indefinitely for effective finishing.

The season ended with another final and another recovery. Against West Ham in Cardiff on 13 May 2006, Liverpool came from 2–0 down to level, fell behind again to Paul Konchesky's cross and were rescued by Gerrard's late long-range equaliser. After the 3–3 draw, Reina saved three penalties as Liverpool won the shoot-out 3–1. The manager had a second major cup in two seasons, with the Super Cup also won between them.

Gerrard's influence should not be diluted into a general compliment to the system. His pass for Cissé and two goals were exceptional acts that Liverpool badly needed. Equally, the final contained contributions from elsewhere: Alonso's cross, Crouch's knockdown and Reina's recovery from earlier mistakes. Benítez's team had acquired more ways to remain in a game. Cardiff showed both that resilience and the fact that resilience was required because the performance had been imperfect.

## A second final, and a changing club

The 2006 Community Shield added another piece of silverware, but no major trophy followed that season. Instead, Liverpool returned to the Champions League final. Their victory at Barcelona in February 2007 was particularly persuasive. The reigning European champions took the lead through Deco; Craig Bellamy equalised and then supplied John Arne Riise for the winner. A 2–1 result at Camp Nou demonstrated the capacity to recover and threaten against an opponent with exceptional attacking resources.

Liverpool advanced past Barcelona, PSV Eindhoven and Chelsea to face Milan again, this time in Athens. Reina saved two penalties in the Chelsea semi-final shoot-out. By now another important midfielder had arrived: Javier Mascherano's move from West Ham was ratified in February 2007. Alongside Alonso, he helped give Liverpool a platform from which Gerrard could play closer to the forward line.

Athens was a different final from Istanbul. Benítez used a five-man midfield with Gerrard supporting Dirk Kuyt, while Jermaine Pennant and Boudewijn Zenden provided width. Liverpool were competitive and avoided the early collapse of 2005, but Inzaghi scored twice. Kuyt's late header could not prevent a 2–1 defeat. Benítez sent on Crouch for Mascherano while chasing the match; Milan's second goal followed. The decision reflected the familiar risk of seeking greater attacking force at the cost of protection elsewhere.

The result was painful without erasing the significance of a second final in three seasons. Liverpool had become a team Europe's strongest clubs repeatedly had to reckon with. Yet the comparison between European effectiveness and domestic limitations remained, and the organisation around the manager was becoming less stable.

Tom Hicks and George Gillett bought Liverpool in February 2007. Their relationship with Benítez deteriorated amid arguments about transfers and communication. In January 2008, Hicks publicly confirmed that the owners had met Jürgen Klinsmann the previous November and had tried to arrange a possible managerial replacement. Hicks presented it as a contingency. Whatever his explanation, the admission made uncertainty over Benítez's position visible to supporters, players and prospective recruits.

Such circumstances were relevant to the conditions in which the manager worked. They do not settle the merits of every selection or signing he made. A fair account has to keep both questions in view: the quality of Benítez's decisions and the ability of Liverpool's leadership to offer a coherent setting for them. As the owners' disputes became part of the club's daily story, separating the two grew increasingly difficult.

## Torres and the most complete Liverpool side

Fernando Torres joined from Atlético Madrid in July 2007 for a club-record fee. His first season produced 33 goals, 24 of them in the league. Liverpool now had an attacker whose pace and finishing could punish defenders who moved forward, while his association with Gerrard became the central attacking partnership of the later Benítez years.

The surrounding structure mattered. Alonso could move possession through or across the midfield, Mascherano could challenge and cover, and Gerrard could operate closer to Torres. The roles were distinct enough to make their strengths complementary. Together they gave Benítez his most complete Liverpool side, combining control of possession with the ability to attack quickly when an opening appeared.

There was another European semi-final in 2008. Liverpool beat Internazionale and then Arsenal, only for Chelsea to prevail 4–3 on aggregate after extra time. Torres equalised in the Stamford Bridge return, but Chelsea's additional-time goals proved decisive. Liverpool had again travelled far in Europe without securing the trophy, while their league ambitions remained frustrated.

The summer of 2008 almost changed the midfield that would underpin the following season. Benítez pursued Gareth Barry, and Alonso was told he could be sold if an appropriate offer arrived. In his later account, Alonso recalled understanding the manager's responsibility while reassessing his own security in the team. Barry did not join and Alonso stayed, producing one of his strongest Liverpool seasons. His testimony also credited Benítez with honouring the promise that he would play if his performances deserved it.

Robbie Keane's arrival proved far less successful. The forward returned to Tottenham on 2 February 2009 after only six months. Benítez acknowledged that selling him without buying a replacement was a risk, explaining that the situation had not been helping the team. The decision belongs in the assessment of squad building alongside the successful acquisition of Torres. A manager's best signings do not make his unsuccessful ones immaterial, especially during a close championship race.

## The championship that escaped

Liverpool's 2008–09 title challenge was substantial. They beat both Chelsea and Manchester United home and away, finished unbeaten at Anfield and scored 77 league goals. The side could win through discipline against strong opponents and overwhelm them when its attackers found space. Alonso, Mascherano, Gerrard and Torres formed an outstanding central group, with Reina and Carragher important behind them.

Its difficulties were less spectacular but decisive. Liverpool drew seven home league matches and four away. A team that lost twice accumulated four fewer points than United, who lost four times but won three more games. Those totals explain why resistance to defeat was insufficient. The contest rewarded turning tight matches into victories, and United did so more often.

On 9 January, Benítez delivered the press conference in which he listed complaints about Alex Ferguson, disciplinary decisions and the treatment of United. It became an enduring episode in the rivalry. Liverpool then dropped points during January and February while United established a strong winning run. The sequence is clear. A claim that the press conference itself caused Liverpool to lose the championship goes beyond what those results can prove.

Torres's hamstring problems also complicated the campaign. Liverpool could win without him, as their early-season victory over United showed, but losing an exceptional forward reduced the manager's options. Injuries help explain some of the difficulty without supplying a complete excuse. The number of draws, the Keane episode and the standard established by United all remain part of the account.

Liverpool's spectacular March week showed how far Benítez's construction had travelled. Real Madrid were beaten 5–0 on aggregate, the Anfield return displaying relentless early attacking pressure. Four days later, Torres, Gerrard, Fábio Aurélio and Andrea Dossena scored at Old Trafford. Liverpool subsequently won ten of their final eleven league games. They did not collapse in the face of the title race; they finished strongly and still could not recover the points already lost.

Benítez signed a new contract in March, but the season's promise did not lead to a stable next stage. Alonso moved to Madrid that August. Alberto Aquilani arrived from Roma with the task of helping fill the vacancy, although an ankle operation meant he was still rehabilitating when signed. Glen Johnson also joined. Liverpool entered 2009–10 attempting to improve and replace important qualities at the same time.

## A difficult ending and an enduring attachment

The final season brought seventh place and 63 points, a fall of 23 from the previous campaign. Liverpool failed to reach the Champions League knockout rounds and lost an FA Cup replay to Reading at Anfield. Their Europa League run ended against Atlético Madrid in the semi-finals. Torres's knee problems added further disruption, while the departure of Alonso had removed a central part of the team's passing structure.

The decline was too large to reduce to one absent player. Recruitment had not maintained the previous balance, performances had deteriorated and ownership conflict continued to surround the club. The material circumstances were difficult; the results were also poor. Benítez's earlier achievements did not make that season satisfactory, just as the season did not retrospectively cancel what he had achieved.

A goalless draw at Hull on 9 May 2010 was his final Liverpool match. On 3 June, the club announced that he was leaving by mutual consent. Chairman Martin Broughton's explanation linked the decision to the disappointing season and the wish for a fresh start. Benítez departed with three major trophies, the Community Shield, two European Cup finals and a league challenge that had briefly made the restoration of the championship seem within reach.

His attachment to Liverpool continued outside the job. That month he donated £96,000 to the Hillsborough Family Support Group. Later interviews also described the work of the Montse Benítez Foundation and the family's connection with the city. These acts and relationships help explain the affection in which he is held without requiring a football result to validate support for families seeking truth and justice.

Benítez's Liverpool was never only the team that came back in Istanbul. It was also the team that learned to keep clean sheets more reliably, returned to a European final, found an exceptional Gerrard–Torres partnership and reached 86 points without winning the league. Its construction involved insight, correction and mistakes. Its ending involved decline within an increasingly troubled club. The full story leaves room for all of that — and for the extraordinary fact that, in his first season, a manager willing to change a failing plan helped Liverpool recover their place at the summit of European football.
