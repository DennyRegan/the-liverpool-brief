# Xabi Alonso: the intelligence at the centre of Liverpool's revival

Xabi Alonso's most important Liverpool goal began with a penalty he did not score. In Istanbul, Dida saved his kick and the ball came back into play. Alonso followed it, reached the rebound and drove Liverpool level at 3–3 against Milan. A moment that might have broken the momentum of the comeback became the moment that completed it.

The urgency of that finish sits beside a different, more familiar image of Alonso: a midfielder taking possession, looking up and sending the ball precisely into a teammate's path. His Liverpool career contained extraordinary goals, but his influence usually came through the quality of the next pass. He could give an attack direction without having to be the player who finished it.

Across five seasons from 2004 to 2009, Alonso made 210 appearances and scored 19 goals. He won the Champions League, UEFA Super Cup and FA Cup, played in a second European Cup final and helped Liverpool take Manchester United close in the league championship. The career that followed in Spain and Germany would bring further honours, but Anfield had already established the qualities that made him such a distinctive footballer.

## Finding his place in a new Liverpool

Alonso arrived from Real Sociedad on 20 August 2004, aged 22, as Rafael Benítez began rebuilding the team. Liverpool paid £10.7 million for him. Luis García joined in the same recruitment period, providing another attacking possibility, while Alonso wore the No.14 shirt and offered a new means of organising the midfield around Steven Gerrard.

Born in Tolosa on 25 November 1981, he was the son of former Spain international Periko Alonso. At Sociedad, a loan spell with Eibar had been followed by a recall under John Toshack, who made the young midfielder captain. By the time Liverpool signed him, Alonso had helped Sociedad finish second in Spain in 2002–03 and had played at Euro 2004. He was young without being an untested prospect.

The move changed his life beyond football. Looking back in 2017, Alonso described Liverpool as his first experience of living away from his parents and outside his familiar surroundings. His account connected professional development with becoming an adult. A player apparently at ease on the pitch was also adapting to a new country and daily life.

His competitive debut came in a 1–0 defeat at Bolton on 29 August. The performance that made his possibilities particularly clear followed at Fulham on 16 October. Liverpool trailed 2–0 at half-time when Benítez replaced Salif Diao with Alonso. The visitors recovered to win 4–2, with Alonso scoring his first Liverpool goal from a deflected free-kick after the deficit had been wiped out.

The goal gave him a place in the result; his passing gave him a place in supporters' understanding of the team. Liverpool could improve by controlling where possession went, allowing players ahead of the ball to receive it in useful positions. Alonso's contribution was not confined to making a spectacular pass. Choosing when to play a simpler one was part of the same intelligence.

## An interrupted season, and a place in history

An ankle broken in a challenge with Frank Lampard on New Year's Day 2005 interrupted that first season. Liverpool lost the Chelsea match 1–0, and an initial expectation of a relatively short absence proved optimistic. Alonso's first start after the injury did not come until 13 April, away to Juventus in the Champions League quarter-final.

With Gerrard unavailable, Liverpool had to defend their 2–1 first-leg advantage in Turin. Alonso and Igor Bišćan worked in the centre of midfield as the visitors secured a goalless draw. Alonso also found Milan Baroš with a pass that created a chance to score. His return demonstrated both sides of his role: helping stop Juventus and offering Liverpool a route out of pressure.

He missed the second leg of the Chelsea semi-final through suspension, but Liverpool reached Istanbul without him. There he started against a Milan midfield whose quality was brutally apparent in the first half. At the interval Liverpool were 3–0 down. Dietmar Hamann's introduction helped provide a more secure centre, and Gerrard gained greater freedom to move forward.

Gerrard's header and Vladimír Šmicer's long-range shot brought Liverpool back into the final. Then Gerrard won the penalty. Alonso's kick was saved, but he reacted before the chance disappeared, beating Alessandro Nesta to the loose ball. The finish belonged to a player normally admired for seeing the game clearly when there was time to assess it. Here he had to recognise and act on an opening that would last only an instant.

Liverpool eventually won on penalties after extra time. Alonso's equaliser was scored during the match, rather than in the shoot-out, and it had made that eventual opportunity possible. His first year in England ended with a European Cup medal despite an injury that had kept him out through an important part of the campaign. The association with Liverpool's greatest modern comeback would remain permanent.

## The range of his contribution

Alonso's second season brought more regular football and further trophies. Liverpool won the UEFA Super Cup and, in May 2006, the FA Cup. In the final against West Ham, Alonso's cross was headed down by Peter Crouch for Gerrard to score Liverpool's second goal. The side eventually won a shoot-out after a 3–3 draw. That contribution was characteristic: an accurate delivery made the next decisive action possible.

Earlier in the cup run, Alonso had scored twice in a remarkable 5–3 victory at Luton. Liverpool were 3–1 behind before recovering. His first goal levelled the scores with a dipping shot from distance. His second came near the end, after Luton's goalkeeper had gone forward for a set-piece. Alonso sent a left-footed shot into the empty goal from inside his own half.

That was an exceptional finish in unusual circumstances. Against Newcastle at Anfield in September 2006, he produced another long-range goal with the goalkeeper still attempting to protect his net. Alonso won the ball from Charles N'Zogbia, moved forward and noticed Steve Harper away from his line. This time he struck with his right foot; Harper slipped while trying to recover and the ball dropped beyond him. Liverpool won 2–0.

The two goals showed more than willingness to attempt the improbable. Both depended on seeing a goalkeeper's position and judging whether the opportunity was real. His normal passing responsibilities demanded a related assessment of space, movement and distance. The skills that helped Liverpool move the ball through midfield could occasionally be turned directly towards goal.

Alonso was not an attacking midfielder defined by a large annual goal total. His 19 Liverpool goals reflected a player whose main responsibility lay elsewhere. That made the memorable strikes a useful window into his ability without turning them into an adequate summary of his work. Liverpool needed the everyday accuracy and availability between those highlights.

There were disappointments too. Milan won the 2007 Champions League final 2–1 in Athens, and Alonso's foul on Kaká preceded the free-kick from which Filippo Inzaghi opened the scoring. A career associated with composed judgement also contained expensive moments in games that mattered. Liverpool had reached a second final in three seasons, but the experience could not repeat the outcome of the first.

## A midfield taking shape, and a future in doubt

Javier Mascherano's arrival gave Benítez another outstanding central midfielder. With Alonso helping distribute possession and Mascherano supplying defensive aggression and cover, Gerrard could operate further forward. Fernando Torres joined in 2007 and provided the pace and finishing to make that arrangement especially dangerous. Alonso's value became closely connected with the freedom Liverpool could give their principal attackers.

The combination did not make every season smooth. Injuries restricted Alonso to 27 appearances in 2007–08. Liverpool again reached the Champions League semi-finals but were beaten by Chelsea. For the player, it was a less settled campaign between the European final of 2007 and the remarkable league challenge still to come.

The following summer brought a different uncertainty. Benítez pursued Gareth Barry and Alonso was told that he might be sold. In a later interview, Alonso recalled reassessing his security in the team while respecting the manager's responsibilities. He also credited Benítez with honouring the subsequent promise to select him if his performances merited it. Barry did not join, and Alonso remained at Anfield.

He responded with a season that showed how important he had become. The proposed change had not materialised; the existing midfield went on to sustain Liverpool's strongest league challenge of his five seasons. Alonso's contribution gave supporters plenty to appreciate while he was still there, before any later assessment of the qualities lost through his departure.

## The final season at Anfield

On 26 October 2008, Alonso scored the only goal at Chelsea. His early shot took a deflection off José Bosingwa and wrong-footed Petr Čech. Liverpool held their advantage, ending Chelsea's 86-match unbeaten home league run. Alonso nearly added another with a free-kick that struck the post. The match placed Liverpool three points clear at the top and provided convincing evidence that their title ambition had substance.

He made 47 appearances and scored five goals in all competitions that season, including 33 league appearances. The increasingly familiar midfield roles gave Liverpool a strong central platform: Alonso could direct possession, Mascherano could help recover it and Gerrard could threaten beyond them. Their effectiveness came through the interaction of those abilities, supported by the other players around them.

Liverpool finished second with 86 points, four behind United. Alonso had helped a team capable of impressive victories become serious contenders across the campaign, but the league title remained beyond him at Anfield. It was an ending with achievement and regret together: his strongest sustained Liverpool football had brought the principal domestic prize close without securing it.

Real Madrid completed his transfer on 5 August 2009. Alonso's contemporary explanation emphasised the desire for a fresh challenge after five years. The previous summer's uncertainty formed part of the background, but he also wanted to experience another stage of his career. Liverpool lost an influential midfielder; Alonso chose to move while his standing remained high.

His departure has often supplied a convenient dividing line in accounts of the Benítez years. It certainly removed a distinctive set of qualities from the team. Yet the appreciation of what Liverpool lost is strongest when expressed positively: a player who could help them hold the ball, change direction, survive pressure and release better-positioned teammates. His absence made those contributions easier to notice.

## The Liverpool connection that remained

Alonso went on to win the Spanish league and another Champions League with Madrid, before joining Bayern Munich in 2014 and adding three Bundesliga titles. With Spain, the European Championship won while he was at Liverpool in 2008 was followed by the 2010 World Cup and Euro 2012. He retired from playing in May 2017 with a record that placed his Anfield years within an exceptional international career.

Gerrard's retirement tribute described the pleasure of playing alongside him and the loss felt when he left. It was a teammate's assessment of something supporters had also recognised: Alonso's passing could improve the football available to everyone around him. Individual acclaim and the experience of sharing a midfield pointed towards the same quality.

Liverpool remained a personal connection too. In a 2017 interview, Alonso spoke about friends in the city and returning with his son, who had been born there in 2008. Later charity appearances at Anfield brought him back into a familiar setting. By 2022 he had also coached Real Sociedad's reserve side to promotion, reflecting on the different demands of preparing young players rather than taking the field himself.

The footballer who scored from his own half twice and reacted to a saved penalty in Istanbul was also the player who made thousands of less conspicuous decisions between those moments. Liverpool's revival needed the drama and the control. Alonso supplied both, leaving after five seasons with his place in the club's history secured by a famous equaliser and reinforced by the intelligence with which he played the rest of the game.
