# Gary McAllister: the experience that helped Liverpool win again

Gary McAllister helped make four of Liverpool’s five goals in the 2001 UEFA Cup final. He supplied the free-kick for Markus Babbel’s opener, converted a penalty, passed to Robbie Fowler before Liverpool’s fourth and delivered the ball that Delfí Geli headed into his own net to end the match. In a final that repeatedly escaped Liverpool’s control, the 36-year-old midfielder kept providing ways forward. Dortmund was the fullest expression of what Gérard Houllier had gained by recruiting him the previous summer.

McAllister’s Liverpool playing career lasted two seasons, producing 87 competitive appearances and nine goals. Its lasting importance lies in the relationship between that brief service and the club’s progress. Houllier was building a team around younger players who needed to learn how to sustain performances and win demanding games. McAllister brought an established football education, then contributed the passes, penalties and free-kicks that turned several of those lessons into results. He was both an example and an active participant.

His route to Anfield had been long enough to make another major opportunity seem unlikely. Born in Motherwell on Christmas Day 1964, he began his senior career with his hometown club before moving to Leicester City in 1985. Five seasons there preceded his transfer to Leeds United in 1990. Those moves had taken him from Scottish football into the English game and eventually into a side capable of winning its highest honour.

At Leeds, McAllister belonged to the midfield of the 1991–92 First Division champions alongside Gordon Strachan, David Batty and Gary Speed. That experience mattered when Liverpool considered him eight years later. He knew the standards required of a championship side and had worked within a midfield whose strengths depended on the combination of its players. His ability to pass and organise play was part of a collective structure, a useful preparation for the role Houllier envisaged.

He moved to Coventry City in 1996 and remained productive well into his thirties. In his final season before joining Liverpool, he scored 13 league goals and was Coventry’s player of the season. This was an experienced midfielder still affecting matches, with the energy and technical quality to play regularly in the Premier League. Liverpool’s interest came at the end of a good campaign rather than after a prolonged withdrawal from serious football.

The move was agreed in May 2000, with McAllister joining on a free transfer that summer after his Coventry contract expired. He was 35. That age inevitably shaped the reaction to his arrival: a club seeking to close the gap on the leading teams was signing someone much nearer the end of his career than the beginning. McAllister understood the question, but his own recent form gave him reason to believe that he could answer it on the pitch.

Houllier wanted him to connect defence and attack, control the tempo and make good decisions under pressure. There was also a responsibility to help the young players develop. McAllister accepted that expectation without treating it as a substitute for playing. In explaining the move at the time, he described the exchange as reciprocal: he could offer experience, while the ambition and energy of the younger players could help him too. Liverpool represented a chance to compete for trophies again.

Steven Gerrard later explained how quickly his initial reservations disappeared. He had first wondered whether the new arrival would take his place. Training revealed a player whose touch, passing, movement and preparation offered things to learn. McAllister could guide a younger midfielder while remaining a team-mate in the same competitive environment. His influence depended on the quality of his football as much as on the advice he offered away from a match.

The beginning was uneven. McAllister made his competitive debut as a substitute in the 1–0 home victory over Bradford City on 19 August 2000. Two days later he started at Arsenal and was sent off after a challenge on Patrick Vieira as Liverpool lost 2–0. The experience did little to reassure those who had doubted the signing. His first months also brought more time among the substitutes than the pivotal spring that followed would suggest.

There were signs of his usefulness before the season reached its decisive stage. On 12 November he scored his first Liverpool goal against Coventry in a 4–1 win at Anfield, and supplied the pass for Gerrard’s goal in the same match. Former colleagues provided the opposition as he demonstrated two parts of his new role: contributing himself and bringing a younger player into the attack. His value was beginning to appear in the team’s play.

Liverpool’s first trophy came on 25 February 2001. McAllister entered the League Cup final against Birmingham City from the bench, and the match finished 1–1 after extra time. He took Liverpool’s first penalty in the shootout and scored. The Reds eventually won 5–4 on penalties, ending the immediate wait for silverware under Houllier. McAllister later regarded that success as a catalyst for the two finals still to come: the squad had experienced the act of winning together.

The weeks from the Goodison derby to the last league game became the defining stretch of his Liverpool career. They contained the sharpest demonstration of his ability to read a moment and the accumulation of work that made it more than one memorable kick. Liverpool were pursuing the FA Cup and UEFA Cup while trying to secure Champions League qualification. There was little room for a poor result or for exhaustion to be allowed to determine the next match.

On 16 April, Liverpool and Everton were tied at 2–2 deep into added time at Goodison Park. Liverpool had twice led, Robbie Fowler had missed a penalty and Igor Bišćan had been sent off. A free-kick in the left channel offered a last opportunity. Most of the ground expected a delivery towards Liverpool’s taller players in the penalty area. McAllister had recently taken a similar free-kick, which Sami Hyypiä had headed narrowly wide.

He recognised that Everton goalkeeper Paul Gerrard was anticipating another cross. As the goalkeeper shifted across, McAllister shot towards the near post. The ball skidded in and Liverpool won 3–2. His later explanations emphasised the observation behind the decision: watching the goalkeeper’s feet was a technique he already used for free-kicks closer to goal. At Goodison, he applied it over a far greater distance. Confidence and precision completed a choice that depended first on noticing an opening.

Three days later, the situation was different but the responsibility similar. Liverpool had drawn 0–0 at Barcelona in the first leg of their UEFA Cup semi-final. At Anfield on 19 April, McAllister converted the penalty that gave them a 1–0 victory and a place in the final. The two legs required Liverpool’s defensive organisation to survive against formidable attacking talent. When the opening came at home, the player entrusted with the kick settled the tie.

The scoring continued. A penalty against Tottenham Hotspur was followed by free-kicks against Coventry and Bradford City. McAllister had scored in five successive Liverpool matches, beginning with the derby. The sequence joined European progress to the league campaign: these were contributions spread across the different demands placed on Houllier’s squad. For a midfielder signed partly to steady younger players, it was an unusually conspicuous way of doing so.

Phil Thompson, Houllier’s assistant, later identified the derby as a change in the direction of the run-in. Liverpool had been beaten by Leeds at Anfield and were under pressure in the contest for a top-three place. The dramatic recovery at Goodison gave the side renewed momentum. McAllister was central to that recovery, but the gains depended on Liverpool carrying the confidence into subsequent fixtures rather than leaving it attached to the memory of one extraordinary goal.

His form did not guarantee a starting place in the FA Cup final on 12 May. Houllier selected a younger midfield against Arsenal, taking account of the opposition’s physical demands and the heat in Cardiff. McAllister was disappointed. He had grown up watching the English cup final and wanted to play from the beginning. Accepting the decision while preparing to contribute from the bench was another part of the experienced role he had undertaken.

He still helped change the match. Arsenal led through Freddie Ljungberg and Liverpool struggled to create the response they needed. McAllister’s free-kick from the left brought the ball into the area; Arsenal failed to clear, Babbel diverted it towards Michael Owen and Owen equalised. Patrick Berger then supplied the pass for Owen’s winner. The 2–1 comeback belonged above all to the striker’s finishing, with McAllister contributing to the passage that had restored Liverpool’s chance.

During the celebrations, Houllier told McAllister to prepare to start in Dortmund. Four days separated the finals, with the closing league fixture still beyond them. The selection decisions were part of managing those demands. Liverpool could not celebrate the FA Cup at length before facing Alavés: the next preparation began almost immediately. For McAllister, the frustration of Cardiff gave way to the opportunity to shape a European final from the outset.

On 16 May, his free-kick found Babbel for the first goal and Gerrard soon made it 2–0. Alavés responded through substitute Iván Alonso, but McAllister’s penalty restored Liverpool’s two-goal advantage before half-time. It appeared a strong position. Two goals from Javi Moreno after the interval removed it, exposing how difficult the match would be to contain. The composure Liverpool had shown in keeping Barcelona out over two legs did not produce a quiet final.

McAllister helped create Liverpool’s next lead by passing to Fowler, who worked a shooting position and made it 4–3. Jordi Cruyff’s late header forced extra time. Alavés were then reduced to nine men, and another McAllister delivery provided the ending: Geli headed the free-kick into his own net. Under the golden-goal rule, the match stopped immediately. Liverpool had won 5–4 and completed the League Cup, FA Cup and UEFA Cup treble.

McAllister received the man-of-the-match award. His involvement in four goals explains why the final remains inseparable from his name, even though its course was chaotic and Liverpool repeatedly surrendered advantages. He kept producing useful football as the pressure changed. A floated delivery, a penalty, a pass into an attacking position and the final free-kick required different actions; his judgement and execution made each count.

There was still a league objective to complete. On 19 May, Liverpool won 4–0 at Charlton Athletic to finish third and secure Champions League qualification. The final run beginning at Goodison had therefore ended with two more cups and the return to Europe’s leading competition. McAllister finished his first season with 49 appearances and seven goals. The signing that had invited doubt in the summer now had a substantial record of achievement behind it.

Two further honours followed in August, during the next season. McAllister scored a penalty in Liverpool’s 2–1 Charity Shield victory over Manchester United on 12 August. On 24 August, Liverpool beat Bayern Munich 3–2 in Monaco to win the UEFA Super Cup. Those results made five trophies for the club in the calendar year.

His second season contained 38 appearances and two goals, although his place in the side became less prominent as it progressed. Liverpool finished second in the league with 80 points and reached the Champions League quarter-finals, where Bayer Leverkusen eliminated them 4–3 on aggregate. McAllister later acknowledged the disappointment of playing less while recognising that he had fulfilled the brief Houllier had given him. His most influential months could not be reproduced indefinitely.

His final Liverpool appearance came against Ipswich Town at Anfield on 11 May 2002, when he entered from the bench in a 5–0 victory. He left for Coventry to become player-manager. The move began another difficult adjustment, and he later reflected that combining playing with management had demanded too much at once. Subsequent coaching work included Leeds, Middlesbrough and a reunion with Houllier as his assistant at Aston Villa. Their football relationship had continued beyond the two Liverpool seasons.

Liverpool brought him back as first-team coach on 11 July 2015, working under Brendan Rodgers. The appointment connected a former player’s experience with another generation, but the coaching spell was brief. When Rodgers departed and Jürgen Klopp arrived that October, McAllister left the first-team staff and accepted an ambassadorial role.

McAllister’s standing at Liverpool rests on both the visible interventions and the daily influence that made them credible. Gerrard remembered a player who set standards through his preparation and helped him understand the game. Supporters remember the derby, Barcelona and Dortmund because those matches changed what Liverpool could achieve. In two seasons, McAllister joined the education of a developing side to its first great successes under Houllier, giving his short Anfield career an enduring place in the club’s history.
