# Kenny Dalglish: a life at Liverpool

Kenny Dalglish scored the goal that retained Liverpool’s European Cup, played in six league championship sides and won the Double in his first season as player-manager. He then rebuilt the attack and produced the football of 1987–88. He helped set the standards by which later Liverpool teams would be judged.

His importance cannot be measured in trophies alone. After Hillsborough, Kenny and Marina Dalglish were alongside bereaved families, attending funerals and helping people who came to Anfield to mourn. Their support continued long after the immediate aftermath. It established a relationship with Liverpool that survived his resignation, years working elsewhere and an imperfect second spell as manager.

Dalglish’s story belongs to the club’s greatest footballing years. It also belongs to its people.

## From Glasgow to Anfield

Born in Glasgow on 4 March 1951, Dalglish grew up supporting Rangers but joined Celtic in 1967. Under Jock Stein he developed into a leading Scottish footballer, winning four league championships, four Scottish Cups and a League Cup. He captained Celtic to the league and Scottish Cup Double in his final season there, 1976–77.

Liverpool already knew something of him. He had travelled south for a trial as a 15-year-old in 1966. When he returned in August 1977, he was an established international, arriving for a British record £440,000. Bob Paisley was buying a replacement for Kevin Keegan, who had left for Hamburg after helping Liverpool become European champions.

Replacing Keegan meant entering a successful side in a position of unusual scrutiny. Dalglish made an immediate contribution: he scored seven minutes into his league debut at Middlesbrough and again against Newcastle United in his first Anfield appearance. He finished his first season with 31 goals in all competitions.

The decisive one came at Wembley on 10 May 1978. Liverpool had reached another European Cup final, against Club Brugge. In the second half, Dalglish met Graeme Souness’s pass and lifted the ball over the advancing Birger Jensen. Liverpool won 1–0.

His first year had ended with the most important goal of the European campaign. Paisley had replaced a central figure in his attack without surrendering the European Cup.

## The player who made others better

Dalglish’s scoring mattered, but his influence extended through the players around him. He could spot an opening and find a pass that brought a teammate into the game. David Johnson benefited from that creativity before Ian Rush became his principal attacking partner.

Rush’s account of their understanding is revealing. Recalling the partnership in a 2014 club interview, he explained that Dalglish encouraged him to run into space and trust the pass to arrive. They came to anticipate each other’s decisions. Rush’s speed and movement gave Dalglish opportunities; Dalglish’s awareness allowed Rush to exploit them.

The partnership gave Liverpool a dependable route to goal. One forward recognised the run; the other trusted the pass to arrive. Dalglish remained a goalscorer himself, but did not need to finish the move to determine its outcome.

The team’s success was sustained. Liverpool won the 1978–79 championship with 68 points under the two-points-for-a-win system, conceding only 16 goals in 42 games. They retained the title the following season. Dalglish played every league match in 1979–80, when Johnson scored 21 league goals.

Further European Cups followed in 1981 and 1984. Under Joe Fagan, the 1983–84 side combined the European Cup with the league championship and League Cup. Rush scored 47 competitive goals that season; his later recollection credited Dalglish with a central part in their attacking understanding.

Recognition came from opponents and observers as well as results. Dalglish was the Football Writers’ Association’s Footballer of the Year in 1979 and 1983, and the players’ choice for their equivalent award in 1983. He also finished second in that year’s Ballon d’Or.

Across his Liverpool playing career he made 515 competitive appearances and scored 172 goals. His playing honours included six league titles, three European Cups, four League Cups, the FA Cup and the UEFA Super Cup. These were the achievements of a forward who combined individual distinction with an unusually productive place in a team.

## Taking responsibility

Dalglish became player-manager on 30 May 1985, aged 34, succeeding Fagan. The appointment came the day after Heysel, where 39 people had died before the European Cup final against Juventus. A charge by Liverpool supporters had led to people being crushed against or falling through a failing wall. Liverpool’s subsequent exclusion from European competition shaped the whole of his first managerial spell.

Paisley assisted him during his first two seasons. Dalglish also began making changes: Steve Nicol and Jim Beglin took over the full-back positions, Steve McMahon arrived in midfield and Jan Mølby became increasingly important to the team’s play. He inherited considerable quality, but responsibility for selection and renewal was now his.

The first season required recovery. Defeat at home to Everton in February left Liverpool eight points behind their neighbours. They responded with an unbeaten run through their final 11 league games. Dalglish brought himself back into the side during the run-in and remained capable of settling matches.

At Stamford Bridge on 3 May 1986, he controlled Beglin’s lofted pass on his chest and volleyed beyond Tony Godden. The 1–0 victory secured the championship. Seven days later, Liverpool beat Everton 3–1 at Wembley to complete the first league and FA Cup Double in their history.

The achievement demonstrated the value of both parts of his job. He had guided the side through the season, then supplied the finishing touch to the league campaign himself. The transition from player to manager had not removed his most immediate means of influencing a match.

## Building his own attack

Liverpool finished the following season without a major trophy. Rush’s departure for Juventus then required a substantial change in the forward line. John Aldridge had already arrived during 1986–87; Dalglish added John Barnes and Peter Beardsley in the summer.

He assembled players whose qualities complemented one another. Aldridge supplied the central finishing, Barnes carried the threat from the left and Beardsley linked the attack. Liverpool no longer depended on reproducing the Dalglish–Rush partnership. They had different ways of opening a defence.

The 1987–88 side began with 29 league matches unbeaten. Its outstanding demonstration came against Nottingham Forest at Anfield in April, a 5–0 victory built on accurate passing, movement and accomplished finishing. Dalglish had built an attack capable of sustained pressure as well as individual invention.

Liverpool secured the title with four matches remaining. Yet the season also exposed the limits of superiority over a league campaign: Wimbledon beat them 1–0 in the FA Cup final. A second Double was within reach and was lost.

Rush returned from Italy in 1988. The following spring Liverpool were again competing for the league and FA Cup. On 15 April 1989 they travelled to Hillsborough for the semi-final against Nottingham Forest.

## Hillsborough

Ninety-seven children, women and men died as a result of the disaster at Hillsborough. They were crushed in the central pens of the Leppings Lane terrace. Survivors, as well as bereaved families, remained central to the struggle for answers that followed.

In April 2016, the new inquests concluded that the 96 people whose deaths they examined had been unlawfully killed. The jury identified failures in policing, the stadium’s safety and the emergency response. It rejected the suggestion that supporters’ behaviour caused or contributed to the dangerous situation at the Leppings Lane turnstiles.

Andrew Devine died in 2021 as a result of the injuries he had sustained at Hillsborough. A separate coroner’s finding of unlawful killing recognised him as the disaster’s 97th victim. The number 97 describes those who died as a result of Hillsborough, not the number who died on the day.

Dalglish was on the touchline when the match was stopped. Members of his family were at the ground. He later recalled the relief of finding his son Paul, recognising that his family had been fortunate.

In the days that followed, Liverpool’s manager took part in work for which football had offered no preparation. He and Marina attended funerals and visited those affected. Anfield opened to mourners. Players, staff and their families spent time with people who came to leave tributes, talk or simply be at the ground.

The details of that support matter more than a count of public appearances. In his autobiography, Dalglish recalled relatives wanting to speak about the players their loved ones had followed. In one account, he fetched Steve Heighway after a widow told him that Heighway had been her husband’s favourite. The club could offer a familiar person and time to listen when it could offer no remedy for what had happened.

Asked about funeral attendance in 2009, Dalglish rejected the idea of keeping a tally. He explained that the club wanted a player or former player represented at every funeral. He stressed that people grieved differently: some players found such visits harder than others, without feeling the losses any less.

Marina’s contribution was substantial, alongside those of the other players’ wives and families. Dalglish’s recollections repeatedly credit their practical work with people arriving at Anfield. The response was collective, involving the club and a wider community that brought supplies and offered help.

The subsequent campaign for truth and justice was led by bereaved families and survivors. Kenny and Marina were important supporters of those families; their contribution should be understood within that continuing struggle, with the families’ own work and persistence at its centre.

Football eventually resumed. Liverpool beat Everton 3–2 after extra time in the FA Cup final, with substitute Rush scoring twice. Dalglish later described the victory as particularly important to him. Its significance belongs alongside the grief of that period, without suggesting that winning could resolve it.

Six days later, Arsenal won 2–0 at Anfield to take the league title. The football season had ended. The consequences of Hillsborough had not.

## The strain and the departure

Liverpool regained the championship in 1989–90, their third under Dalglish and their 18th overall. He also made the last appearance of his playing career that season. He had continued to lead a successful side while carrying experiences whose effect, by his own later account, he did not fully understand at the time.

His resignation was announced on 22 February 1991, two days after the 4–4 FA Cup replay at Everton. Describing it simply as a reaction to a turbulent match would misrepresent his explanation.

In his autobiography, written with Henry Winter, Dalglish identified Hillsborough as the most important factor in his decision to leave. He described strain accumulating over the intervening months, difficulties at home and a loss of confidence in making decisions. 

He also wrote that he had decided before the Everton game that he needed to stop. The draw was his last match, not a sufficient explanation for his departure. His account makes clear that continuing to manage had become a personal burden he could no longer sustain.

His first spell had brought three league championships and two FA Cups. In assessing its end, the honours and the human cost both belong in the record.

Dalglish subsequently managed Blackburn Rovers to the 1994–95 Premier League title. At Newcastle United he oversaw a second-place finish and reached the 1998 FA Cup final. He later returned to Celtic, where he took charge of the team that won the League Cup in 2000. His standing in Scottish football also rested on 102 international appearances and 30 goals, with appearances at the World Cups of 1974, 1978 and 1982.

## A return with limits

Dalglish rejoined Liverpool in an Academy and ambassadorial role in 2009. In January 2011, after Roy Hodgson’s departure, he returned as manager on an interim basis.

Working with Steve Clarke, he helped Liverpool recover to sixth place. A 3–1 victory over Manchester United was among the clearest signs of improvement. He received a three-year contract in May, with the task of turning that recovery into sustained progress.

The following season brought Liverpool’s first trophy since 2006. They overcame Cardiff City on penalties in the League Cup final and also reached the FA Cup final, losing to Chelsea. Those cup runs were worthwhile achievements. Finishing eighth in the league left the larger objective unmet, and the club dismissed him in May 2012.

His handling of the Luis Suárez affair was a serious failure of judgement. In December 2011 an independent FA commission found that Suárez had racially abused Patrice Evra and imposed an eight-match ban. Dalglish publicly backed Suárez and wore a shirt supporting him, as players did before the match at Wigan. Loyalty to a player did not justify that response to a finding of racial abuse.

After Suárez refused Evra’s handshake at Old Trafford in February 2012, Dalglish apologised for his conduct in the subsequent television interview. His apology addressed the interview, rather than the earlier defence of Suárez following the commission’s decision. His achievements and his support for Liverpool’s people do not remove this episode from an honest assessment of his management.

## A lasting place

Dalglish returned to Liverpool’s board as a non-executive director in 2013. In 2017 the Centenary Stand was renamed in his honour. His knighthood followed in 2018, recognising contributions that included support for the Hillsborough families and the Marina Dalglish Appeal.

Established in 2005 after Marina’s treatment for breast cancer, the appeal has supported cancer care on Merseyside. It is another part of the family’s relationship with the area that extends beyond football.

There is a substantial case for considering Dalglish Liverpool’s most important individual figure. It rests on the breadth of what he contributed: the player who helped sustain European supremacy, the manager who won and rebuilt, and the man who stood alongside people when their connection to the club was bound up with bereavement.

The winner at Wembley and the title-clinching volley at Chelsea remain defining football moments. His place in Liverpool’s history also rests on less visible acts: visiting, listening and continuing to offer support when the match was long over.
