# Tommy Smith

Tommy Smith was there when Liverpool first won the FA Cup. He was captain when they first won a European trophy. When the European Cup finally arrived, he scored the goal that put them ahead for the second and decisive time. Across those three landmarks, in 1965, 1973 and 1977, his role changed from young defender to established leader to recalled veteran. His importance survived the changes around him.

The nickname ‘Anfield Iron’ captured the force of Smith’s football, but his career needs a wider description. Liverpool trusted him in central defence and at full-back; he could pass accurately, support an attack and finish. His 638 competitive appearances and 48 goals record both durability and a contribution extending beyond stopping opponents. Shankly’s Liverpool and Paisley’s Liverpool each had room for him, even when keeping that place meant accepting a different job.

Born in Liverpool on 5 April 1945, Smith joined the club in 1960. He came through its junior ranks and made his first-team debut against Birmingham City on 8 May 1963. The path was not an uninterrupted climb: he made no senior appearance in the following season, when Liverpool won the championship. Nor was his eventual position settled from the beginning. His early football included playing in attack before defence became his home.

He established himself during 1964–65, joining the side that took Liverpool to Wembley against Leeds United. Smith had only just turned 20. Around him were the players who had carried the club back towards the top of English football, including captain Ron Yeats, Roger Hunt and Ian St John. He had entered their team in time to help it achieve something none of its predecessors had managed.

The final remained goalless through 90 minutes. Gerry Byrne played on with a broken collarbone, and extra time brought goals from Hunt and Leeds’ Billy Bremner before St John’s header settled it 2–1. Smith’s first major honour was therefore also Liverpool’s first FA Cup. His place in that breakthrough was earned over a season of increasing responsibility, rather than bestowed on a youngster at the end of a successful campaign.

In 1965–66 he played in all 42 league fixtures as Liverpool became champions again. This time he was fully involved in the title, a regular member of a defence in which his strength and use of the ball complemented the imposing Yeats. The reliability continued: Smith was also ever-present in the league in 1966–67 and 1968–69. For a player remembered so often through a tackle or a confrontation, that sustained availability is a substantial part of the record.

He was young enough to remain important when Shankly rebuilt. The team of the early 1970s required experience as well as new players, and in 1970 Smith became captain. The appointment put a product of Liverpool’s own system in charge on the field as the club worked towards another championship. His leadership belonged to a period of development before it became associated with silverware.

The 1971 FA Cup final brought a painful interruption to that progress. Smith captained Liverpool against Arsenal and saw Steve Heighway give them the lead in extra time, only for Eddie Kelly and Charlie George to overturn it. A 2–1 defeat left him walking away from Wembley without the cup he had won six years earlier. Liverpool had reached another final with a changed team; completing the job remained the next demand.

Two years later Smith led the side to the league championship and UEFA Cup. The European final against Borussia Mönchengladbach required Liverpool to defend a result as well as create one. A 3–0 victory at Anfield gave them a considerable advantage, but two first-half goals by Jupp Heynckes in the return left that advantage precarious. They conceded no more. The 3–2 aggregate victory brought the club its first European trophy, and Smith collected it as captain.

Smith’s position changed in 1973. Emlyn Hughes succeeded him as captain, while the year also brought a dispute with Shankly over being left out against Arsenal. Losing the captaincy was a significant change for a player who had led a double-winning side, but his Liverpool career continued. He returned to regular football and contributed to another important change in the team, increasingly operating at full-back.

The 1974 FA Cup final supplied a particularly clear account of what he could do there. Liverpool beat Newcastle United 3–0, with Smith at right-back and Hughes as captain. Smith and Ian Callaghan were the two members of the starting team who had also played in the 1965 victory. Their presence joined two distinct Shankly sides on the same Wembley pitch.

For the third goal, Heighway found Smith advancing on the right of the penalty area. Smith sent the ball across for Kevin Keegan to finish. It was an attacking contribution requiring the right movement and a useful final pass, qualities easily lost beneath the reputation for ferocity. The score was already 2–0, but the move remains a good illustration of why Liverpool could use Smith outside the centre of their defence. He understood more than one part of the game.

Shankly’s retirement that summer brought Paisley to the manager’s office. Smith continued to contribute as Liverpool won another league and UEFA Cup double in 1975–76, although his place was becoming less secure. Younger defenders offered new competition, and the player who had once completed whole league seasons without missing a match now had to spend more time outside the starting side.

By 1976–77 he was contemplating the end. An injury to Phil Thompson brought him back into regular contention in March, and the final months of the season became far more substantial than a farewell appearance. Liverpool won the league again, and Smith was in the team chosen to face Mönchengladbach in the European Cup final in Rome. At 32, he had an opportunity to add the trophy that remained absent from his career.

On 25 May, Terry McDermott put Liverpool ahead before Allan Simonsen equalised. After the hour, Steve Heighway’s corner from the left reached Smith, whose header restored Liverpool’s lead. Phil Neal’s subsequent penalty made the final score 3–1. Smith’s goal was the decisive intervention between a match level at 1–1 and the victory that established Liverpool as European champions for the first time.

He stayed for one more season. Liverpool added the European Super Cup and retained the European Cup, with Smith contributing to the campaign but missing the final through an injury suffered away from football. The distinction matters: Rome was his winning European Cup final appearance; the following year he helped Liverpool reach another triumph without taking the field at Wembley. His final season still produced 34 competitive appearances.

Smith left in 1978 and joined former team-mate John Toshack at Swansea City. In 1978–79 he made 43 appearances and scored twice as Swansea gained promotion from the Third Division, then retired from playing. His international career had been much shorter than his club career: England selected him once, against Wales in 1971. Liverpool supplied the lasting measure of his football.

When Smith died on 12 April 2019, aged 74, after living with dementia, the tributes included memories of the support he gave others. Dalglish recalled Smith helping him and his wife settle after their move to Merseyside. Phil Thompson remembered the commitment that had made Smith compelling to watch from the Kop before he became a team-mate. Those recollections added a human dimension to the familiar image of the uncompromising defender.

Howard Gayle later described suffering racist abuse from Smith at Melwood in his autobiography, published in 2016.

His Liverpool career covered four championship-winning campaigns, two FA Cup victories and the club’s emergence as a European power. It also contained a lost captaincy, disappointment at Wembley and periods when he had to win back his place. Smith kept contributing through them. The header in Rome is the moment most readily recalled; the long career that made him available to score it is just as important.
