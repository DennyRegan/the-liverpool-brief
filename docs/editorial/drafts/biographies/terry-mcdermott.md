# Terry McDermott: the running and the reward

Terry McDermott’s most celebrated Liverpool goal began at the other end of the pitch. Against Tottenham Hotspur at Anfield on 2 September 1978, he went from helping defend a corner to heading in Steve Heighway’s cross as Liverpool completed a 7–0 victory. David Johnson’s pass had released Heighway down the left; McDermott supplied the movement that turned the cross into a finish. The goal brings several of his qualities into one sequence: stamina, anticipation and the willingness to keep joining an attack even when the match was already won.

There were many other goals, some more important to the result and some still more difficult to execute. Across 329 competitive Liverpool appearances McDermott scored 81 times, a formidable return from midfield. But the record had an unpromising beginning. The Liverpool supporter from Kirkby reached his boyhood club only to discover that belonging to its squad and establishing himself in its team were very different achievements.

## Home, but waiting

McDermott had started his professional career at Bury before moving to Newcastle United. In May 1974 he played against Liverpool in the FA Cup final, a 3–0 defeat for Newcastle and Bill Shankly’s final competitive match as Liverpool manager. Six months later Bob Paisley brought him to Anfield for £175,000. The change carried an obvious personal appeal, yet Liverpool were acquiring an experienced First Division player rather than simply granting a local supporter his wish.

His debut came in the Merseyside derby at Goodison Park on 16 November 1974. Phil Neal also made his first Liverpool appearance in the 0–0 draw. Paisley had put two of the players who would become associated with his greatest sides into the same demanding occasion. Their subsequent progress, however, differed sharply. McDermott did not rapidly become an automatic selection.

He made 15 appearances during the remainder of 1974–75 and only ten in all competitions the next season. Initially used in a run of league games, he then found himself moving in and out of the side. Brian Hall and Jimmy Case were among the midfield alternatives. Liverpool could be patient with a signing because the team had other ways to function; the player had to live with the frustration that patience imposed.

In 1975–76 the club won the league and UEFA Cup while McDermott remained on the margins. He made only nine league appearances and did not receive a championship medal. For him, the season’s successful conclusion was accompanied by the difficulty of being less involved than he had hoped.

The breakthrough came in 1976–77, though even then his progress was uneven. He competed for selection before becoming a regular towards the season’s end. His total rose to 38 appearances, and he helped Liverpool retain the league championship. The rise in responsibility is as revealing as the trophy: he was now being trusted to influence games that would decide the club’s ambitions.

## Two goals on the way to Rome

The FA Cup semi-final against Everton at Maine Road on 23 April 1977 gave McDermott one of his first enduring Liverpool moments. He opened the scoring with a delicate chip over David Lawson. Liverpool had a player capable of changing an apparently obvious shooting opportunity into something the goalkeeper was less prepared to face. The match ended 2–2, and Liverpool won the replay 3–0.

Manchester United then beat Liverpool 2–1 in the final. For McDermott, who had been a losing finalist with Newcastle three years earlier, Wembley again ended in disappointment. Liverpool’s possible treble had gone, and the players had only four days before the European Cup final. The most important week of his career required him to move quickly from a lost opportunity to another challenge.

At the Stadio Olimpico on 25 May, McDermott supplied the opening goal against Borussia Mönchengladbach. He moved into space to receive Heighway’s pass and finished in the 27th minute. The run was characteristic: Liverpool’s attack acquired another threat because a midfielder recognised where he could arrive, rather than waiting for the ball to come to him in a settled position.

Allan Simonsen equalised after half-time, but Tommy Smith’s header and Neal’s penalty secured a 3–1 victory. Liverpool had won their first European Cup, and McDermott had made a defining contribution to it. The transformation from the reserves to Rome had taken time. Its result was a player whose place in the club’s most important story could no longer be confined to the list of available squad members.

## Becoming indispensable

McDermott later identified another European night as a turning point in his confidence. Hamburg came to Anfield for the second leg of the Super Cup on 6 December 1977, bringing Kevin Keegan back to the ground he had left that summer. Liverpool won 6–0, completing a 7–1 aggregate victory. McDermott scored three times, with his goals coming in a burst spanning the end of the first half and the beginning of the second.

His retrospective explanation concerned position as much as finishing. Moved in from a wider role, he felt encouragement to regard the central area as his natural territory. That recollection helps explain why the hat-trick mattered to him alongside the Rome goal. A player could become a European champion while still finding the position and confidence that would make his contribution repeatable.

Liverpool retained the European Cup in May 1978, McDermott starting the final against Club Brugge at Wembley. Dalglish scored the only goal. The following September’s seventh goal against Tottenham then displayed a different relationship between individual and team success. McDermott’s finish was spectacular, but the quality of the move depended on the passes and running that preceded it. He was both a beneficiary of Liverpool’s football and one of the players who made it possible.

The range of his contribution was broader than running into the penalty area. His passing gave teammates opportunities; his movement provided them with options. Alongside Souness, Ray Kennedy and Liverpool’s other midfielders, he helped connect controlled possession to forward progress. A powerful shot meant that opponents could not treat his arrival outside the box as harmless, while his willingness to keep moving made him difficult to account for throughout a match.

That influence could decide less extravagant contests too. On 9 December 1978 he scored both goals in a 2–0 league victory over Nottingham Forest at Anfield. The first was a penalty; the second came after the interval. Liverpool ended Forest’s 42-match unbeaten league run and struck at a rival that had frustrated them repeatedly. McDermott could be the decisive scorer against champions as well as the final participant in a seven-goal celebration.

Liverpool reclaimed the league in 1978–79 and retained it in 1979–80. The latter season brought McDermott an exceptional individual distinction: he became the first player to win the Football Writers’ Association Footballer of the Year and the PFA Players’ Player of the Year awards in the same season. The recognition reached beyond Liverpool. Writers and fellow professionals had both judged his contribution worthy of their leading award.

## The spectacular and the necessary

Another goal against Tottenham stood out during that award-winning season. At White Hart Lane on 8 March 1980, McDermott put the ball into the air and volleyed it into the far corner. Liverpool won the FA Cup quarter-final 1–0, and the strike became the BBC’s goal of the season. It was a moment of unusual technique carrying a straightforward competitive consequence: without another Liverpool goal, that finish separated the teams.

McDermott’s own later account was less polished than the repeated highlights might suggest. He was uncertain whether the touch that lifted the ball had been intentional or poor control. His willingness to leave that uncertainty intact is part of the goal’s appeal.

At Pittodrie on 22 October 1980 he produced another remarkable winner, chipping from a narrow angle against Aberdeen. Liverpool won the European Cup second-round first leg 1–0. The goal showed a different answer to a different problem: where the Spurs volley relied on striking through the ball, this occasion rewarded the judgement to lift it. McDermott had several ways to turn an opening into a goal.

The 1980–81 season was his most prolific at Liverpool, with 22 goals in 60 appearances across all competitions. Liverpool lost the league title, but the season ended with the club’s first League Cup and another European Cup. In the League Cup final replay against West Ham at Villa Park, McDermott helped create Dalglish’s equaliser before Hansen scored the winner in a 2–1 victory.

He then started against Real Madrid in Paris on 27 May. Alan Kennedy’s goal secured a 1–0 win and McDermott’s third European Cup medal. The finals connect two sides of his value. He could supply an important attacking contribution himself, as in Rome, or remain part of the midfield through which Liverpool worked towards another player’s decisive moment.

## A changing place in the team

McDermott continued scoring in 1981–82. His 20 goals included 14 in the league, his highest league total for Liverpool. Yet he played 29 league games rather than the previous season’s 40. The scoring figures alone cannot describe his circumstances: he remained productive while his place became less secure as Paisley renewed the side.

Ronnie Whelan, Sammy Lee and Craig Johnston provided younger options. The League Cup final against Tottenham on 13 March 1982 offered a particularly clear picture of a team changing while it continued to win. McDermott started but was replaced by David Johnson before Whelan equalised late in normal time. Whelan scored again in extra time and Rush added the third as Liverpool won 3–1.

Liverpool also regained the league championship. McDermott’s last three full seasons had yielded 58 goals, but the returns did not prevent his departure. Johnston’s growing involvement contributed to the pressure on his position. As at the beginning of his Liverpool career, first-team football could not be assumed; this time he was an established winner facing the claims of those trying to become one.

His final appearance came against Dundalk at Anfield on 28 September 1982. Liverpool won 1–0, McDermott supplying Whelan for the winner. The moment is a useful final image: the departing midfielder helping a younger player decide a European tie. He returned to Newcastle later that month.

## A Liverpool life beyond the team

McDermott’s later football career included a return to Newcastle’s coaching staff alongside Keegan, followed by work with other managers, including former Liverpool teammates Dalglish and Souness. Those connections extended the relationships formed during his playing years. His allegiance to Liverpool also remained visible through visits, hospitality appearances and the sharing of memories with supporters at Anfield.

In an interview with Tony Barrett, McDermott described the appeal of the ground when it was quiet and the strange distance between the retired player and the young man who had once run across its pitch. He still returned to the Tottenham header when telling that story. The goal offered a concrete connection between the supporter he had been, the player he became and the club whose history he could now explain to others.

In August 2021, McDermott publicly announced through Liverpool that tests had led to a diagnosis of early-stage Lewy body dementia. The club offered support to him and his family, and he spoke about wanting to continue attending matches. His words made the enduring human relationships around his football career clear, beyond the scores and medals by which that career is usually measured.

McDermott’s Liverpool story remains one of a place earned slowly and used expansively. He could read a pass, make a run and finish with conviction, then do the work again. The unforgettable goals preserve the brilliance. The difficult beginning, sustained contribution and eventual challenge from younger players show the full career behind them.
