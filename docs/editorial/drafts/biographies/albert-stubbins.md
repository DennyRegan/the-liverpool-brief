# Albert Stubbins: the centre-forward of Liverpool’s post-war revival

Albert Stubbins met Billy Liddell’s delivery almost horizontally above the frozen Anfield pitch on 1 March 1947. The diving header beat Birmingham City goalkeeper Gil Merrick, and the centre-forward slid across the ground after the ball had gone in. It was his second goal in a hat-trick which carried Liverpool to a 4–1 FA Cup quarter-final victory. Liddell later remembered the flight and force of the finish; Stubbins remembered the damage to his knees. The move combined the skill and physical commitment which made him such an immediate Liverpool favourite.

He had arrived less than six months earlier, recruited by George Kay after a competition for his signature which included Everton. Liverpool were resuming normal league football after the war, and the striker from Newcastle became central to their first championship since 1923. His Liverpool career would contain a Cup final, a contractual dispute and a decline in availability as well as that triumphant beginning. The complete record was 178 appearances and 84 goals. Its outstanding season belonged to a club and a manager rebuilding their ambitions after an interruption of seven years.

Stubbins was born in Wallsend and trained as a draughtsman. His early football took him through amateur involvement with Sunderland to Newcastle United before the war. Wartime regional competitions gave him extensive experience and a formidable scoring reputation, although their goals belonged to a different category from the peacetime Football League record. By 1946, Liverpool were pursuing an accomplished forward. His request for a transfer offered a route into the First Division while Newcastle remained in the Second.

Kay had managed Liverpool since 1936. He had brought Liddell and Bob Paisley to the club before the war and, as league football approached its return, took his players on a tour of the United States and Canada. That preparation helped the existing team come together; Stubbins joined later, after the new season had begun. He was an addition to work already under way. Liverpool’s opening results showed both promise and fragility, with a 7–4 win over Chelsea followed by a 5–0 defeat against Manchester United.

The transfer was completed on 12 September 1946. Liverpool paid a club-record £13,000, a substantial commitment to solving their need for a centre-forward. Stubbins was at the cinema while officials sought him, and contemporary reporting described messages sent to cinemas asking him to go to St James’ Park. His own later account gave the episode its famous coin toss: with Liverpool and Everton waiting, he used it to decide which club to hear first. Kay and chairman Billy McConnell impressed him sufficiently to secure his agreement. The coin determined the order of conversation; Stubbins made the decision about his career.

He made his debut two days later at Bolton Wanderers. Liverpool’s lead had been cancelled when Stubbins scored in the 82nd minute, and Jack Balmer added another goal to complete a 3–1 victory. That was an immediate return on the investment, but also an early indication of the relationship at the front of Kay’s side. Balmer and Stubbins would finish the league campaign with 24 goals each. Liverpool now had two forwards capable of turning opportunities into a sustained challenge.

Stubbins possessed the strength associated with a traditional centre-forward, together with speed and skill on the ball. He could move past an opponent, combine with teammates and finish the attack himself. A Liverpool Echo preview of his Anfield debut anticipated a demanding afternoon for Everton centre-half Tommy Jones because of the new striker’s pace. Liddell’s service and Balmer’s presence gave him partners rather than leaving all the attacking work to one man. The threat came from the qualities they brought together.

The Liverpool supporters helped him settle. Stubbins later remembered a patience with his poorer matches which some teammates did not enjoy. His ability gave them plenty to celebrate, and the championship campaign supplied occasions on which a new signing could quickly become part of the club’s identity. The goals counted towards the same cause as Balmer’s prolific scoring and Liddell’s attacking influence. Stubbins had joined a team with ambitions he could recognise, then made himself one of the reasons those ambitions became credible.

The Birmingham victory expressed more than one centre-forward’s bravery. A contemporary Daily Mirror report described Liverpool deliberately concentrating attacks through the middle, with Balmer playing close to Stubbins while Willie Fagan’s position on the right continued to occupy the opposition. Ray Lambert explained the plan afterwards. The diving header became the remembered image, but the hat-trick also belonged to a team which had examined how Birmingham defended and arranged its forwards to exploit the space.

Liverpool reached the FA Cup semi-final, where Burnley held them to a goalless draw at Ewood Park before winning the replay 1–0 at Maine Road. The chance of a league and Cup double had gone. The league challenge survived, however, and Liverpool won six and drew one of their final seven league fixtures. The run included victories over Manchester United and Arsenal before the visit to Wolverhampton Wanderers. A difficult season, stretched by severe winter weather, had left the championship unresolved at the end of May.

On 31 May 1947 Liverpool travelled to Molineux with 55 points, one fewer than Wolves. A draw would leave their hosts ahead, so Kay’s team needed to win. Balmer scored first; Stubbins added the second before half-time, converting a move supplied by Bob Priday. Jimmy Dunn pulled a goal back for Wolves, and Liverpool had to withstand the pressure which followed. Cyril Sidlow’s goalkeeping helped preserve the 2–1 victory. The striker’s twenty-fourth league goal had supplied the margin his teammates defended.

Even then, Liverpool could not celebrate a confirmed championship. Stoke City still had a game left and would take the title on goal average if they won at Sheffield United. The wait lasted a fortnight. On 14 June, while Liverpool were playing Everton in the Liverpool Senior Cup final at Anfield, news came through that Stoke had lost. Liverpool were champions of England for the fifth time. Stubbins’s contribution was unmistakable, but the sequence also shows why the Molineux win and the actual confirmation of the title were separate events.

The following season confirmed his individual quality while changing the team’s prospects. Stubbins played 40 league matches and scored 25 goals, becoming Liverpool’s outright leading league scorer. On 6 March 1948 he scored all four in a 4–0 victory over Huddersfield Town at Anfield. Yet Liverpool could not sustain their position among the title contenders. The centre-forward’s productivity continued after the championship; the side’s success did not simply reproduce itself around him.

The interruption in 1948–49 was more complicated than a loss of form. Stubbins delayed signing his contract, and his appearances fell to eighteen across league and Cup. In his later explanation, the disagreement concerned an arrangement McConnell had made for him to write a weekly Football Echo column. Journalism was already part of his plans for life beyond playing. After the chairman’s death, Stubbins said the board did not consider itself bound by the promise, and the uncertainty delayed his signing.

He remembered the matter as amicable, while injuries also restricted the season. The player was thinking about a second career as well as negotiating his immediate football. The loss to Liverpool was nevertheless real. A forward who had been almost continuously available during his first two seasons now supplied six league goals in fifteen league appearances. The team had to continue without the same dependable contribution from its recent championship centre-forward.

Stubbins returned to a larger role in 1949–50, playing 28 league matches and reaching ten league goals. Liverpool began the season with nineteen unbeaten league games, before their early promise weakened. His football still had variety. In a goalless derby that August, Leslie Edwards’s contemporary report singled out a Stubbins dribble which created a chance for Liddell, whose shot struck a post. It is a useful glimpse of the ability behind the scoring record: even a match without goals could contain a piece of attacking play shaped by his close control.

The FA Cup offered another opportunity. At Stockport County on 11 February 1950, Stubbins scored Liverpool’s second goal after Fagan had opened the scoring. A late reply left the final score 2–1. He then led the attack in the 2–0 semi-final win over Everton at Maine Road, where Paisley and Liddell scored. Three years after the Birmingham hat-trick had taken Liverpool to the last four, Stubbins had reached the final itself. The club would appear at Wembley for the first time.

Arsenal won that final 2–0 on 29 April, with Reg Lewis scoring both goals. Stubbins started at centre-forward in a Liverpool side containing several of his championship teammates, but they could not produce another trophy. In a souvenir account of his career at the time, he had identified Molineux and the Everton semi-final as especially enduring memories. Wembley added the disappointment of a great occasion which did not deliver the hoped-for ending. His championship medal remained the major honour of his Liverpool career.

The final also affected his friend Paisley, who was left out despite scoring in the semi-final. Stubbins later recalled advising him against making a hurried decision to leave Liverpool in his disappointment. The advice reflected the consideration he offered a teammate at a difficult moment. Both were from the North-East, and their friendship continued beyond their playing years. He could be forceful in the penalty area without becoming an indifferent colleague outside it.

Injuries and reduced selection gradually brought his Liverpool playing career to its close. He made 24 appearances in 1950–51, twelve the following season and five in 1952–53. Kay retired through illness in 1951, and Stubbins’s final match came under Don Welsh: a 3–1 defeat at Stoke City on 3 January 1953. The new signing Sammy Smyth was also in the side that day. Stubbins’s place was becoming part of the team’s past while Liverpool searched for forwards who could meet its present needs.

He left later in 1953 for a brief player-coach spell at Ashington. Football and writing remained connected to the rest of his working life, which included coaching in New York and sports journalism back in the North-East. Liverpool retained an attachment to him after his return home. When he died in December 2002, contemporary tributes remembered his approachable manner as well as the bravery and finishing which had made him a post-war favourite.

His photograph also appeared in the collage on the Beatles’ 1967 album Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band. Stubbins recalled being asked for permission to use it. That became an unusual additional trace of his fame, but his Liverpool standing had already been established on the pitch. The championship goals, the Birmingham header and the later Cup run explain it more fully: he brought a proven forward’s ability into Kay’s revived team and helped give Anfield its first great successes after the war.
