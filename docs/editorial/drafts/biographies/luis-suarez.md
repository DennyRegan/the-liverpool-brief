# Luis Suárez: brilliance, responsibility and Liverpool’s near miss

There were evenings when Luis Suárez seemed to expand the possibilities of a football pitch. Against Norwich City at Anfield in December 2013, a dropping ball far from goal became an invitation to shoot. A defender blocking his route became somebody over whom the ball could be lifted. A free-kick offered another way to place a finish beyond John Ruddy. Four goals arrived through different solutions to different problems, and Liverpool won 5–1. The variety mattered as much as the number. Suárez could invent chances where another forward might see no chance at all.

That evening belongs within a Liverpool career which was both exceptional and deeply troubled. Between January 2011 and the summer of 2014, he scored 82 goals in 133 competitive appearances, won the League Cup and became the outstanding attacking player in a team that came close to the league title. He also received major suspensions for racially abusing Patrice Evra and biting Branislav Ivanović. His ability transformed matches; his misconduct harmed opponents, damaged Liverpool’s reputation and repeatedly deprived the team of his services. Both are essential to understanding his time at Anfield.

## The forward Liverpool recruited

Suárez was born in Salto, Uruguay, on 24 January 1987 and began his senior career with Nacional in Montevideo. His route to Liverpool passed through the Netherlands, where Groningen signed him in 2006. Ten goals in 29 league appearances during his first Dutch season were enough to attract Ajax. In August 2007, the Amsterdam club agreed a €7.5 million transfer for the 20-year-old. Groningen’s insistence on an agreed fee, following an unsuccessful attempt to escape his contract through arbitration, meant this was already a career shaped by ambition and contested moves.

Ajax gave that ambition a larger stage. Suárez’s scoring accelerated, reaching 35 Eredivisie goals in 2009–10. He became captain and passed a century of first-team goals for the club in July 2010. These were substantial achievements before Liverpool entered the picture. The player arriving in England had already learned to carry responsibility in a major European club’s attack, to operate against organised defences and to produce goals at a rate which made further interest inevitable.

His reputation also carried a warning. In November 2010 he bit PSV Eindhoven midfielder Otman Bakkal and received a seven-match domestic suspension. The disciplinary problems that followed in England did not begin with the unfamiliarity of the Premier League. Liverpool were recruiting an unusually gifted footballer whose conduct had already brought a serious sanction in another country.

International football had made him still more visible. At the 2010 World Cup he scored three goals as Uruguay reached the last four, but was sent off for deliberately handling on the goal line against Ghana in the quarter-final. Asamoah Gyan missed the resulting penalty and Uruguay progressed through the shoot-out; Suárez missed the semi-final through suspension. The incident became part of his worldwide notoriety. Liverpool’s new striker was already known for both decisive attacking contributions and a willingness to cross a boundary in pursuit of victory.

The move to Merseyside came during Liverpool’s own upheaval. Kenny Dalglish had returned as manager in January 2011, taking charge of a club rebuilding confidence after a difficult period. A deal worth up to £22.8 million was agreed with Ajax near the end of the transfer window. Suárez arrived on deadline day, when Fernando Torres left for Chelsea and Andy Carroll joined from Newcastle. The changes made Liverpool’s attack a subject of immediate scrutiny. Suárez inherited the No.7 shirt associated with Dalglish, but his first task was to establish how his own football would fit.

## An immediate impression, then a difficult first full season

His debut on 2 February supplied a quick answer. Liverpool were leading Stoke City 1–0 when he replaced Fabio Aurelio after 63 minutes. Sixteen minutes later he rounded the goalkeeper and scored, despite a defender’s attempt to clear the ball before it crossed the line. The finish completed a 2–0 win at the Kop end. It offered an early glimpse of the movement and confidence which would make his presence so conspicuous.

More revealing was the 3–1 home victory over Manchester United in March. Dirk Kuyt scored all three Liverpool goals, while Suárez’s contribution to their creation made him central to the afternoon. He could unsettle a defence without receiving the final statistical reward. Close control allowed him to keep moving in crowded spaces, and an apparent loss of balance or imperfect touch did not necessarily end an attack. Defenders had to continue responding because he continued trying.

His initial half-season comprised 13 league appearances and four goals. The influence was greater than that modest scoring total suggests, but the distinction is useful: the record-breaking finisher of 2013–14 did not emerge fully formed on his first day in England. Liverpool first saw a forward who could open matches through individual invention. The increasingly reliable conversion of those openings developed afterwards.

The summer of 2011 brought success with Uruguay. Suárez helped win the Copa América and was named the tournament’s best player. He scored both goals in the semi-final against Peru and opened the scoring in the 3–0 final victory over Paraguay. Returning to Liverpool with an international trophy reinforced his standing. He was capable of performing at the centre of a successful side, rather than simply supplying spectacular moments in a team seeking its direction.

At Liverpool, the cup competitions provided the clearest rewards of 2011–12. His two goals at Stoke in October overturned a deficit and secured a 2–1 League Cup win. The equaliser was a curling finish after he worked room for himself; the winner was a late header. The contrast illustrated the range Liverpool could use: an inventive dribbler could also finish a cross through timing and determination. His contribution helped sustain the run which eventually brought the club’s first trophy since 2006.

The final against Cardiff City in February 2012 required extra time and penalties after a 2–2 draw. Suárez played in Liverpool’s victory, although Martin Škrtel and Kuyt supplied the goals before the shoot-out. It remained his only major team honour at the club. That fact gives his career an unusual shape. Much of his greatest football came later, when the team’s attacking standard rose sharply without bringing another trophy.

He also helped Liverpool reach the FA Cup final. Against Everton in the semi-final at Wembley, he equalised before Carroll headed the winner in a 2–1 victory. In the league, his understanding with Steven Gerrard was evident when he created two of the captain’s three goals in a 3–0 Anfield derby win. These were occasions when his work served a teammate’s finish and Liverpool’s collective purpose. The season nevertheless remained inconsistent, and the FA Cup final ended in defeat by Chelsea. Seventeen goals in 39 appearances represented a useful return rather than the complete fulfilment of his talent.

## The Evra case and Liverpool’s response

The most serious events of that season concerned his conduct towards Manchester United defender Patrice Evra during the 1–1 draw at Anfield on 15 October 2011. On 20 December, an independent FA regulatory commission found that Suárez had used insulting words which referred to Evra’s colour. It imposed an eight-match first-team suspension and a £40,000 fine. This was a disciplinary finding of racial abuse, reached under the FA’s rules and civil standard of proof.

The commission examined witness accounts, video evidence and Spanish-language expert evidence. Its written reasons found Evra credible and rejected important parts of Suárez’s account as unreliable. The experts considered the alleged language racially offensive in the circumstances, including in Uruguay. The decision cannot accurately be reduced to a harmless word misunderstood by English football. The confrontation and the commission’s findings about what was said were central to the ruling.

Liverpool defended Suárez publicly. On 3 January 2012 the club announced that it would not appeal, while continuing to dispute the commission’s conclusions. Suárez separately maintained his denial. On 5 January he apologised for offence caused while still insisting that his use of the term had not been derogatory. The distinction matters because an apology for offence was not an acceptance of the commission’s account of the exchange.

The dispute brought another damaging episode at Old Trafford in February. Suárez did not shake Evra’s hand before the match. On 12 February he apologised for that decision, and managing director Ian Ayre said the player had assured Liverpool beforehand that he would take part in the handshake. Ayre described his behaviour as unacceptable. The club’s public criticism on this occasion differed markedly from its earlier defence of him.

These episodes place responsibilities on the way Suárez is remembered. His football did not invalidate Evra’s complaint or the disciplinary finding. Liverpool’s loyalty to a player did not make every argument advanced in his defence correct. The case also showed how a club’s response could extend the damage beyond the original incident. Admiration for his attacking skill and an accurate account of what occurred can coexist, but the latter must have its own weight.

## Greater responsibility under Rodgers

Brendan Rodgers became manager in 2012 and sought to build a more coherent possession and attacking game. Suárez signed a new contract that summer. He was increasingly the player to whom Liverpool turned when a promising passage needed a decisive conclusion, or when possession alone had failed to produce one. His role required movement across the front rather than an unchanging position between the centre-backs.

The Europa League qualifier against Hearts in August illustrated the immediate dependence. Liverpool were in danger of being taken to extra time when Suárez scored late to secure a 2–1 aggregate victory. He carried the ball forward, escaped defenders and finished from an awkward position. That intervention belonged to a broader pattern: he could rescue a game through an attack substantially of his own making, even while Rodgers was trying to establish a system in which chances would be shared.

Against Newcastle at Anfield in November, he controlled José Enrique’s long pass on his chest while running, went around Tim Krul and equalised in a 1–1 draw. It was a remarkable piece of technique performed at speed. Yet the result also exposed a limitation of Liverpool’s position. A goal of exceptional quality could earn only a point. Suárez could change an individual passage of play more readily than he could remove every weakness in a developing team.

Daniel Sturridge’s arrival in January 2013 helped distribute the attacking burden. Another striker able to move quickly, finish accurately and combine near the penalty area gave Suárez a different partnership from the one Liverpool had attempted with Carroll. Philippe Coutinho’s arrival added further imagination behind them. Rodgers now had the beginnings of an attack in which Suárez’s movement could lead somebody else into space, rather than always ending with his own shot.

His seasonal return rose to 30 goals in 44 appearances, including 23 in the league. He finished second to Gareth Bale in the PFA Players’ Player of the Year vote and was named in its team of the year. Liverpool finished seventh, but their leading scorer was performing at a level which made the distance between individual quality and collective results increasingly apparent. The next challenge would be to turn that quality into a sustained challenge near the top.

## Ivanović, suspension and a contested summer

Before that could happen, Suárez committed another serious offence. During the 2–2 draw with Chelsea at Anfield on 21 April 2013, he bit Ivanović’s arm. The match officials did not see the incident. Suárez remained on the field and scored Liverpool’s late equaliser, but the goal could not diminish what had happened earlier. The club issued his apology that evening, alongside statements from Ayre and Rodgers condemning the behaviour.

On 24 April an FA commission imposed a ten-match suspension. Suárez had admitted violent conduct but disputed the FA’s argument that the standard three-match penalty was insufficient. The commission agreed that a longer punishment was required. He did not appeal. The suspension covered the final four fixtures of 2012–13 and the first six competitive fixtures of the following season, including five Premier League matches. It would be inaccurate to describe him as missing the first six league games of 2013–14.

The summer also brought a dispute over his future. Arsenal’s £40 million plus £1 offer was rejected by Liverpool, and Rodgers made Suárez train separately during the disagreement. His wish to leave put the relationship under strain after the club had supported him through earlier difficulties. Liverpool retained him, but the eventual title challenge should not make that outcome appear inevitable. The player around whom the attack would be built had recently been seeking a different club.

He returned to competitive football in September. On 20 December he signed another long-term contract. Whatever uncertainty had preceded it, his performance now supplied powerful evidence of renewed commitment on the pitch. The first major task was to win back places and points that his own suspension had put beyond reach; the larger opportunity was to help a better-balanced Liverpool side make use of his most productive years.

## The extraordinary season of 2013–14

Suárez’s final Liverpool campaign produced 31 Premier League goals in 33 appearances, none from penalties. He equalled the then record for a 38-match Premier League season despite missing its first five league fixtures. The achievement joined scoring volume to invention. Liverpool could threaten through combinations, counter-attacks, set-pieces or an isolated moment in which he found an unexpected route towards goal.

The Norwich match on 4 December remains the clearest concentrated example. His four goals included the long-range volley, a close-range finish, the lifted ball and driven shot past a defender, and the free-kick. He also helped create Raheem Sterling’s goal. Norwich had already conceded two hat-tricks to him at Carrow Road in 2012; this was his third league treble against the same opponents. The repeat did not make the performance routine. Its force lay in how many different methods the defenders were required to confront.

Eleven days later he captained Liverpool in Gerrard’s absence and scored twice in a 5–0 victory at Tottenham. Jordan Henderson, Jon Flanagan and Sterling also scored. Suárez’s excellence was now embedded in a team able to overwhelm an opponent collectively. The speed of the supporting runs and the willingness of midfielders and defenders to advance gave his decisions more consequences. An opponent concentrating on him could find another Liverpool player arriving unmarked.

The partnership with Sturridge became central to the title challenge. At Cardiff in March, Liverpool recovered from two deficits to win 6–3. Suárez scored three times, Sturridge once, and each created a goal for the other. Sturridge’s back-heel into Suárez’s run was a particularly clear illustration of their understanding. Neither forward needed to occupy one fixed lane. Their movement gave Rodgers choices about how to arrange the attack while preserving its capacity to combine.

Liverpool’s momentum gathered through the spring. An eleven-match league winning run included a 5–1 demolition of Arsenal, a 3–0 win at Manchester United and a 3–2 home victory over Manchester City. Suárez was the leading scorer, but Sturridge, Sterling, Coutinho, Henderson and Gerrard were integral to the surge. The team had moved from seventh place the previous season to a position in which supporters could reasonably believe the long wait for a championship might end.

It did not. Chelsea won 2–0 at Anfield, then Crystal Palace recovered from 3–0 down to draw 3–3. Liverpool finished second, two points behind Manchester City. Suárez’s distress after the Palace match became an enduring image of the season. His 31 league goals earned the Golden Boot, the PFA players’ and football writers’ awards, and the Premier League Player of the Season award. He also shared the European Golden Shoe with Cristiano Ronaldo. The individual recognition was extensive; the missing league trophy remained the defining collective disappointment.

## Leaving, and the legacy of a short career

The events which followed must be kept distinct from the Ivanović suspension. At the 2014 World Cup, Suárez bit Italy defender Giorgio Chiellini during Uruguay’s match on 24 June. FIFA imposed a nine-match international suspension and a four-month ban from football-related activity. In August, the Court of Arbitration for Sport confirmed the principal sanctions but limited the four-month suspension to official matches, permitting training and other football activities. This was a separate offence and sanction in international football.

Liverpool announced a transfer agreement with Barcelona on 11 July. Rodgers said the club had reluctantly accepted Suárez’s wish to move to Spain, while the player cited a long-held ambition and his wife’s family connection to the country. The transfer ended his Liverpool career after three and a half years. He had helped restore Champions League qualification, but would leave before representing the club in that competition.

At Barcelona, he went on to win the Champions League and score in the 2015 final against Juventus. His subsequent achievements, including four Spanish league titles with Barcelona and another with Atlético Madrid, confirmed that Liverpool had employed a forward capable of sustained success at the highest level. They help explain the scale of the talent which had briefly belonged to Anfield.

Suárez’s Liverpool legacy is unusually concentrated. One League Cup sits beside an extraordinary final league season, repeated moments of invention and a title challenge which stopped just short. His movement and technique made spectators expect something unlikely to happen; his partnership with Sturridge helped turn that expectation into a formidable team attack. The disciplinary findings and suspensions also remain part of the record. Liverpool’s history has room to recognise the footballing greatness fully while remembering, with equal clarity, the responsibilities he failed to meet.
