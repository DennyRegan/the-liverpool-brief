# Bob Paisley: Liverpool’s greatest manager

Six league championships. Three European Cups. Nine seasons in charge. Bob Paisley’s record is the strongest argument for his place as Liverpool’s greatest manager. Its significance becomes clearer when you look at how he achieved it: by improving an outstanding team, replacing its most influential players and continuing to win.

He inherited a club transformed by Bill Shankly and took it to its first European Cup. Then he won two more. Across his reign, Liverpool could lose a major player, change their captain or rebuild their attack and still finish as champions. Paisley made those transitions part of the club’s success.

By the time he became manager in 1974, he had already spent 35 years at Anfield.

Born in Hetton-le-Hole, County Durham, on 23 January 1919, Paisley joined Liverpool from Bishop Auckland in May 1939. War interrupted his career before he could establish himself. He served with the Royal Artillery in North Africa and Italy, returning to a football career that would bring both a championship and a lasting personal disappointment.

Playing at left-half, he made 33 league appearances in Liverpool’s title-winning season of 1946–47. In 1950, he scored against Everton in the FA Cup semi-final but was omitted from the side for the final against Arsenal. He later said that experience helped him when he had to make difficult selection decisions himself.

His playing career ended in 1954, the year Liverpool were relegated. He had made 277 competitive appearances and scored 13 goals, with wartime football counted separately. He stayed to work with the reserves and became the club’s physiotherapist, building the knowledge of players and their injuries for which he remained renowned.

Shankly arrived in 1959 to find Paisley already on the staff. Their work together helped restore Liverpool to the summit of English football. Paisley belonged to the group of coaches associated with the Boot Room, alongside men including Joe Fagan and Ronnie Moran. They discussed performances, opponents, recruitment and preparation. The room became famous because of the work done by the people in it.

That collective expertise mattered throughout Paisley’s career. His achievements were supported by coaches and scouts who knew the club’s requirements intimately. He had helped develop the system he would eventually lead.

When Shankly retired in 1974, Paisley needed persuading to take over. At 55, he was accepting his first senior managerial appointment. He lacked Shankly’s appetite for public performance, but the players already knew his football judgement. Writing after Paisley’s death, Ray Clemence recalled a manager who explained his tactical instructions simply, commanded respect and could be compassionate when a player had problems away from football.

Liverpool finished second in his first league campaign. Paisley then began making changes that would give the team a different shape.

One of his most important decisions involved Ray Kennedy, Shankly’s final signing. Kennedy had arrived from Arsenal as a forward and struggled to establish his best form. Paisley moved him to the left of midfield. From there, Kennedy became an essential member of the teams that won all three of Paisley’s European Cups. It was an example of his ability to recognise what a player could contribute beyond the position in which he had been bought.

The first league title came in 1976. Liverpool trailed at Wolves in their final league fixture before goals from Kevin Keegan, John Toshack and Kennedy secured a 3–1 victory. They also beat Club Brugge 4–3 over two legs in the UEFA Cup final. In his second season, Paisley had matched Shankly’s league and UEFA Cup double of 1973.

The following year brought Liverpool’s first European Cup.

They retained the league championship but lost the FA Cup final 2–1 to Manchester United. Four days later, they faced Borussia Mönchengladbach in Rome. Goals from Terry McDermott, Tommy Smith and Phil Neal gave Liverpool a 3–1 victory and the trophy they had never previously won.

Neal later recalled a specific instruction that illustrated Paisley’s preparation. Expecting Berti Vogts to mark Keegan closely, the manager wanted his forward to keep moving across the pitch. Keegan’s running tested Vogts throughout the game and eventually won the penalty from which Neal completed the scoring. Paisley’s tactical contribution could be expressed in a few words and still shape a final.

Keegan then left for Hamburg. Paisley brought Kenny Dalglish from Celtic to replace him. Alan Hansen also arrived in 1977, with Graeme Souness joining from Middlesbrough in January 1978. Those three Scots would be central to Liverpool’s next great side.

The decisive goal in the 1978 European Cup final came from two of them. Souness supplied the pass; Dalglish lifted his finish over the Brugge goalkeeper. Liverpool won 1–0 at Wembley and retained the trophy. Within a year of losing Keegan, they were European champions again.

Paisley’s football demanded skill and patience as well as competitiveness. He described learning from continental teams how to intercept the ball and begin attacks with a carefully chosen first pass. Small-sided training games helped develop the quick decisions and movement his side needed. Recalling the 1978 final, Souness emphasised how effectively Liverpool could control European opponents through their passing.

The league campaign of 1978–79 showed what that football could produce. Liverpool scored 85 goals, conceded 16 and collected 68 points from 42 matches under the two-points-for-a-win system. They finished eight points ahead of Nottingham Forest. The attacking output and defensive record belonged to the same team: Paisley had made Liverpool exceptionally difficult to score against without restricting their ability to score themselves.

There were defeats that exposed their limitations. Forest eliminated the holders in the first round of the 1978–79 European Cup; Dinamo Tbilisi knocked them out at the same stage the following season. Paisley never won the FA Cup as manager. His record needs no suggestion that every decision worked or every competition ended in victory.

Liverpool retained the league title in 1980 and recovered their European standing in 1981. Ray Kennedy’s away goal against Bayern Munich took them through the semi-final. In Paris, Alan Kennedy scored the only goal against Real Madrid. Paisley became the first manager to win the European Cup three times. That season also brought Liverpool’s first League Cup, secured against West Ham United in a replay.

The next task was renewal. By Boxing Day 1981, a 3–1 home defeat by Manchester City had left Liverpool 12th. Paisley changed the captaincy, giving Souness the responsibility previously held by Phil Thompson. It was a substantial decision: Thompson had lifted the European Cup only months earlier.

Younger players were taking on greater importance. Ronnie Whelan emerged as Kennedy’s successor on the left of midfield, while Ian Rush became established in attack. In the 1982 League Cup final, Whelan scored twice and Rush once as Liverpool beat Tottenham 3–1 after extra-time. In the league, they won 20 of their final 25 matches to finish four points ahead of Ipswich Town.

Paisley had rebuilt another winning side. In an interview with LFChistory, Neal recalled that success never removed the prospect of a new signing arriving to compete for an established player’s position. Recruitment was a constant pressure on the team to improve. The next championship mattered more than the security of the men who had won the last one.

His final season, 1982–83, brought a sixth league title, this time by 11 points, and a third successive League Cup. After Manchester United were beaten 2–1 at Wembley, Souness asked Paisley to collect the trophy. A manager whose work was usually done away from public attention climbed the steps himself.

He handed over to Fagan in 1983 having won six championships, three European Cups, the UEFA Cup and three League Cups. Those 13 major honours were accompanied by the 1977 European Super Cup. Fagan’s league, European Cup and League Cup treble the following season demonstrated the quality of the team and coaching structure Paisley had helped sustain.

Paisley subsequently served on the board and returned to advise Dalglish when he became player-manager in 1985. His later years were affected by Alzheimer’s disease. He died on 14 February 1996, aged 77.

Shankly’s achievement was to transform Liverpool’s standing and ambitions. Paisley had been part of that work, then proved capable of taking responsibility for it himself. His distinction as manager was the combination of European success, repeated championships and the judgement to keep changing a successful team. Liverpool won their first European Cup under him. Six years later, he left them as champions of England again.
