# Tommy Lawrence: the goalkeeper behind Shankly’s first winners

Liverpool were only a goal ahead when George Eastham took his penalty at Anfield on 18 April 1964. Tommy Lawrence saved it, preserving the lead on the afternoon Bill Shankly’s team secured the championship. The eventual 5–0 score made the victory look straightforward. At the moment Lawrence intervened, Arsenal still had a chance to change the match. Frank McGhee’s contemporary report recorded the Kop giving the goalkeeper a song of his own after the save. Liverpool’s forwards would finish the celebration; Lawrence had helped keep it on course.

There was another goalkeeper in that story. Jim Furnell, now playing for Arsenal, had been Liverpool’s first choice when an injury gave Lawrence his opportunity eighteen months earlier. The Scot had waited five years for a first-team match, then established himself so firmly that his presence became routine. He would play 390 times for Liverpool, winning the championships of 1964 and 1966 and the club’s first FA Cup in between. Shankly’s first successful side had its foundations in a goalkeeper who was both reliable in his ordinary work and willing to attempt something unfamiliar.

Born in Dailly, Ayrshire, on 14 May 1940, Lawrence joined Liverpool as an amateur in April 1957 and signed professional forms that October. He had tried local football with Stockton Heath Albion before coming to Anfield. His arrival belonged to the years before Shankly, when Liverpool were still outside the First Division. Joining the club and becoming its goalkeeper were very different achievements: the young recruit had a long apprenticeship ahead of him.

Liverpool won promotion in 1962 without Lawrence making a senior appearance. His chance arrived in their first season back in the top flight, after Furnell was injured. On 27 October he made his debut at The Hawthorns, where Derek Kevan scored the only goal for West Bromwich Albion and Jimmy Melia had a penalty saved. It was a losing introduction, but Lawrence kept his place. The opportunity had finally opened, and his response persuaded Shankly to continue with him.

The nickname attached to Lawrence could obscure the quality that sustained that decision. The Flying Pig referred to his substantial build and the surprising spectacle of his saves. It was an affectionate description, rather than an adequate assessment of his football. Roy Evans, who joined Liverpool in 1964, later emphasised Lawrence’s positional sense. Courage, judgement and consistency mattered as much as the ability to make a conspicuous dive. He was trusted because he could read trouble and deal with it repeatedly.

Shankly also asked him to defend farther from his goal. When Liverpool pushed up, Lawrence moved towards the edge of his penalty area, ready to intercept a ball played behind the back line. That shortened the space an opposing forward could exploit and allowed the defenders to operate with a goalkeeper prepared to come out behind them. His passing was another useful quality. The role required him to decide when to leave the security of his line, rather than wait for every attack to reach the goalmouth.

Lawrence recalled that the arrangement initially frightened him and puzzled supporters who wanted him to retreat. Practice at Melwood helped make it workable. It was the kind of responsibility later described as a sweeper-keeper’s role: his starting position and readiness to intercept supported the way Liverpool defended. Evans remembered an alert player adapting to a side which pressed opponents farther up the field. Lawrence’s value lay in making that relationship work in matches, taking responsibility for space his teammates had left behind.

The 1963–64 title was Lawrence’s first full season as Liverpool’s established goalkeeper. The campaign did not begin with an immediate procession of victories: early home defeats shook confidence before Shankly’s side gathered momentum. Lawrence later identified the Easter sequence, with victories over Tottenham on either side of a win at Leicester, as a decisive passage. He remembered the strength of the relationships within a well-balanced team. His championship-day penalty save gave a visible expression to work that had been accumulating throughout the season.

Success opened a new field of responsibility. Lawrence kept goal when Liverpool made their competitive European debut against KR Reykjavík on 17 August 1964, winning 5–0 in Iceland. The journey began a campaign which would carry them to the European Cup semi-finals. Domestic ambitions continued alongside it, with the FA Cup still missing from the club’s honours. For the goalkeeper, the season brought the demands of unfamiliar opponents as well as the pressure attached to ending a very familiar wait.

Liverpool’s 1965 Cup run included uncomfortable matches against Stockport County and two meetings with Leicester, the team that had eliminated them in the 1963 semi-final. A goalless draw at Filbert Street brought the tie back to Anfield, where Roger Hunt scored the winner. Lawrence later remembered the replay for the extraordinary noise. The goalkeeper’s part in that progress was not incidental: Liverpool had needed the clean sheet away to earn the chance to settle the match in front of their own supporters.

At Wembley on 1 May, he was one of the eleven who completed the 2–1 victory over Leeds United. There were no goals in normal time; Hunt put Liverpool ahead in extra time, Billy Bremner equalised and Ian St John supplied the winner. Lawrence had played throughout the campaign and was still in goal when Liverpool finally won the Cup. The trophy joined the previous year’s championship and gave Shankly’s first team another achievement Liverpool had pursued for generations.

Three days later, the same goalkeeper faced Internazionale at Anfield. Liverpool won the first leg of their European Cup semi-final 3–1, only to lose 3–0 in Milan. The return brought a particularly painful episode for Lawrence: Joaquín Peiró took the ball as he bounced it and scored into the unguarded goal. Liverpool protested, but the goal stood. Alongside the disputed free-kick which produced Inter’s opener, it became part of the enduring argument about that defeat. The established result was elimination, 4–3 on aggregate, however bitterly Liverpool regarded the circumstances.

The following season offered domestic reassurance. Lawrence started all 42 league matches as Liverpool regained the championship, conceding 34 goals. He was one of five players who started every league game. The goalkeeper’s availability formed part of a remarkably stable team, and it mattered over a longer stretch: across the six league seasons from 1963–64 to 1968–69, he missed only four matches. Shankly could plan around a familiar last line of defence, week after week.

Another European opportunity ended in disappointment. On 5 May 1966 Lawrence played in the Cup Winners’ Cup final against Borussia Dortmund at Hampden Park. Hunt equalised after Sigfried Held put Dortmund ahead, but Stan Libuda scored in extra time and Liverpool lost 2–1. Lawrence’s team had reached the last four of the European Cup and a European final in successive seasons without winning either competition. Its domestic successes had established the club’s new standing; the first continental trophy remained for a later Liverpool side.

Ray Clemence arrived in 1967, but his signing did not immediately end Lawrence’s time. Clemence later recalled finding an incumbent at the height of his career, despite Shankly having suggested that an early route into the first team would be possible. The younger goalkeeper spent more than two years waiting for a regular place. This was meaningful competition for Lawrence, and his continued selection showed that he was still earning the position. In 1968–69 Liverpool conceded only 24 league goals, a club record at the time, with Lawrence ever-present.

The change came during 1969–70. Clemence had already made his league debut before Liverpool travelled to second-division Watford for an FA Cup quarter-final on 21 February 1970. Lawrence was in goal when Barry Endean’s second-half goal gave Watford a 1–0 win. That defeat accelerated Shankly’s renewal of the side. Clemence was selected for the next league match, against Derby County, and became the regular goalkeeper. Lawrence’s long spell as first choice had ended, although his Liverpool playing career had one more appearance to come.

It arrived on 26 April 1971 in a 2–2 draw with Manchester City at Maine Road. Shankly fielded a much-changed team between a league visit to Nottingham Forest and the second leg of a Fairs Cup semi-final against Leeds. Lawrence and Ron Yeats were among the experienced players in it, while Evans also played. The Football League fined Liverpool for the selection. For Lawrence, this unusual fixture was the 390th and final first-team match, more than a year after the Cup defeat often treated as his endpoint.

He left for Tranmere Rovers in November 1971. At thirty-one, he still had football to play, and the move brought another 80 league appearances before a spell at Chorley. His Scotland career had amounted to three senior caps, a small international record beside his sustained Liverpool service. After playing, he returned to factory work in Warrington as a quality controller. The career beyond Anfield was quieter than the years in which his dependable presence had accompanied the club’s return to the summit.

Evans’s recollection supplies something the statistics cannot. As a young player, he found Lawrence and his teammates ready to help him; later reunions brought the same cheerful, unassuming man. When Lawrence died aged 77 in January 2018, Liverpool announced a minute’s applause at Anfield before the match against Manchester City. The respect followed a career in which personality and performance had reinforced each other. He belonged to the team whose achievement younger Liverpool players inherited.

Lawrence’s place in that history rests on more than his nickname or the comparison with modern goalkeepers. He waited for an opening, made himself indispensable and accepted a role which helped his defenders play as Shankly required. The two championships and first FA Cup were the work of a whole side, but its goalkeeper contributed both continuity and decisions at moments when continuity was not enough. On the day Arsenal threatened to interrupt Liverpool’s title celebration, the ball went to Eastham’s penalty spot and the responsibility went to Lawrence. He answered it.
