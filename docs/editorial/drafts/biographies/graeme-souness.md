# Graeme Souness

Liverpool retained the European Cup in 1978 through a pass from Graeme Souness and a finish from Kenny Dalglish. Six years later, Souness left the club after lifting the same trophy as captain. Between those evenings came five league championships, two further European Cup victories and four successive League Cups. His Liverpool playing career lasted six and a half years, but few midfielders have exercised such influence over so much winning football.

The captain who departed after the 1984 final returned as manager in 1991. That second spell brought an FA Cup, declining league results and a serious breach of trust through his dealings with the Sun. Both careers belong to Liverpool’s history. The difficult return requires proper attention; so does the exceptional footballer whose passing, strength and leadership had helped the club dominate at home and abroad.

Born in Edinburgh on 6 May 1953, Souness began his professional development at Tottenham Hotspur. First-team opportunities were scarce, and a spell with Montreal Olympique preceded the move to Middlesbrough that established him in English league football. Under Jack Charlton, Middlesbrough won the Second Division championship in 1973–74. By the time Liverpool approached him, he was an experienced top-flight midfielder rather than a prospect being purchased for the reserves.

Bob Paisley signed him in January 1978 for about £350,000, then a record fee between English clubs. Liverpool were already European champions, and Souness had to find a place in a team with its own established methods. His debut came against West Bromwich Albion that month. In February he scored his first Liverpool goal, a forceful volley against Manchester United at Anfield. It offered an early example of the shooting that accompanied his work in midfield.

His most important intervention that spring was more delicate. Club Brugge defended deeply in the European Cup final at Wembley, keeping Liverpool waiting for a breakthrough. Souness supplied the pass that sent Dalglish through to lift the ball over the goalkeeper for the only goal. Recalling it for UEFA years later, Souness emphasised the timing and weight of the delivery. The assist was an expression of the control that made him valuable: he could decide a match without having to overwhelm an opponent physically.

That distinction matters to any account of his football. Souness could tackle, withstand pressure and impose himself in a confrontation, but Liverpool also relied on what he did after receiving possession. He saw and executed passes, supported the attack and scored. Alongside players such as Terry McDermott, his range of skills gave Liverpool a midfield capable of both protecting the team and creating opportunities. Describing him simply as its enforcer leaves too much of the job unexplained.

The 1978–79 championship was his first. Liverpool collected 68 points under the two-points-for-a-win system and conceded only 16 goals in 42 league matches. Souness played in 41 of those fixtures. They retained the title in 1979–80, again with him appearing in 41 league games. He was becoming part of the regular structure of Paisley’s side, available week after week as its standards were tested across another long season.

European success returned in 1980–81. Souness scored hat-tricks against Oulu Palloseura and CSKA Sofia during the run, the latter in a 5–1 quarter-final victory at Anfield. Those goals show another aspect of his contribution: he could turn an important European tie through his own finishing. Liverpool went on to beat Real Madrid in the final, giving Souness his second European Cup. The same season brought the first of the club’s four consecutive League Cup wins.

Paisley made him captain in January 1982, replacing Phil Thompson. Liverpool had been 12th after losing to Manchester City on Boxing Day, but won 20 of their remaining 25 league matches and finished as champions. The change of captain was part of that season’s response to difficulty, rather than an honour bestowed on a side already cruising towards the title. Souness would lead the club to three successive championships, carrying the captaincy from Paisley’s final years into Joe Fagan’s first.

Fagan’s 1983–84 side won the league, League Cup and European Cup. Souness made 61 appearances and scored 12 goals during the campaign. One of them settled the League Cup final replay against Everton at Maine Road on 28 March. After a goalless match at Wembley, his left-footed shot supplied the only goal of the replay. Liverpool’s first trophy of the season had come directly from their captain, against opponents who made them work for it.

His competitiveness also included acts of violence. Souness acknowledged punching Dinamo Bucharest’s Lică Movilă, breaking his jaw, during the 1984 European Cup semi-final at Anfield.

Liverpool reached the final in Rome, where Roma had the advantage of playing in their own stadium. Phil Neal’s goal and Roberto Pruzzo’s equaliser left the score at 1–1, and extra time failed to separate the teams. Souness converted his penalty in the shootout; Alan Kennedy eventually struck the kick that completed a 4–2 victory. Liverpool had won their fourth European Cup, and Souness his third. This time he received it as captain.

It was his final competitive appearance for Liverpool. He left for Sampdoria in the summer of 1984 with 359 appearances and 55 goals, having won the league in five of his six full seasons at Anfield. The European Cup final assist at the beginning and the successful penalty at the end provide two precise examples of what he contributed on the biggest occasions. Between them lay the regular league work on which the club’s success depended.

Sampdoria won their first Coppa Italia with Souness in 1985. In 1986 he became Rangers’ player-manager, beginning a period that brought three Scottish league titles and four League Cups under his management. His international career also extended beyond his Liverpool years: Scotland selected him 54 times between 1974 and 1986, and he played in three World Cups. Liverpool were therefore recruiting an experienced and successful manager when they brought him back, as well as a former captain.

He took over on 16 April 1991. Dalglish had resigned in February, with Ronnie Moran taking temporary charge before Souness arrived from Rangers. Only five league games remained. Liverpool finished second to Arsenal, and the task for the new manager was to renew the side while maintaining the standards of a club that had won the title the previous season. The need for change was real, but he inherited a team with recent achievement as well as ageing players.

The changes came quickly. Mark Wright, Dean Saunders and Mark Walters arrived, while Peter Beardsley, Steve Staunton and Steve McMahon were among those who left. Established relationships in the team were being dismantled as Souness tried to construct another side. The difficulty was not merely deciding that Liverpool needed younger players. Recruitment and departures had to leave a functioning team capable of competing immediately, and the results did not sustain the club’s position near the top.

Injuries made the first full season harder. John Barnes and Ronnie Whelan missed substantial stretches, and other senior players were also unavailable. There were useful additions and emerging players: Rob Jones arrived from Crewe Alexandra, while Steve McManaman became increasingly important. Liverpool nevertheless finished sixth in 1991–92. The side had lost the league consistency that Souness himself had helped establish as a player, though a cup run offered a more successful ending.

That run took place alongside a serious threat to the manager’s health. Souness underwent heart surgery in April 1992, and Moran oversaw the team while he recovered. Liverpool overcame Portsmouth in a semi-final replay decided on penalties and reached Wembley against Sunderland. Souness was back in the dugout for the final, with Moran leading the team out. Michael Thomas and Ian Rush scored in a 2–0 win, with McManaman making an important contribution to the victory.

The FA Cup was a genuine achievement amid a difficult season, and Moran’s work was an essential part of it. It was also the only major trophy of Souness’s Liverpool management. The final cannot be treated as evidence that the rebuilding had already succeeded: league performances had fallen well below the level the club expected, and the next campaign would produce another sixth-place finish.

A separate failure had already damaged his standing with supporters. While recovering from surgery, Souness agreed to an exclusive interview with the Sun. It appeared on 15 April 1992, the third anniversary of Hillsborough. The newspaper was boycotted by Liverpool supporters following its false reporting about the disaster. The decision to deal with it, compounded by publication on that date, caused hurt that extended far beyond disagreement about transfers or results.

Souness later apologised for the interview and acknowledged the offence he had caused. In the account reported by the Liverpool Echo in 2011, he accepted that speaking to the newspaper had been a serious mistake. That apology is part of the record, without requiring any conclusion about how supporters should respond to it. His relationship with Liverpool had been changed by a decision made as manager, concerning a matter much larger than football performance.

On the pitch, the FA Cup holders went out to Bolton Wanderers in 1993. Another sixth-place league finish left Souness with further rebuilding to attempt. The emergence of Robbie Fowler provided something substantial for the future: Souness gave him his senior debut against Fulham in September 1993, and the teenager scored five times in the return League Cup tie at Anfield. His introduction belongs alongside Jones and McManaman when assessing what the period contributed to the team that followed.

Those positives did not reverse the managerial verdict. Souness resigned on 28 January 1994, shortly after a home FA Cup defeat by Bristol City. He had been in charge for 157 competitive matches. A manager recruited to restore Liverpool’s supremacy had won one cup and failed to sustain a league challenge. The injury problems and transition help explain the difficulty of the task; they do not turn its outcome into a success.

In a 2018 Sky Sports interview, Souness identified the speed of his changes as a major mistake. He also regretted taking responsibility for contract negotiations, describing the difficulty of arguing over pay and then asking the same players to give everything for him on Saturday. These were his own reflections on management, acknowledging errors beyond the quality of individual signings. His account described the difficulty of carrying authority from a successful playing career into decisions affecting players as their manager.

His subsequent managerial career took him to Galatasaray, Southampton, Torino, Benfica, Blackburn Rovers and Newcastle United. At Liverpool, however, the contrast remained particularly stark. The player helped sustain one of the club’s finest periods and captained its 1984 treble. The manager’s attempt at renewal ended in disappointment, with the Sun interview leaving a separate and enduring issue of trust. A full account of Souness has to retain the distinction between those achievements and failures, while recognising that both belong to the same man.
