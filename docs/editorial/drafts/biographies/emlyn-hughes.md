# Emlyn Hughes: the captain who carried Liverpool into Europe’s elite

Emlyn Hughes lifted Liverpool’s first European Cup in Rome and its second at Wembley. Between those two presentations lies the image most closely associated with his career: the captain at the front of a team that had learned to win beyond England. Yet he had been at Anfield for six years before winning his first major honours. His contribution began in Bill Shankly’s rebuilding, when success still had to be recovered, and continued as Bob Paisley turned that renewed team into European champions.

Born in Barrow-in-Furness on 28 August 1947, Hughes was the son of Fred Hughes, a rugby league international. His own route into professional football was uncertain. He played youth football at Barrow and worked as a mechanic before Blackpool offered him an opportunity. There he developed into a first-team player under Ron Suart, making his senior debut in the final match of the 1965–66 season.

An early interview with Jimmy Armfield, preserved by LFChistory, shows the work beneath the later reputation for exuberance. Hughes recalled being told he was too young and small when he first tried to join Blackpool. He returned and eventually gained his chance. He described going back to the ground in the afternoons to improve his acceleration and turning, and studying established players’ positioning. Armfield praised his willingness to learn. The energy that became so familiar at Liverpool had been accompanied by deliberate efforts to improve his game.

Shankly brought him to Liverpool in February 1967 for £65,000. Hughes was 19. He made his debut against Stoke City on 4 March and played ten league matches before the end of the season. The side still contained players who had won the 1964 and 1966 championships, but Hughes offered something for the future: the ability to operate at full-back or in midfield, the stamina to keep making forward runs and a powerful shot.

His nickname, “Crazy Horse”, captured the visible enthusiasm, although it could obscure how useful his range of abilities became. A player who could be moved between positions gave Shankly options while older members of the team were replaced. Hughes was young enough to grow with the new side and sufficiently established to help hold it together. He was already a Liverpool regular before Ray Clemence, Steve Heighway, John Toshack and Kevin Keegan became central to the next phase.

There was no immediate procession of trophies. Liverpool lost the 1971 FA Cup final to Arsenal, then narrowly missed the league championship in 1971–72. The decisive return to success came the following season. Hughes appeared in 65 competitive matches in 1972–73, an unusually substantial contribution even within a team accustomed to demanding schedules. He was involved almost continuously as Liverpool pursued domestic and European honours together.

At Goodison Park on 3 March 1973, he supplied a particularly direct contribution to the title race. The derby was goalless until Hughes rounded goalkeeper David Lawson with ten minutes left. He added a second eight minutes later, securing a 2–0 win that kept Liverpool top. These were goals from a player still working in midfield, arriving late in a match when his sustained running could change the result. His Liverpool career is often recalled through defending and captaincy, but the Goodison double belongs to the earlier Hughes too.

The championship was accompanied by the club’s first European trophy. Liverpool beat Borussia Mönchengladbach 3–0 at Anfield in the UEFA Cup final’s opening leg, then survived a 2–0 defeat in Germany. Hughes had his first league title and UEFA Cup, but Tommy Smith was still the captain of that winning side. Keeping that distinction matters: Hughes’s celebrated sequence of trophy presentations had yet to begin.

Shankly made him captain in 1973, and his work increasingly centred on the middle of defence. The succession was not harmonious. Smith’s later account made clear his resentment, while Paisley acknowledged that the two men did not get on. Their personal relationship should not be confused with the team’s effectiveness: both continued to contribute to Liverpool’s success. The captain seen smiling with trophies was part of a dressing room in which professional co-operation could coexist with lasting dislike.

Alongside Phil Thompson, Hughes helped give the defence a different shape. Thompson later described them as two midfield players operating at centre-back as Shankly changed Liverpool’s football. Their partnership placed value on using the ball as well as winning it. Hughes could carry possession out of defence; Thompson’s passing supplied another route into midfield. It was a different use of Hughes’s qualities, with his running now beginning behind the midfield rather than within it.

The 1974 FA Cup final offered a strong demonstration. Newcastle United’s attack, led by Malcolm Macdonald, failed to score against Hughes and Thompson. Liverpool won 3–0, with Kevin Keegan scoring twice and Steve Heighway once. Hughes collected his first major trophy as Liverpool captain. Shankly’s last season had ended with another cup, and the player he had signed as a teenager was now leading the side.

Paisley’s appointment preserved Hughes’s importance. In 1975–76, Liverpool repeated the league and UEFA Cup double. The European final against Club Brugge required recovery in both legs: Liverpool turned a 2–0 home deficit into a 3–2 win, then conceded first in Belgium before Keegan equalised. The second match finished 1–1, making the aggregate score 4–3. Hughes captained another European success, this time under a manager whose own first major honours were establishing the next era.

He played every competitive match in 1976–77, 62 in total, and was chosen as the Football Writers’ Association Footballer of the Year. Liverpool retained the league title and reached two finals. Manchester United denied them the FA Cup, but four days later, on 25 May, they faced Mönchengladbach in Rome for the European Cup.

Hughes played in central defence that night. Terry McDermott put Liverpool ahead, Allan Simonsen equalised and Smith restored the lead with a header before Phil Neal’s penalty completed a 3–1 victory. Liverpool had won Europe’s leading club competition for the first time. Hughes’s place in the presentation reflected years of service through the rebuilding and the move to a new manager, rather than a captaincy acquired only once the side had become successful.

The 1977 UEFA Super Cup added another honour, and in 1978 Hughes lifted the European Cup again. Club Brugge held Liverpool through a goalless first half at Wembley, but Kenny Dalglish finished a pass from Graeme Souness to decide the final 1–0. The names involved in the winner also indicated how the team was changing around its captain. New players were taking substantial roles even while Hughes remained the man who collected the cup. Liverpool’s European supremacy depended on that continuing renewal.

It eventually reduced his own place in the side. Injury and Alan Hansen’s emergence restricted Hughes to 16 league appearances in 1978–79, though he played 28 times across all competitions. He earned a fourth championship as Liverpool conceded only 16 league goals. The successful season did not restore his former level of involvement, and he left for Wolverhampton Wanderers in August 1979. His Liverpool record stood at 665 competitive appearances and 49 goals.

His England career had developed alongside the club years. He made his debut against the Netherlands in November 1969, won 62 caps and captained the side 23 times. Fifty-nine of those appearances came while he was a Liverpool player; the final three followed his move to Wolves. England used his versatility across defence and midfield. He travelled to the 1970 World Cup but did not play, an international limitation within a career that brought such substantial achievement in European club football.

Wolves added a trophy Liverpool had not given him. On 15 March 1980, Hughes captained them to a 1–0 League Cup final victory over Nottingham Forest, partnering George Berry in defence as Andy Gray scored the winner. A move to Rotherham United as player-manager followed in 1981. After that job ended in 1983, there were further short playing spells, including Hull City and Swansea City. His later public career included television and a team captain’s role on A Question of Sport.

Hughes died on 9 November 2004, aged 57, after a brain tumour. At Liverpool, the two European Cups remain the clearest pictures of his achievement. The longer record is of a player who helped recover success, changed position as his responsibilities grew and remained valuable under two different managers. The enthusiasm was unmistakable; the sustained usefulness behind it explains why he was there to lift those trophies.
